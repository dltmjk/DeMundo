# De Mundo — Dialogue I (De materia mundi), Knot xvi

*Latin title:* Nodus XVI. Mundo non esse opus corpore caetera continente (table: Non esse opus corpore caetera continente)  
*English title:* Knot XVI. That for the World there is no need [non esse opus] of a body containing the rest [caetera]  
*Pages:* PDF pp. 127–136 = printed pp. 116–125 (from 'Ereu. Nequaquam ego, Andabata' on p. 116 to the end of Dialogue I on p. 125)

## Dialogue I, Knot xvi (printed pp. 116–125)

| Latin | English |
|---|---|
| [p. 116] [Margin: Nodus XVI. Mundo non esse opus corpore caetera continente.] Ereu. Nequaquam ego, Andabata, modo velis eam nobis substantiam demonstrare. | [p. 116] [Margin: Knot XVI. That for the World there is no need [non esse opus] of a body containing the rest [caetera].] Ereu. By no means I, Andabata, provided you be willing to demonstrate that substance to us. |
| Interea polliceor tibi salva et integra constitura omnia quae diffracto vase, ne effluant pertimescis, fulguris enim vice fungar, quae vino, {?disiecto} vase, subtile reticulum, quo contineatur inducit. | Meanwhile I promise you that all things will stand [constitura] safe and whole which, the vessel being shattered, you greatly fear lest they flow out; for I shall discharge the office [vice fungar] of the lightning, which, the vessel being thrown apart [{?disiecto}], draws over the wine [vino … inducit] a subtle little net [reticulum], by which it may be contained. |
| Faciemus enim ut sicut arbores radicibus, homines pedibus telluri {?sus-deque} innitimur, sic et fluida quae diffugitura metuis, verso introrsus conatu, separari summopere ne vacuum admittant, horrescant. | For we shall bring it about that, just as trees by roots, [we] men by feet lean upon the earth [telluri] up and down [{?sus-deque}], so also the fluids which you fear are about to flee apart, the endeavour [conatu] being turned inwards, may shudder [horrescant] in the highest degree at being separated, lest they admit a vacuum. |
| Ventis quoque imperabimus ne eo ascendant, sed contineant se prope solidum globum cuius ultima tantummodo effluvia, ad hanc subtilem maceriam elevantur. | The winds also we shall command that they ascend not thither, but contain themselves near the solid globe, whose last [ultima] effluvia only are raised to this subtle enclosure-wall [maceriam]. |
| Andab. Et non vides hos fluores summum [p. 117] ventum efficere qui cum circulo {?ferantur}, ut tu vis (mihi enim ignotum est) ferocissimo motu versus sphaerae superficiem ciebuntur, et velum tuum subtile concutient. | Andab. And do you not see that these flowings [fluores] make a highest [summum] [p. 117] wind, which, since they are borne in a circle [circulo] {?ferantur}, as you will (for to me it is unknown), will be stirred with a most ferocious motion towards the surface of the sphere, and will shake your subtle veil? |
| Ereu. Nullo modo hoc permittendum, sed quoniam (ut ais) in circulum est motus, innoxius erit circumstantibus, proximo tantum aere deraso, nisi aliud tibi videtur. | Ereu. In no way [is] this to be permitted; but since (as you say) the motion is into a circle, it will be harmless to the things standing around, the nearest air only being scraped off, unless something else seems [right] to you. |
| Asphal. Quoniam de superficie extima mundi incidit sermo; num videtur tibi aliquid de ea nobis constare posse, vel certe cum ratione proponi? [Margin: Superficiem extimam mundi non esse partibus elatioribus et depressioribus inaequalem.] | Asphal. Since the talk [sermo] has fallen upon the outermost [extima] surface of the world: does it seem to you that anything about it can be established [constare] for us, or at least be proposed with reason [ratione]? [Margin: That the outermost surface of the world is not unequal with more elevated and more depressed parts.] |
| Ereu. Huius quod cogitanti mihi aliquando se obtulit, non ero tibi parcus, quid recte secusve dixero, tuo quo polles iudicio, et animi tranquillitate aestimandum relinquo. | Ereu. Of this, which once offered itself to me [as I was] thinking, I shall not be sparing to you; what I shall have said rightly or otherwise, I leave to be weighed [aestimandum] by your judgement, in which you are strong [polles], and by tranquillity of mind. |
| Hoc imprimis habeto nullis extimum Coelorum Conopaeum tuberibus vel cavis sive efferri, sive dehiscere. | This hold first of all: that the outermost Canopy [Conopaeum] of the Heavens with no swellings [tuberibus] or hollows either is borne outward, or gapes open. |
| Et hoc, ipso quo vacuum interfectum est argumento. | And this by the very argument by which the vacuum was slain [interfectum est]. |
| Sive enim extent aliqua, brevior linea recta a vertice ad aliam superficiei partem deduci poterit quam per radicem; sive lacunae sint, eas rectis posse tegi necesse est, quare distantiam habes non saturatam, i. vacuum. | For whether some [parts] stand out, a shorter straight line will be able to be drawn from the vertex to another part of the surface than through the root; or whether there be hollows [lacunae], it is necessary that they can be covered by straight [lines]; wherefore you have a distance not saturated [non saturatam], i.e. a vacuum. |
| De hoc ergo nullatenus laboro. | About this therefore I in no degree labour [laboro]. |
| Proxime neque planam figuram facile in eo credam cum planae superficies angulis cohaereant, anguli vero eminentiam, nisi fallor, inutilem inducunt, cui enim bono extantiores mediis [p. 118] superficiebus angulos factos aestimabimus? aut qua naturae virtute, cum introrsus, ut supra dictum est, naturae momentum vergat. [Margin: Neque esse angulare.] | Next, neither should I readily believe a plane figure in it, since plane surfaces cohere by angles, but angles, unless I am deceived, bring in a useless eminence [eminentiam]; for to what good [cui bono] shall we reckon angles made standing out further than the middle [p. 118] surfaces? or by what virtue of nature, since, as was said above, the moment [momentum] of nature tends inwards. [Margin: Nor that it is angular.] |
| Andab. Quot ambagibus agis, Ereuni? non praestat uno verbo dicere, figura sphaerica omnium est nobilissima. | Andab. With how many windings [ambagibus] you proceed, Ereunius! Is it not better to say in one word: the spherical figure is of all the most noble. |
| Mundus entium nobilissimus, coniugio ea copulemus. Video enim te eo tandem delapsurum. | The World [is] of beings [entium] the most noble: let us couple them in wedlock. For I see that you will at length slip down [delapsurum] thither. |
| Ereu. Nescio naturam nobilitate coniugia facere, utilitate vero seu commoditate singula singulis adaptare facile concessero. | Ereu. I do not know [that] nature makes marriages by nobility; but that by utility or commodity [commoditate] it fits single things to single things I shall readily have conceded. |
| Unde si sphaericam utilissimam nactus fuero, ea etiam asciturus sum. | Whence, if I shall have found the spherical [figure] most useful, I am about to adopt [asciturus sum] it [ea] also. |
| Andab. Nihil igitur formam et spectabilitatem orbis et universi cordi habes? quam tamen tanti fieri a prudentissimis vides. | Andab. In nothing, then, do you hold at heart [cordi habes] the form and spectability [spectabilitatem] of the orb [orbis] and of the universe? which nevertheless you see to be made of so much [account] by the most prudent. |
| Ereu. Obiectas spectatoribus partes video, oculos evocante figura, et maiestate ornari, sepositas vero partes utilitati penitus consecratas, | Ereu. Parts set before [obiectas] spectators I see to be adorned with a figure calling forth the eyes, and with majesty; but parts set apart [sepositas] wholly consecrated to utility; |
| quare et cum tibi libuerit, Chimaeras per spatium imaginarium bombinantes agere ad esitandas secundas intentiones, curabimus ut extima mundi superficies per innumeras {?artium} species efflorescat, ad earum voluptatem perficiendam. | wherefore also, when it shall have pleased you to drive Chimaeras buzzing [bombinantes] through imaginary space to the eating-up [esitandas] of second intentions [secundas intentiones], we shall take care that the outermost surface of the world blossom forth through innumerable species of arts [{?artium}], for accomplishing their pleasure. |
| Nunc, dum nemo spectator adest, restat ut quae figura sit commodissima, nos spectemus. | Now, while no spectator is present, it remains that we look at which figure is most commodious [commodissima]. |
| Si ergo quod reliquum est mundi solibus et Planetariis discur-[p. 119]sibus per varios circulos, nostro quem incolimus et spectamus est analogum, hoc habemus, totum mundum ex vaporibus sphaericis esse compactum. [Margin: Esse curvilineam fortasse sphaericam.] | If therefore what is remaining of the world is, in suns and Planetary runnings-[p. 119]about [discursibus] through various circles, analogous to ours which we inhabit and look at, this we have: that the whole world is compacted out of spherical vapours. [Margin: That it is curvilinear, perhaps spherical.] |
| Sphaeras autem minori spatii detrimento figura alia quam sphaerica claudi posse non facile crediderim. | But that spheres can be enclosed with less loss [detrimento] of space by a figure other than a spherical one, I should not readily believe. |
| Si itaque in sphaerica omnia diditus est orbis, non videtur absque ratione pellis ipsius etiam in sphaeram expandi. | If therefore the orb [orbis] has been distributed [diditus est] into spherical [things] all, it does not seem without reason [that] its skin [pellis] also is spread out into a sphere. |
| Si vero non minorum tantummodo corporum sed etiam mundorum mille sunt facies, curvam illam dono qua brevissima utilissime tot species tegentur. | But if there are a thousand faces [facies] not only of lesser bodies but also of worlds, I grant [dono] that curved [one], by which, [as] the shortest, so many species will most usefully be covered. |
| Exclusis enim planis, et angulis non restat quid praeterea cogitetur. | For, planes and angles being excluded, there does not remain what besides may be thought of. |
| Nolo tamen etiam hoc tacere, me nescio quo mentis instinctu sphaericitati favere, quod in caeteris naturae operibus quasi perpetuam quandam Analogiam video. | I am unwilling, however, to be silent on this too: that I by I know not what instinct of mind favour sphericity, because in the other works of nature I see as it were a certain perpetual Analogy. |
| Dico in plantis, Animalibus, insectis, Piscibus perfectis, et ipso homine. | I mean in plants, Animals, insects, perfect Fishes [Piscibus perfectis], and man himself. |
| Unde videor mihi suspicari posse ipsam universalem nostri mundi fabricam, quantum ad centri et circumferentiae rationes, etiam in ignotis mundis expectari posse. | Whence I seem to myself to be able to suspect that the very universal fabric of our world, as far as regards the reasons [rationes] of centre and circumference, can be expected also in unknown worlds. |
| Sed quonam vos me agitis? num in tenebras exteriores expellere machinamini? | But whither are you [pl.] driving me? Surely you are not contriving to expel [me] into the outer darkness [tenebras exteriores]? |
| Nihil enim ultra hanc superficiem ulterius sperandum est, et iam ipsa fessa lumina quae accensa sunt, in colloquii principio quasi nictantia somni nos commonent. | For nothing beyond this surface is to be hoped for further; and already the tired lights themselves, which were lit at the beginning of the colloquy, as if winking [nictantia], remind us of sleep. |
| Et vox deficiens neque Herculem duobus resistere posse innuit. | And [my] failing voice intimates [innuit] that not even Hercules can resist two. |
| [p. 120] Asphal. Non, sic Ereuni, sed me potius auscultatorem et discipulum habuisti; hunc Andabatam (fateor) aliquando ferociorem passus es, sed nosti virum. | [p. 120] Asphal. Not so, Ereunius; but you have had me rather [as] listener and disciple; this Andabata (I confess) you have suffered sometimes rather fierce, but you know the man. |
| Non tamen me excuso, quin immisericors tibi fuerim, qui tantum et de tam arduis rebus perpetim quasi loqui te permiserim et hoc ieiunum. | Not however do I excuse myself [from the charge] that [quin] I have been [fuerim] unmerciful to you, I who permitted you to speak so much and about such arduous matters almost [quasi] without a break [perpetim], and this [you] fasting [ieiunum]. |
| Sed ignoscet bonitas tua quae tanta nos sermonis dulcedine pellexit, ut omnis modestiae et eutrapeliae fecerit immemores. | But your goodness will pardon, which has enticed us with so great a sweetness of discourse that it has made [us] unmindful of all modesty [modestiae] and pleasantry [eutrapeliae]. |
| Nunc itaque voco servulum, qui nova lumina quae cubitum nos ducant ferat, et Zythum tibi saccaro et Molucca nuce condiat. | Now therefore I call the little servant, who may bring new lights to lead us to bed [cubitum], and may season for you beer [Zythum] with sugar and the Molucca nut. |
| Tu modo ne vacet hora, hoc interim expedi, num Coelum maius, minusve possit esse quam reapse existat? | Do you only, lest the hour be idle, meanwhile unfold [expedi] this: whether the Heaven could be greater or less than it in fact [reapse] exists? |
| Ereu. Inquisitio haec vel causas Physicas respicit, vel naturam contemnens in ipsum authorem anhelat. | Ereu. This inquiry either looks to Physical causes, or, despising nature, pants towards the author himself. |
| Si naturales causas quaerimus, iam Andabatae respondi motum vehementem circa ultima mundi nullum esse posse qui eum augeat; [Margin: Mundum non posse augeri a causis naturalibus.] | If we seek natural causes, I have already answered Andabata that no vehement motion can be around the last [parts] [ultima] of the world which might increase it; [Margin: That the World cannot be increased by natural causes.] |
| qui autem circa nos motus contractione vel dilatatione fiunt, nosti quod ab aliis partibus vicissitudinario motu compensetur. | but [as for] the motions which come about around us by contraction or dilatation, you know that [this] is compensated by other parts with a vicissitudinary [vicissitudinario] motion. |
| Unde nil huiusmodi expectari potest quod mundi pomaeria proferat. | Whence nothing of this kind can be expected which might carry forward [proferat] the pomerium [pomaeria] of the world. |
| Asphal. Sed etiam hoc doce, si causa potens alioqui adforet, quo incommodo distineretur? | Asphal. But teach this also: if a cause otherwise powerful were at hand, by what inconvenience [incommodo] would it be held back? |
| [p. 121] Andab. Nescis, Asphali, si quae pars accresceret illi, per spatia imaginaria ingrediendum fore, in quibus motum non permittit nobis Ereunius, qui tamen, si mundus augeretur, causis naturalibus necessarius foret, postquam augmentum non nisi successive a natura posset contingere, neque successio sine motu intelligi. | [p. 121] Andab. Do you not know, Asphalius, that if any part were to grow onto it, it would have to be gone [ingrediendum fore] through imaginary spaces, in which Ereunius does not permit us motion; which [motion] however, if the world were increased, would be necessary to natural causes [causis naturalibus], since [postquam] increase could not happen from nature except successively, nor succession be understood without motion. |
| Ereu. Non hoc vereor, Andabata, non enim localis hic motus foret sed quantitativus; unde reale, et extrinsecum spatium nequicquam desideratur. | Ereu. This I do not fear, Andabata; for this motion would not be local but quantitative; whence a real and extrinsic space is required [desideratur] in vain. |
| Sed quod timeo est, ne si mundus excrescat, maxime si sphaericus sit (sed tantumdem est si alterius lineae curvae) non possit nisi undique simul crescat, et vel maior sphaera sit, vel figuram exuat, dum {?excoedemata} refugit; | But what I fear is, lest, if the world grow out, most of all if it be spherical (but it is just as much if [it be] of another curved line), it cannot [grow] unless it grow everywhere at once, and either become a greater sphere, or put off its figure, while it shrinks from out-swellings [{?excoedemata}]; |
| quidlibet autem horum partibus mundi tribuere, supra earumdem naturam sit, et, quod hinc sequitur, causarum naturalium. | but to attribute any one of these to the parts of the world would be [sit] above the nature of the same, and, what follows hence, [above that] of natural causes. |
| Si vero ad authorem naturae me revocas, disputant Metaphysici unicam esse materiam, et eam quantitati sine fine pariter succrescere; unde ex finis ratione dumtaxat ei demensam esse portionem qua gaudet. | But if you call me back to the author of nature, the Metaphysicians dispute that matter is single [unicam], and that it grows up equally with quantity without end; whence [it is] only from the reason [ratione] of the end [finis] that the portion which it enjoys has been measured out [demensam esse] to it. |
| Verum ista et alia principia quam quibus hactenus insuevimus novasque clepsydras flagitant, praeterquam quod argutae et spinosae sunt contemplationis similia, meditationique magis quam colloquiis idonea. | But these things demand both other principles than those to which we have hitherto been accustomed, and new water-clocks [clepsydras]; besides that they are like [similia] [things] of a subtle and thorny contemplation, and more suited to meditation than to colloquies. |
| Sed et ecce ser-[p. 122]vum cum necessariis paratum. | But see, too, the ser-[p. 122]vant ready with the necessaries. |
| Asphal. Epota itaque quod offert, hic est aromatites pauperum, et ut nunc sumus, potior ut credo hippocratico, | Asphal. Drink off, then, what he offers: this is the spiced wine [aromatites] of the poor, and, as we now are, better, as I believe, than hippocras [hippocratico]; |
| nam vacuis committere venis nil nisi lene decet, et Zythus magis ad somnum facit quam vinum, et Myristica capiti defesso iucunda censetur a medicis: saccharum vero Anglis nunquam est intempestivum, | for to commit to empty veins nothing but [what is] mild is fitting; and beer [Zythus] makes more for sleep than wine; and the nutmeg [Myristica] is reckoned pleasant to a wearied head by the physicians; but sugar to the English is never unseasonable; |
| et post potionem (si me audis) parumper quiesce, dum ego Andabatae hoc impono negotii, ut summa rerum a te commemoratorum fastigia colligat, quo melius cerebro subsidant quae audivimus. | and after the draught (if you heed me) rest a little, while I lay on Andabata this [piece] of business, that he gather the highest summits [summa … fastigia] of the things recounted by you, so that what we have heard may settle the better in the brain. |
| Andab. Ego libens partem unam sustinuero si tu societatem laboris non recuses: non enim ausim tam foelicem memoriam mihi polliceri, ut in nullo me destituat. [Margin: Dictorum recapitulatio.] | Andab. I shall gladly have sustained one part, if you do not refuse fellowship [societatem] of the labour; for I should not dare to promise myself so happy a memory that it fail me in nothing. [Margin: Recapitulation of what was said.] |
| Age ergo, te quoque operi accinge, et me praeeuntem habe: | Come then, gird yourself too for the work, and have me going before: |
| Exorsus ergo a mundi quantitate neque infinitum, neque infinitos esse pronunciavit, sed unicum vacuumque in eo nullum, densi quoque rarique eam esse naturam quam docuit Aristoteles. | Having begun, then, from the quantity of the world, he pronounced [it] to be neither infinite, nor [the worlds to be] infinite [in number], but single [unicum], and no vacuum in it; of the dense also and the rare, that the nature is that which Aristotle taught. |
| Deinde corpora solida sine liquidis non posse moveri, et inanes esse qui tamdiu nos oppresserunt orbes Planetarum. | Next, that solid bodies cannot be moved without liquids, and that the orbs of the Planets which so long oppressed us are empty [inanes]. |
| Adiecit coelos quoniam divisibiles ex necessitate etiam esse mortales. | He added that the heavens, since divisible, are of necessity also mortal. |
| Immortalitatem coelorum et motum eorumdem, et figuram sphaericam ad Idolomaniam tuendam [p. 123] esse introducta, vel ad Astrologorum calculos melius concinnandos. | That the immortality of the heavens, and the motion of the same, and the spherical figure, were introduced for protecting Idol-mania [Idolomaniam] [p. 123], or for arranging [concinnandos] the better the calculations of the Astrologers. |
| Tandem ad hoc devenit ut caeteras mundi partes repletas entibus nostratium similibus argueret. | At length he came to this, that he argued the other parts of the world [to be] filled with beings like those of our country [nostratium]. |
| Cometas non ignes esse, sed nubes lucidas; a refractione, caudas, ab obviis causis motum habere exposuit. | That comets are not fires, but lucid clouds; [that they] have from refraction [their] tails, from obvious [obviis] causes [their] motion, he set forth. |
| Stellas quoque firmamenti non a nostro lucere sole. | That the stars also of the firmament do not shine from our sun. |
| Cometas si qui sunt in coelis non esse caudatos vel saltem non apparere. | That comets, if any are in the heavens, are not tailed, or at least do not appear. |
| Terram quoque non esse magnetem; aerem effluvium telluris esse, et singula sibi cognatis astra effluviis obsideri. | That the Earth [terram] also is not a magnet; that the air is an effluvium of the earth [telluris], and that the stars singly are besieged [obsideri] by effluvia akin to themselves. |
| Tu quoque, Asphali, de individuorum impossibili repetitione adiecisti, sed non inultus: statim enim egregia omnium corporum connexione te compensavit. | You also, Asphalius, added [something] about the impossible repetition of individuals, but not unavenged: for at once he compensated [compensavit] you with an excellent connexion of all bodies. |
| Cum nihil superesset, extimam nobis mundi superficiem coagmentavit, et tandem eam perrumpi non posse demonstravit. | When nothing remained, he joined together [coagmentavit] for us the outermost surface of the world, and at length demonstrated that it cannot be broken through. |
| Haec mihi memoria suggerit: tu Asphali, vide moneas siquid omiserim. | These things memory suggests to me: you, Asphalius, see that you advise [me] if I have omitted anything. |
| Asphal. Imo vero plura omisisti, Andabata, sed recte quoniam incidentia ipsoque orationis filo intertexta, potius quam nodi erant. | Asphal. Nay rather, you have omitted more [things], Andabata, but rightly, since they were incidental [incidentia] and interwoven with the very thread of the discourse, rather than knots [nodi]. |
| Unum quorum vellem vel ipse nobis Ereunius ignovisset, videlicet quod plurium e coelo nobis spem praecidit, declarata tubi longispicis natura, saltem ipsa spe delectati fuissemus. | One of which I could wish that even Ereunius himself had pardoned [ignovisset] us, namely that he cut off for us the hope of more [plurium] from the heaven, the nature of the far-seeing tube [tubi longispicis] having been declared; at least we would have been delighted by the hope itself. |
| Ereu. Etiam hoc vobis detrimentum qua-[p. 124]tenus in me situm est resarcio. | Ereu. This loss too, in so-[p. 124]far as it lies in me, I repair for you [pl.]. |
| Si tamen aequum facio in lucem eos protrahendo qui necdum e sua quiete cuperent avelli. | If, however, I do what is fair in dragging forth into the light those who would not yet desire to be torn away from their own quiet. |
| Ut, ut fuerit sic habetote. Vidi epistolam cuiusdam ex nostratibus, ad authorem libelli aurei scriptam, cum modulo cuiusdam artificii quo mirum in modum Telescopii vires augebat. [Margin: Thomas Gascoinus Telescopii promotor.] | However it may have been [Ut, ut fuerit], so hold it [pl.]. I saw a letter of a certain one of our countrymen [ex nostratibus], written to the author of the golden little book [libelli aurei], with a model [modulo] of a certain artifice by which in wonderful manner he was increasing the powers [vires] of the Telescope. [Margin: Thomas Gascoigne [Gascoinus], promoter of the Telescope.] |
| Non quo maiora redderet obiecta, sed quo accuratius visorum partes et distantias deprehenderet. | Not that he might render objects greater, but that he might more accurately detect [deprehenderet] the parts and distances of things seen. |
| Mira sunt quae pollicetur adhuc iuvenis et ignotus, sed ingenii sublimis et patientissimi simul et ardentissimi versus Mathematicas studii. | Wonderful are the things which he promises, still young and unknown, but of a sublime wit [ingenii] and of a study [studii] at once most patient and most ardent towards the Mathematics. |
| Fortassis ad omnia quae cognitu nobis necessaria sunt de coelis, aditus nobis aperietur. | Perhaps to all things which are necessary for us to be known [cognitu] about the heavens an approach [aditus] will be opened to us. |
| Andab. Quasi vel non omnia, vel aliqua scitu necessaria forent. | Andab. As if either not all, or some [things] were necessary to be known [scitu]. |
| Ereu. Siccine credis, et non vides ea quae ignota nobis creent molestiam magis esse scitu necessaria, quam ea quae prorsus latent. | Ereu. Do you believe thus, and do you not see that those things which, unknown, create trouble [molestiam] for us are more necessary to be known than those which lie wholly hidden? |
| Talia sunt quae ipsa videntur, sed causae latentes admirationem cient. | Such are [those] which themselves are seen, but hidden causes stir up wonder [admirationem]. |
| Ea vero quae in errorem nos inducunt (maxime si perniciosus sit) qualia sunt quae Philosophi de coelorum immortalitate docuerunt, maiori adhuc nos incommodo afficiunt. | But those which lead us into error (most of all if it be pernicious), such as are those which the Philosophers taught about the immortality of the heavens, affect us with greater inconvenience [incommodo] still. |
| Huiusmodi itaque scientiam necessariam vides cum quae pure latent, curiositate magis quam necessitate appetantur. | The science [scientiam] of [things] of this kind, therefore, you see [to be] necessary, whereas [cum] the things which lie purely hidden are sought more by curiosity than by necessity. |
| [p. 125] Asphal. Si sic est, Ereuni, aliam necesse est nobis pollicearis disputationem de motu coelestium corporum. Ingenti enim me admiratione constringunt, et molestia afficiunt. | [p. 125] Asphal. If it is so, Ereunius, it is necessary that you promise us another disputation on the motion of the celestial bodies. For they bind me fast with huge wonder [admiratione], and affect [me] with trouble [molestia]. |
| Ereu. Ego vero quidlibet polliceor, modo nunc me cubitum dimittas. | Ereu. I indeed promise anything whatever, provided now you dismiss me to bed. |
| Asphal. Puer praefer lumina: nolo enim ut huius dicti omen intereat. | Asphal. Boy, carry the lights before [us]: for I do not wish that the omen of this saying should perish. |

## Words and phrases with alternative or special meanings

- ***Mundo non esse opus corpore caetera continente*** — rendered “that for the World there is no need of a body containing the rest”. the table (FM2) has 'Non esse opus corpore caetera continente' Ereunius denies a solid outermost shell (Aristotle's outermost sphere / Empyrean): the fluids of the world hold together by their own 'horror' of separation (horror vacui), so no container is needed.
- ***fulguris … vice fungar … quae vino, disiecto vase, subtile reticulum … inducit*** — rendered “discharge the office of the lightning, which … draws a subtle little net over the wine”. reticulum = little net, membrane, film Classical prodigy: lightning shatters a jar and the wine stands congealed in its shape (Seneca, Nat. Quaest. II.31; Pliny, NH II.137). White uses it as a model of a container-less boundary.
- ***sus-deque (susque deque)*** — rendered “up and down”. on both sides; topsy-turvy; 'it is all one' Men stand on the earth with their feet both 'above' and 'below', i.e. antipodes: gravity is centripetal everywhere. Reading of the scan uncertain.
- ***conatus (verso introrsus conatu)*** — rendered “endeavour”. striving, tendency, impetus Key term in White and Digby for a body's inclination to motion; cf. 'momentum naturae … introrsus vergat' on p. 118.
- ***horrescere (separari)*** — rendered “shudder at”. dread, abhor Personified horror vacui: the fluids 'abhor' separation lest they admit a vacuum.
- ***maceria*** — rendered “enclosure-wall”. garden wall, fence Ironic for the outermost 'surface' of the world, which is merely the last effluvia of the globe.
- ***fluores*** — rendered “flowings”. fluids, flows, fluxes Andabata's term for the fluid parts of the world; distinct from 'fluida'.
- ***summum ventum*** — rendered “a highest wind”. the uppermost wind; a supreme (i.e. very great) wind Ambiguous: both 'at the top' (of the world) and 'most violent'.
- ***extima superficies mundi*** — rendered “the outermost surface of the world”. extreme, last Aristotelian 'ultimum caeli'; cf. glossary 'superficies'.
- ***Conopaeum (Coelorum)*** — rendered “Canopy”. mosquito-net, bed-curtain (κωνωπεῖον) Classical word for a curtained bed; the vault of the heavens as a canopy.
- ***distantia non saturata*** — rendered “a distance not saturated”. unfilled, unsatisfied White's definition of a vacuum as a distance not filled with body (cf. Knot iii on the vacuum).
- ***cui bono*** — rendered “to what good”. for whose benefit Cassius' maxim (Cicero, Pro Milone 32), here 'for what purpose'.
- ***momentum (naturae)*** — rendered “moment”. impetus, tendency, weight; importance Here the physical sense (inclination of nature), not 'importance' as elsewhere in the glossary.
- ***commoditas / commodissima*** — rendered “commodity / most commodious”. convenience, advantage, fitness Opposed to 'nobilitas': Ereunius chooses shapes by usefulness, not by dignity.
- ***spectabilitas*** — rendered “spectability”. visibility, being fit to be looked at, showiness Rare late-Latin word; Andabata's appeal to beauty and dignity of shape.
- ***Chimaeras per spatium imaginarium bombinantes … ad esitandas secundas intentiones*** — rendered “Chimaeras buzzing through imaginary space to the eating-up of second intentions”. bombinare = to buzz, hum; esitare = to keep eating Allusion to Rabelais' mock scholastic title 'Quaestio subtilissima, utrum Chimaera in vacuo bombinans possit comedere secundas intentiones' (Pantagruel II.7). Secundae intentiones = logical second-order concepts.
- ***artium species*** — rendered “species of arts”. reading uncertain; perhaps 'antium' (forelocks) or another word Ornament for the imaginary spectators of the world's outside.
- ***discursus (Planetarii)*** — rendered “runnings-about”. courses, circuits The planets' wandering courses.
- ***diditus est (orbis)*** — rendered “has been distributed”. spread abroad, divided up Lucretian verb (didere).
- ***pellis (orbis)*** — rendered “skin”. hide, outer covering Metaphor for the outermost surface of the world.
- ***Piscibus perfectis*** — rendered “perfect Fishes”. fully-formed, complete Probably 'perfect animals' (i.e. generated by seed) as opposed to insects (imperfect animals, spontaneously generated).
- ***tenebrae exteriores*** — rendered “outer darkness”.  Matthew 8:12, 22:13; joke on being thrust beyond the surface of the world.
- ***neque Hercules duobus (resistere posse)*** — rendered “not even Hercules can resist two”.  Proverb 'Ne Hercules quidem contra duos' (Plato, Phaedo 89c; Erasmus, Adagia I.v.39).
- ***eutrapelia*** — rendered “pleasantry”. wit, ready humour; (in Aristotle) a virtue of play Aristotle, Nic. Eth. IV.8; in Paul (Eph. 5:4) a vice.
- ***Zythum*** — rendered “beer”. Egyptian barley beer (ζῦθος) Humanist Latin for English ale/beer; spiced with sugar and nutmeg.
- ***nux Molucca / Myristica*** — rendered “the Molucca nut / nutmeg”.  Nutmeg from the Moluccas (Banda islands).
- ***aromatites*** — rendered “spiced wine”. aromatic wine (Pliny, NH XIV.92) Asphalius' joke: spiced beer is the poor man's aromatic wine.
- ***hippocraticum*** — rendered “hippocras”. spiced sweetened wine ('vinum Hippocraticum') Named after the 'Hippocrates' sleeve' strainer.
- ***vacuis committere venis nil nisi lene decet*** — rendered “to commit to empty veins nothing but [what is] mild is fitting”.  Quoted from Horace, Satires II.4.25–26.
- ***pomaeria proferre*** — rendered “carry forward the pomerium”. extend the boundaries Roman ritual of extending the sacred city boundary (Tacitus, Annals XII.23); the world's limits.
- ***motus quantitativus vs localis*** — rendered “quantitative vs local motion”. augmentation vs change of place Aristotle's kinds of motion (Physics V): growth needs no extrinsic space.
- ***excoedemata*** — rendered “out-swellings”. perhaps from Greek ἐξοιδήματα, swellings Unusual word; reading not certain.
- ***unica materia … quantitati sine fine pariter succrescere*** — rendered “matter is single and grows up equally with quantity without end”. matter is indifferent to any amount of quantity Scholastic: prime matter is one and of itself indifferent to any size; only the end (finis) fixes its portion. Pun on fine/finis.
- ***clepsydrae (novae)*** — rendered “new water-clocks”. more time In court, speaking-time was measured by the clepsydra; 'new water-clocks' = more time to speak.
- ***Idolomania*** — rendered “Idol-mania”. madness for idols, idolatry White's polemical coinage for the pagan cult of the heavens (Knot vii).
- ***incidentia … potius quam nodi*** — rendered “incidental [things] … rather than knots”. digressions White's own distinction between the 'knots' (theses) and the matter woven in on the way.
- ***tubus longispicus*** — rendered “far-seeing tube”. telescope Another of White's periphrases (cf. tubus astrisequus, coelispex).
- ***Gascoinus / author libelli aurei*** — rendered “Gascoigne / the author of the golden little book”.  The margin names 'Thomas' Gascoigne, but the person described (young, unknown, a device on the telescope that measures parts and distances rather than magnifying) is evidently William Gascoigne (c.1612–1644), inventor of the filar micrometer. The 'golden little book' is probably William Oughtred's Clavis Mathematicae (1631), to whom Gascoigne wrote about his invention c. 1640–41 (identification probable, not certain).
- ***modulus*** — rendered “model”. small measure, scale; model Probably a description or drawing of the device sent with the letter.
- ***omen (huius dicti)*** — rendered “the omen of this saying”.  Asphalius treats Ereunius' promise of a 'disputation on the motion of the heavenly bodies' as binding: it announces Dialogue II (De forma mundi, seu de motu magnorum corporum).

## Translator's notes

- Unit: Dialogue I, Knot xvi, complete in one part (the last knot of Dialogue I). PDF pp. 127–136 = printed pp. 116–125, from 'Ereu. Nequaquam ego, Andabata' (p. 116, margin 'Nodus XVI. Mundo non esse opus corpore caetera continente') to the end of Dialogue I ('…huius dicti omen intereat.', p. 125, signature Q iij). PDF p. 137 (= p. 126) is blank; Dialogue II, Knot i ('Introductio') begins on PDF p. 138 = printed p. 127. Offset +11 holds throughout.
- Title: margin 'Mundo non esse opus corpore caetera continente'; the table of nodi (FM2) gives 'Non esse opus corpore caetera continente'. The margin title is used. Further marginal sub-headings are placed in their rows: pp. 117 (two), 119, 120, 122, 124.
- Uncertain readings: (a) p. 116 'disiecto' printed 'dificcto' (blurred; 'disiecto' fits 'diffracto vase' just before). (b) p. 116 'sus-deque' printed 'suſ-déque', = susque deque, 'up and down'. (c) p. 117 'ferantur' badly blurred; sense secure. (d) p. 118 'artium' printed apparently 'antium'; 'artium' (r/n confusion) assumed in the translation. (e) p. 121 'excoedemata' (excœdemata): apparently a coinage from Greek ἐξοιδήματα. (f) p. 116 'quae vino … inducit': 'quae' is feminine though 'fulgur' is neuter; taken as referring to the lightning. (g) p. 118 'ea etiam asciturus sum': 'ea' taken for 'eam' (the figure); alternatively ablative 'by that [way]'. (h) p. 121 'causis naturalibus' may go with 'augeretur' (increased by natural causes) or with 'necessarius' (necessary to natural causes); the latter followed in the English, the former equally possible. (i) p. 121 'contemplationis similia' read as 'like [things] of … contemplation'. (j) p. 124 'Quasi vel non omnia, vel aliqua…' is elliptical: Andabata scoffs that knowledge of the heavens is not needed; translated literally.
- Questions printed with a full stop (pp. 117 'Et non vides…', 118 'non praestat…', 124 'Siccine credis…') are given a question mark in English.
- Verification pass (sentence-by-sentence against the Latin and 300 dpi crops of pp. 116, 117, 118, 123): 3 corrections — (1) p. 117 'anguli vero … inducunt': 'vero' first rendered 'and', corrected to 'but'; (2) p. 120 'Non tamen me excuso, quin immisericors tibi fuerim': first rendered as a denial ('that I have not been unmerciful'), corrected to the admission 'I do not excuse myself [from the charge] that I have been unmerciful'; (3) p. 124 'sed causae latentes admirationem cient': a supplied '[whose]' removed to keep to the Latin. Moods re-checked (credam, cohaereant, vergat, copulemus, efflorescat, cogitetur, permiserim, ferat/condiat, possit/existat, compensetur, crescat/sit/exuat, colligat/subsidant, cuperent, creent, appetantur subjunctive; tenses of concessero, nactus fuero, sustinuero future perfect, delectati fuissemus pluperfect subjunctive) and negatives (nullis, nullatenus, neque … neque, non nisi, necdum, neque Herculem) confirmed. No omissions or additions found.
- Conventions as in glossary.md: long s normalised; æ/œ → ae/oe; '&' → et; consonantal u → v; supplied words in [ ]; Latin of key words in [ ] after the English; [p. n] marks printed page starts; [pl.] marks a plural 'you'.
