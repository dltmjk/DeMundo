## Dialogue II, Knot xii, part 1 (printed pp. 239–248)

| Latin | English |
|---|---|
| [p. 239] Ereu. Si tu quoque, Andabata, eodem es animo, hoc primum edissere, num ex luna putes vapores tolli sicut ex tellure? | [p. 239] Ereu. If you also, Andabata, are of the same mind, set forth [edissere] this first: whether you think vapours are raised out of the moon just as out of the earth [tellure]? |
| [Margin: Nodus XII. Lunam non moveri immediate a sole.] Andab. Video (si vera sunt quae heri a te audivimus) necessarium esse, & ex iis etiam nubes & ventos & caetera Meteora confici nostris aut eadem aut certe proportionalia. | [Margin: Knot XII. That the Moon is not moved immediately by the sun.] Andab. I see (if the things are true which yesterday we heard from you) [that it] is necessary, and [that] out of them also clouds and winds and the other Meteors are made [confici], either the same as ours or certainly proportional [to them]. |
| [p. 240] Ereu. Ventosam itaque Lunam quoque existimas? | [p. 240] Ereu. Windy, therefore, you reckon the Moon also? |
| Andab. Vel quam maxime. Video enim ex calidissimis regionibus excitatos vapores facile in altum tolli; & quod ex hoc fit, in ventos magna abundantia converti: & hoc eo magis quo montosam esse summe lunam ex figura patet. Contractis itaque viis vapores furere necesse est, quae etiam esse causa possit, ut non facile in nubes coalescant, ventorum furiis agitati. | Andab. Even as much as possible. For I see vapours stirred up out of the hottest regions are easily raised on high; and, what comes about from this, are turned into winds in great abundance: and this the more, by as much as it is evident from [its] figure [ex figura] that the moon is in the highest degree mountainous. With the ways [viis] therefore drawn together, it is necessary that the vapours rage; which also may be the cause that they do not easily coalesce into clouds, being agitated by the furies of the winds. |
| Ereu. Et quoniam hoc solis opera conficitur, sol autem vicesimo octavo circiter die totam iam obit lunam, vide num vento huic tantumdem contingat. | Ereu. And since this is accomplished by the work of the sun, and the sun about the twenty-eighth day has already gone round [obit] the whole moon, see whether just as much [tantumdem] befalls this wind. |
| Andab. Videtur, & sicut mare orbem terrarum ambit, sic ventus ingens lunam totam oberrabit, nisi fallor. | Andab. It seems [so]; and just as the sea goes round the orb of the lands [orbem terrarum], so a huge wind will wander over [oberrabit] the whole moon, unless I am deceived. |
| Ereu. Recte hoc addidisti; debuisses enim cogitasse num ingens aliqua vallis vel mare totam lunam ambiat, quo ferri possit ventus. Cum enim ex gravioribus vaporibus consistat; etiam maiores nostrorum montium non superat ventus (ut experientia in pluribus regionibus constat) nedum eos qui in luna sunt qui nostros immane superant. | Ereu. Rightly you added this; for you ought to have thought whether some huge valley or sea goes round the whole moon, by which [quo] the wind could be carried. For since it consists of heavier vapours, the wind does not surmount even the greater of our mountains (as is established by experience in many regions), much less those which are in the moon, which surpass ours enormously [immane]. |
| Andab. Sint omnes lunae colles Atlantes & Tenariffi, necesse tamen est vapores eos superare, cum acceperimus videri illuminatos, & extremis montibus superfusos: [p. 241] sed & apud nos (ubi nullus prae lunae fervore calor) ad quinquagesimum tamen milliare crepuscula elevantur. | Andab. Let all the hills of the moon be Atlases and Teneriffes, yet it is necessary that the vapours surmount them, since we have received [acceperimus] [that they] are seen illuminated, and poured over the outermost [extremis] mountains: [p. 241] but with us also (where [there is] no heat in comparison with [prae] the glow [fervore] of the moon) the twilights [crepuscula] are nevertheless raised up to the fiftieth mile. |
| Ereu. Esto, Andabata, sed quid his vaporibus fiet? | Ereu. Be it so, Andabata; but what will become of these vapours? |
| Andab. Circa lunam & versus solem movebuntur: sic enim movetur ille ventus quem ex mari pacifico excitavimus ad omnes Oceani tumultus concitandos. | Andab. They will be moved about the moon and towards the sun: for so is moved that wind which we stirred up out of the Pacific sea for rousing all the tumults of the Ocean. |
| Ereu. Luna itaque etiam ipsa, si ab his vaporibus movetur, solem sicut illi, sequetur. | Ereu. The moon, therefore, itself also, if it is moved by these vapours, will follow the sun just as they [do]. |
| Andab. Sic se habet. | Andab. So it is [sic se habet]. |
| Ereu. Immo sic non se habet, unde ab his vaporibus nequaquam {?c}ietur luna. Quomodo enim? num tactis montium cacuminibus totam circumagent? qui enim vallibus haerent rupibus dissepti cursum nullum habent, nisi intra suos carceres; Praeterea, esto habeant, num spiritus adeo tenues & scissiles immediate solida & saxa prae se agent? nequaquam hos naturae mores ex aliis ipsius machinamentis colligimus. Adde quod primus motus in circulum circa suum centrum foret: Talem autem lunae Phaenomena repudiant. Hoc itaque maneat lunam non immediate a sole commotis vaporum fluxibus cieri. [p. 242] Sed vis mihi liberaliter aliquam gratiam facere? | Ereu. Nay rather, so it is not; whence by these vapours the moon will by no means be stirred [cietur]. For how? Will they, the summits of the mountains having been touched, drive [it] round whole? For those which cling to the valleys, fenced apart [dissepti] by crags, have no course except within their own barriers [carceres]; Moreover, be it that they have [one], will spirits [spiritus] so thin and divisible [scissiles] immediately drive solid things and rocks before them? By no means do we gather these manners [mores] of nature from her other contrivances [machinamentis]. Add that the first motion would be in a circle about its own centre: but such [a motion] the Phenomena of the moon reject. Let this therefore remain: that the moon is not stirred [cieri] immediately by the fluxes of vapours set in motion by the sun. [p. 242] But will you do me liberally some favour [gratiam]? |
| Andab. Pete. | Andab. Ask. |
| Ereu. Fac itaque lunam cum hoc suo vaporum motu effluviis terrestribus esse demersam: & expedi mihi quid inde eventurum sit? | Ereu. Make [it so], then, that the moon, with this its own motion of vapours, has been sunk in the terrestrial effluvia: and unfold [expedi] to me what will come about thence? |
| [Margin: Lunam ab effluviis terrae moveri.] Andab. Circumvenisti me, Ereuni, nisi enim per dolum me cepisses, nunquam a me extorsisses ut lunam telluris obsessam effluviis concederem. Sed perinde est: largior, non concedo. Petis autem quid inde sit futurum, nempe hoc ut luna cum tellure singulis viginti quatuor horis circumagatur a terra, & ipsius effluviis? | [Margin: That the moon is moved by the effluvia of the earth.] Andab. You have got round [circumvenisti] me, Ereunius; for unless you had taken me by guile, you would never have wrung from me that I should grant the moon beset [obsessam] by the effluvia of the earth [telluris]. But it is all one [perinde est]: I bestow [it], I do not concede [it]. And you ask what will be thence; namely this: that the moon with the earth [tellure] in each twenty-four hours is driven round by the earth [terra], and by its effluvia? |
| Ereu. Credis hoc cum vapores lunam movere non possint, ab effluviis telluris eandem iri motum? | Ereu. Do you believe this, since the vapours cannot move the moon, that by the effluvia of the earth the same [moon] will be moved [iri motum]? |
| Andab. Stet itaque (per me licet) si eam a nullo vis pati motum. | Andab. Let it stand, then (for my part it is allowed [per me licet]), if you wish it to suffer motion from nothing. |
| Ereu. Sed quomodo stare poterit, si tota eam complexa & amplexa moles vaporum, in gyrum agatur. Necessum enim est aliquam ei fixuram depangere qua haereat si loco suo moto non & ipsa moveatur; Hanc autem qualem fingere potes? | Ereu. But how will it be able to stand, if the whole bulk [moles] of vapours, having embraced and enfolded [complexa & amplexa] it, is driven in a ring [in gyrum]. For it is necessary to drive in [depangere] some fixing [fixuram] for it, by which it may hold fast, if, its place having been moved, it itself also is not to be moved; and this [fixing], of what sort can you feign [fingere]? |
| Andab. Cur itaque a vaporibus suis moveri eandem recusabas, si nunc ab alienis exagitatum eam vadis? | Andab. Why then did you refuse that the same [moon] is moved by its own vapours, if now you go to stir her up [exagitatum eam vadis] by foreign [ones]? |
| [p. 243] Ereu. Attende duorum motuum naturam. Videbis in priori divelli ab iis quibus naturaliter haeret, vel certe ea secum deferre, palam proinde est vi & causa opus ad hoc esse. In posteriori vero ut maneat & non moveatur, parem dilacerationem, & quod sequitur, causae & virtutis naturam requiri. Esse itaque unde ab terrenis effluviis progressum capiat, quantumvis a propriis idem non possit pati. | [p. 243] Ereu. Attend to the nature of the two motions. You will see in the former [that it] is torn away from those things to which it naturally clings, or certainly carries them with itself; it is plain accordingly that there is need of force and cause for this. But in the latter, that it remain and not be moved, [you will see that] an equal tearing-apart [dilacerationem] and, what follows, the nature of a cause and virtue [virtutis] is required. [You will see] therefore [that there] is [that] whence it may take progress [progressum] from the earthy effluvia, however much it cannot suffer the same from its own. |
| Andab. Attamen fixura est opus, & valida quidem; Cum enim luna 28. tandem diebus tellurem circumeat, hoc est, eam periodum exigat quam effluvia telluris 24. horis, tantae tarditatis tibi causa reddenda est. | Andab. And yet there is need of a fixing [fixura], and a strong one indeed; For since the moon goes round the earth [tellurem] only at length [tandem] in 28 days, that is, completes [exigat] that circuit [periodum] which the effluvia of the earth [complete] in 24 hours, the cause of so great a slowness must be rendered [reddenda] by you. |
| [Margin: Gravitatem facere ut luna tardius moveatur.] Ereu. Utinam etiam gravitare lunam versus telluris centrum mihi gratificareris. Ea lege qua densa in fluminibus ipso tardius flumine decurrere cernimus, tecum agerem. | [Margin: That heaviness [gravitatem] makes the moon be moved more slowly.] Ereu. Would that you would also gratify me [with this]: that the moon gravitates [gravitare] towards the centre of the earth [telluris]. By that law by which we perceive dense things in rivers to run down more slowly than the river itself, I would deal with you. |
| Andab. Non admitto, ipsus enim lunam non gravitare hesterna disputatione, cum tibi serviret, affirmasti. Tuis itaque te verbis alligo & omnis palinodiae iure tibi interdico. | Andab. I do not admit [it]; for you yourself [ipsus] affirmed in yesterday’s disputation, when it served you, that the moon does not gravitate. With your own words therefore I bind you, and I forbid [interdico] you the right of every palinode [palinodiae]. |
| Ereu. Cogis me itaque dictum inficiari. | Ereu. You compel me, then, to deny [what was] said. |
| Andab. Ego vero ad te provoco, Asphali, num cum heri astris a ruina metuerem si nostratium elementorum forent participes, non iste Ereunius opposuerit, nihil verendum [p. 244] quoniam attrahens versus centrum nullum appareret. Nunc cum figenda est luna, ne plus quam ipsi opportunum sit a terrae effluviis trahatur, vult pro postulato accipere tellurem versus ipsam gravitare. | Andab. I for my part appeal [provoco] to you, Asphalius: whether, when yesterday I feared for the stars from ruin if they were partakers of the elements of our country [nostratium], this Ereunius did not object [that] nothing was to be feared, [p. 244] since no [thing] attracting towards the centre appeared. Now, when the moon is to be fixed, lest it be drawn by the effluvia of the earth more than is convenient for him [ipsi], he wishes to take as a postulate [pro postulato] that it itself gravitates towards the earth [tellurem versus]. |
| Ereu. Si non aliud petis nihil arbitro opus est; habes me confitentem. Sed enim tum temporis nihil gratificatus fueras de incessu lunae infra telluris regnum. Et si quidem quod pluries monui, ab extrinseco habetur, ut moles aliqua ad medium devergat, aut contra fortassis, ex hac ipsa conclusione lunae sequetur, eam gravium esse classi connumerandam: ut iam non ex gratia, sed iure tanquam ad concessa consequens hoc postulem. Sed non sunt alienae messi immittendae manus. | Ereu. If you ask nothing else, there is no need of an arbiter; you have me confessing. But at that time [tum temporis] you had granted [gratificatus fueras] nothing about the moon’s going [incessu] beneath the kingdom of the earth [telluris]. And if indeed, as I have warned more than once, it is had from without [ab extrinseco] that some bulk [moles] inclines [devergat] to the middle, or perhaps the contrary, from this very conclusion about the moon it will follow that it is to be numbered with the class of heavy things [gravium]: so that now not out of favour [ex gratia] but by right, as consequent upon the things conceded, I may demand [postulem] this. But hands are not to be thrust into another’s harvest [alienae messi]. |
| Andab. Me quoque molliorem habe, gratum enim mihi accidit videre unde aestus marini cum lunae motu concursus evenit: dum enim aestus ut pote telluri proximus contra eiusdem motum in singulos dies 28. parte itineris proficit, & luna 27. partium mensura ab effluviorum cursu deficit, ut pote in supremo orbe collocata, ubi & vapores tenuissimi & orbita spatiosissima est, fit ut lunae & aestus pares cursus a contrariis causis profecti eandem sortiantur legem. | Andab. Have me also [as] softer; for it has befallen [me] as a pleasing thing to see whence the concurrence [concursus] of the sea-tide [aestus marini] with the motion of the moon comes about: for while the tide, inasmuch as [it is] nearest to the earth [telluri], advances against its motion each day by a 28th part of the journey, and the moon falls short of the course of the effluvia by the measure of 27 parts, inasmuch as [it is] placed in the highest orb, where both the vapours are most thin and the orbit most spacious, it comes about that the equal courses of the moon and of the tide, proceeding from contrary causes, obtain by lot [sortiantur] the same law. |
| Ereu. Modo sortiantur, hoc enim imprimis [p. 245] patentissimum est, aestum duplici cursu & recursu horam suam deficere quam uno luna circuitu proficit. [Margin: {?Aestui} & lunae motus non congruere.] Ut videas hoc Phaenomenon quod tot & tantos authores in eam sententiam illaqueavit ut aestum a luna suspenderent, in ipso suo cardine vacillare. Praeterea aestum meridianae lunae culminationi respondere a nemine quod sciam artifice notatum est, caeterarum vero horarum incerta lex est ut nihil liquidi statui possit. | Ereu. If only [modo] they obtained [it]; for this is above all [p. 245] most patent, that the tide in a double course and recourse falls short [by] its hour, which the moon gains [proficit] in one circuit. [Margin: That the motions of the tide and of the moon do not agree.] So that you may see this Phenomenon, which has ensnared so many and so great authors into that opinion that they made the tide hang [suspenderent] on the moon, totter on its very hinge [cardine]. Moreover, that the tide answers to the noon culmination [culminationi] of the moon has been noted by no craftsman [artifice], that I know of; but of the other hours the law is uncertain, so that nothing clear [liquidi] can be established. |
| [Margin: Aestum rubri maris non esse menstruum.] Asphal. Immo oculatum tibi maioris mysterii testimonium fero ex Codino quodam, qui de se an de alio loquatur non satis memini; sed asserit dum mare rubrum navigaret, idem quatuordecim totos dies sensim inflatum, totidem alios decubuisse, & hoc tanta constantia affirmat ut male feriatos vocet, aestum in aliam causam quam lunarem remittere ausos. | [Margin: That the tide of the Red Sea is not monthly [menstruum].] Asphal. Nay rather, I bring you an eye-witness [oculatum] testimony of a greater mystery out of a certain Codinus, who, whether he speaks of himself or of another, I do not well remember; but he asserts [that] while he was sailing the Red Sea, the same [sea], swollen gradually for fourteen whole days, lay down [decubuisse] for as many others; and he affirms this with so great constancy that he calls ill-employed [male feriatos] those who have dared to refer [remittere] the tide to another cause than the lunar. |
| Ereu. Narratne author quibus instrumentis, quibus argumentis, quibus littoribus notatis hoc collegerit qui expertus fuerit artifex, alioqui mihi facile persuaserim virum probum alienis notis & argumentis hoc sibi imponi passum. His itaque dimissis quem putas actum sibi secaturam lunam an ipsum eum quem terra sub Ecliptica terit, aut proprium aliquem sibi selecturam. | Ereu. Does the author relate with what instruments, with what arguments, with what shores noted, he gathered this, [and] what craftsman [artifex] [it was who] had experienced [it]? Otherwise I could easily persuade myself that the honest man suffered this to be imposed on him by others’ notes and arguments. These things therefore dismissed: which track [actum] do you think the moon will cut for itself, whether that very one which the earth wears [terit] under the Ecliptic, or will choose for itself some proper [one]? |
| Andab. In aperto constitutum est lunam [p. 246] propriam sibi semitam calcare, sed quo iure non satis video. | Andab. It is established in the open that the moon [p. 246] treads a proper path [semitam] for itself, but by what right I do not well see. |
| Ereu. Atmosphaeram itaque in omnibus sui partibus aequalem credis an aliquo modo variegatam? | Ereu. The Atmosphere, then, do you believe [it] equal in all its parts, or in some way variegated? |
| [Margin: Cur luna non feratur sub Ecliptica.] Andab. Satis intelligo solis rotis qua transeunt accendi & dilatari Atmosphaeram, atque proinde tota quam vocant torridam, Zona, vapores elevatiores esse & multiplicatos, sed quid inde moliris? | [Margin: Why the moon is not carried under the Ecliptic.] Andab. I understand well enough that by the wheels of the sun, where they pass across, the Atmosphere is kindled and dilated, and accordingly in the whole Zone which they call torrid the vapours are more elevated and multiplied; but what are you contriving [moliris] thence? |
| Ereu. Scire aveo postquam vapores ad summum orbem ascenderint quod in Zona torrida sola potest evenire, quid iis fiat? | Ereu. I long to know, after the vapours have ascended to the highest orb, which can come about in the torrid Zone alone, what becomes of them? |
| Andab. Credo ubi refrixerint, aliquantulum decumbere in propinquas partes humiliores, & sic toto orbe rotundari: sicut enim aquam cernimus & quae in illa natant, ad mensuram suae gravitatis centro aquae libratione applicari, sic necessum est etiam in vaporibus contingere postquam gravitatis in iis naturam agnoscimus. | Andab. I believe [that], when they have grown cold, [they] lie down a little into the neighbouring lower parts, and so are rounded in the whole orb: for just as we perceive water, and the things which float in it, to be applied to the centre of the water by balancing [libratione] according to the measure of their heaviness [gravitatis], so it is necessary [that it] befall also in vapours, after [postquam] we acknowledge the nature of heaviness in them. |
| Ereu. Habes itaque Zonam torridam, maxime vero eam partem, cui sol rectius imminet, perpetuo tumultu agi aliorum vaporum ascendentium, aliorum reiectorum; fit itaque ut si luna sub Ecliptica situm occupet, brevis futura sit morae illa statio; solent enim si notavisti, flumina innatantia sibi tardiora paulatim ex ipso torrente proturbare, & ad la- [p. 247] tus littusque propellere: quod hic maiori necessitate certum est, postquam vides ipsum flumen nimia exturgescentia egurgitare se; quare & secum innatantia. Sic autem propulsa quam lineam assumant? | Ereu. You have, therefore, [that] the torrid Zone, but most of all that part over which the sun hangs more directly [rectius], is driven by a perpetual tumult of vapours, some ascending, others thrown back; it comes about therefore that, if the moon occupies a site under the Ecliptic, that station [statio] will be of brief stay [morae]; for rivers are wont, if you have noted [it], to thrust out little by little from the torrent itself the floating things slower than themselves, and to push [them] to the [p. 247] side and the shore: which here is certain by a greater necessity, since you see the river itself disgorge [egurgitare] itself by excessive swelling-up [exturgescentia]; and therefore also the things floating with it. But, thus pushed forth, what line would they assume? |
| [Margin: Cur bis in mense Eclipticam secet.] Andab. Postquam indubitatum est naturam per lineas brevissimas agere, in superficie autem sphaerica brevissima semper est circuli maximi pars; per circulum aliquem maximum telluris volutari lunam vel natare potius consentaneum est. Et ego, iam praeire tibi possum quo tendis. Quoniam enim circulus maximus quemlibet alium in duo dividit aequalia, & in mediis inter sectiones semper maxima utrimque est circulorum distantia: Hinc inferes lunam bis in mense Eclipticam subire, & iterum toties ab eadem in Septentrionem & Austrum summe removeri. Et hoc omnino negare non sustineo: fert enim secum evidentiam, sed unde sumemus quantam esse oporteat hanc distantiam? | [Margin: Why it cuts the Ecliptic twice in a month.] Andab. Since it is undoubted that nature acts through the shortest lines, and on a spherical surface the shortest is always a part of a greatest circle [circuli maximi]; it is fitting [consentaneum] that the moon roll, or rather swim, along some greatest circle of the earth [telluris]. And I can now go before you [to] where you are tending. For since a greatest circle divides any other [greatest circle] into two equal [parts], and in the middles between the sections the distance of the circles on either side is always greatest: Hence you will infer that the moon twice in a month goes under the Ecliptic, and again as often is removed from the same to the North and the South to the utmost [summe]. And this I cannot bring myself [non sustineo] to deny altogether: for it carries evidence with itself; but whence shall we take how great this distance must be? |
| Andab. [sic; read Ereu.] Nos quidem ab experimento; ut quantam Astronomicis mensuris accipimus, tantam eam credamus: Ipsa autem luna a compositione & ordine effluviorum; donec enim impetus eam eiiciens validior est quam ut contra niti possit, reflectetur ab ea. Ubi convenienter eam superaverit, suo actu fluet. Nosti enim, credo, in sphaera mota [p. 248] non aequaliter omnes partes ferri, sed vehementius eas quae proxime circulum maximum ambiunt; caeteras, quo remotiores eo minus, donec ad ipsos Polos immotos progressio terminetur. | Andab. [sic; read Ereu.] We, indeed, from experiment; so that as great as we receive it by Astronomical measures, so great we may believe it [to be]: but the moon itself, from the composition and order of the effluvia; for as long as the impetus casting it out is stronger than that it can strive against [it], it will be bent back [reflectetur] from it. When it has suitably overcome it, it will flow by its own act [suo actu]. For you know, I believe, that in a sphere moved [p. 248] not all the parts are carried equally, but more vehemently those which go round nearest the greatest circle; the others, the more remote, the less, until at the unmoved Poles themselves the progression is ended. |
| Andab. Sed vide ne tuo te laqueo irretieris, si enim motus quo remotior est a maiori circulo, eo debilior est, eveniet profecto ut luna quae ab Ecliptica remotior est, eo etiam minus contra suam inclinationem a motu telluris agatur: quare motus ipsius in maxima distantia, quam quadraturam appellant, erit languidissimus, & in ipsa Ecliptica vehementissimus; Cum tamen Astronomi sub Ecliptica languere, in quadraturis autem efferri notent & doceant. | Andab. But see that you do not ensnare yourself in your own noose; for if motion, by as much as it is more remote from the greater circle, by so much is weaker, it will surely come about that the moon, which [the more] remote it is from the Ecliptic, by so much the less also is driven against its inclination [inclinationem] by the motion of the earth [telluris]: wherefore its motion at the greatest distance, which they call the quadrature [quadraturam], will be most languid, and in the Ecliptic itself most vehement; Whereas nevertheless the Astronomers note and teach [that it] languishes under the Ecliptic, but in the quadratures is carried out [efferri]. |
| [Margin: Cur luna in quadraturis sit velocior.] Ereu. Non expectabam a te, Andabata, tantam Astronomices peritiam, sed quoniam quando vis in omnibus vales, experiamur si quis tibi satisfaciendi supersit modus. Notavistine proinde tum in aquis tum vel maxime in aere vehementiori motui vim subesse condensandi? | [Margin: Why the moon is swifter in the quadratures.] Ereu. I did not expect from you, Andabata, so great a skill in Astronomy [Astronomices]; but since, when you wish, you are strong in all things, let us try whether any mode remains of satisfying you. Have you noted, accordingly, both in waters and even most of all in air, that under a more vehement motion there lies [subesse] a force [vim] of condensing? |
| Andab. Rarefaciendi saepius inaudivi, condensandi nunquam. | Andab. Of rarefying I have quite often heard tell [inaudivi]; of condensing, never. |

## Words and phrases with alternative or special meanings

- ***Lunam non moveri immediate a sole*** — rendered “That the Moon is not moved immediately by the sun”. not directly / without intermediary Knot title: White denies that the sun moves the moon through the moon's own vapour-winds (the moon-analogue of his solar wind theory of the tides); the moon is moved instead by the earth's effluvia.
- ***Meteora*** — rendered “Meteors”. all atmospheric phenomena (clouds, winds, rain) Aristotelian 'Meteorologica': sublunary phenomena, not only shooting stars.
- ***ex figura*** — rendered “from [its] figure”. appearance, telescopic shape The moon's mountainousness inferred from its telescopic appearance (Galileo, Sidereus nuncius).
- ***Atlantes & Tenariffi*** — rendered “Atlases and Teneriffes”. the highest mountains Atlas and the Pico de Teide (Tenerife), stock examples of the highest mountains in early modern geography.
- ***crepuscula ... ad quinquagesimum milliare*** — rendered “twilights ... to the fiftieth mile”. height of the twilight-producing atmosphere Alhazen's estimate of the height of the vapours causing twilight (c. 50 miles), widely repeated (e.g. by Kepler, Digby).
- ***cieri / cietur*** — rendered “be stirred / will be stirred”. set in motion, roused Physical sense of cieo; reading {?c}ietur on p. 241 supported by 'cieri' at the end of the same speech.
- ***carceres*** — rendered “barriers”. starting-stalls of the circus; prisons; confines Circus image: the vapours run only within their own enclosures.
- ***spiritus ... scissiles*** — rendered “spirits ... divisible”. subtle airs/vapours; easily cut 'spiritus' here physical (thin exhalations), not soul.
- ***primus motus*** — rendered “the first motion”. the immediate (first-caused) motion Here not the Ptolemaic primum mobile, but the motion the vapours would first impose: rotation about the moon's own centre.
- ***gratiam facere / gratificari*** — rendered “do a favour / gratify”. grant, concede as a favour Disputation idiom: a premise granted 'ex gratia' as opposed to 'iure' (by right), p. 244.
- ***largior, non concedo*** — rendered “I bestow [it], I do not concede [it]”. I grant for argument's sake but do not admit Scholastic distinction between granting a premise for the sake of argument and conceding it as true.
- ***iri motum*** — rendered “will be moved”. future passive infinitive Supine + iri.
- ***exagitatum eam vadis*** — rendered “you go to stir her up”. supine of purpose with verb of motion; or a misprint for 'exagitatam [esse]' Taken as supine (cf. 'cubitum ire'); uncertain.
- ***fixura*** — rendered “fixing”. fastening, anchorage, peg What would hold the moon still in a rotating vapour-sphere; 'depangere' (drive in) continues the image.
- ***dilaceratio*** — rendered “tearing-apart”. rending, disruption Ereunius: staying still against a moving medium requires as much violence as being torn away.
- ***periodus*** — rendered “circuit”. period Glossary rendering kept (spatial circuit), though here also temporal (28 days vs 24 hours).
- ***gravitare*** — rendered “gravitate”. tend downward, be heavy Glossary term; Andabata recalls yesterday's (Dialogue I) denial that the moon gravitates.
- ***palinodia*** — rendered “palinode”. recantation Stesichorus' recantation; the disputant forbidden to retract.
- ***ab extrinseco*** — rendered “from without”. from an extrinsic cause White's repeated thesis that heaviness/lightness is not an intrinsic quality but given from outside.
- ***alienae messi immittendae manus*** — rendered “hands to be thrust into another's harvest”. to meddle with another's business Proverbial (falcem in alienam messem mittere); here: the doctrine of heaviness belongs to another discussion.
- ***aestus marinus*** — rendered “sea-tide”. tide White's tide theory (Dialogue II, earlier knots) assigns the tide to the earth's motion and winds, not the moon.
- ***culminatio*** — rendered “culmination”. transit of the meridian Astronomical term: moon's passage over the meridian at noon.
- ***artifex*** — rendered “craftsman”. expert, practitioner Used of a skilled observer (cf. 'ex arte').
- ***Codinus*** — rendered “Codinus”. unidentified An author who reports a 14-day rise and 14-day fall of the Red Sea; not identified (Georgius Codinus, the Byzantine, seems unlikely).
- ***male feriatos*** — rendered “ill-employed”. idle, frivolous, at leisure in a bad way Horace, Odes IV.6.14 ‘male feriatos Troas’: those idly or wrongly at holiday.
- ***oculatum testimonium*** — rendered “eye-witness testimony”. ocular witness Plautine 'testis oculatus'.
- ***actus*** — rendered “track”. path, right of way; act Roman land term (a cattle-track); a lunar path, contrasted with 'suo actu' (by its own act) on p. 247.
- ***sub Ecliptica*** — rendered “under the Ecliptic”. in the plane of the ecliptic White: the moon cannot keep to the ecliptic because the torrid zone's vapour tumult pushes it aside, like floating things to a river bank.
- ***rotae solis*** — rendered “wheels of the sun”. the sun's chariot Poetic (Phaethon's chariot).
- ***libratio*** — rendered “balancing”. equilibration Floating bodies settle by weight around the centre.
- ***circulus maximus*** — rendered “greatest circle”. great circle Spherical geometry: shortest path on a sphere; two great circles bisect each other, giving two nodes and two greatest latitudes.
- ***quadratura*** — rendered “quadrature”. 90° elongation from the sun Here (Andabata) used of the moon's greatest distance from the ecliptic (properly its greatest latitude, 90° from the nodes); astronomically ‘quadrature’ is 90° elongation from the sun. White's conflation is kept as printed.
- ***efferri*** — rendered “be carried out”. be swifter, be borne along strongly Opposed to 'languere' (languish).
- ***Astronomices*** — rendered “of Astronomy”. Greek genitive of Astronomice Greek-form noun.
- ***vis condensandi / rarefaciendi*** — rendered “force of condensing / rarefying”.  Ereunius' claim that rapid motion condenses air and water prepares his explanation of the moon's speed in the quadratures (part 2).
- ***inaudivi*** — rendered “I have heard tell”. heard indirectly/by hearsay in-audire: to hear a rumour.

## Translator's notes

- Unit: Dialogue II, Knot xii, part 1 of 2. PDF pp. 250–259 = printed pp. 239–248, from Ereunius’ ‘Si tu quoque, Andabata, eodem es animo…’ (p. 239) to Andabata’s ‘Rarefaciendi saepius inaudivi, condensandi nunquam.’ (p. 248). Part 2 begins with Ereunius’ ‘Et ego fortassis secus quam oportuerat locutus sum’ on p. 248 (PDF 259) and runs to the start of Knot xiii on p. 256.
- Offset +11 holds (running heads 239–248; signatures Hh p. 241, Hh ij p. 243, Hh iij p. 245). Marginal notes: p. 239 knot title; p. 242 ‘Lunam ab effluviis terrae moveri’; p. 243 ‘Gravitatem facere ut luna tardius moveatur’; p. 245 ‘{?Aestui} & lunae motus non congruere’ and ‘Aestum rubri maris non esse menstruum’; p. 246 ‘Cur luna non feratur sub Ecliptica’; p. 247 ‘Cur bis in mense Eclipticam secet’; p. 248 ‘Cur luna in quadraturis sit velocior’.
- Uncertain or difficult readings: (1) p. 241 ‘nequaquam {?c}ietur’: first letter broken; read cietur (cf. ‘cieri’ later in the same speech). (2) p. 247 the speech ‘Nos quidem ab experimento…’ is headed ‘Andab.’ though Andabata spoke the previous speech and Andabata answers ‘Sed vide ne tuo te laqueo irretieris’; almost certainly a misprint for ‘Ereu.’ — kept as printed with [sic; read Ereu.]. (3) p. 242 ‘ab alienis exagitatum eam vadis’: taken as supine of purpose (‘you go to stir her up’); perhaps a misprint for ‘exagitatam’. (4) p. 245 first word of the margin, ‘Æstui’ (or ‘Æstus’), partly blurred. (5) p. 244 ‘tellurem versus ipsam gravitare’: ‘versus’ taken as postpositive (tellurem versus = towards the earth), ipsam = the moon; the alternative ‘that the earth gravitates towards it’ contradicts the argument. (6) p. 247 ‘reflectetur ab ea’ / ‘eam superaverit’: ‘ea/eam’ probably the Ecliptic (or the impetus); rendered ‘it’. (7) p. 245 ‘Codinus’ not identified. (8) p. 243 ‘ipsus’ (archaic = ipse) and ‘interdieo’ printed for ‘interdico’ (normalised).
