*English title:* Knot XVII. Why most of men fall away [excidant] from beatitude.  
*Pages:* PDF pp. 435–446 = printed pp. 424–435 (from Ereunius’ ‘Ego vero nihil polliceor’ on p. 424 to Ereunius’ ‘…Quid ais esse motum?’ on p. 435).

## Dialogue III, Knot xvii (printed pp. 424–435)

| Latin | English |
|---|---|
| Ereu. Ego vero nihil polliceor. Sed quoniam hoc me levasti onere, ut rem ita se habere probem (quod difficillimum foret) hac qua praeivisti, tua premo vestigia, & tecum [Margin: Nodus XVII. Cur plerique hominum a beatitudine excidant.] suppono nationem hominum infimam cognoscentium creaturarum sedem occupare. | Ereu. I indeed [vero] promise nothing. But since you have relieved me of this burden, that I should prove the thing to be so (which would be most difficult), by this [way] by which you went before, I press your footsteps, and with you [Margin: Knot XVII. Why most of men fall away [excidant] from beatitude.] I suppose the nation [nationem] of men to occupy the lowest seat of knowing [cognoscentium] creatures. |
| Quid enim deterius post hoc expectari potest ut tot sint instrumenti ad finem ad quem natum est, & tam difficiles motus & ubi commune sit caeteris omnibus quae nos experimur, plerumque ad propositum finem pertingere, in paucis deviare, nostrum genus non tantum in plerisque deerret, sed vix aut ne vix quidem in paucis (si solam naturae vim intueamur) immo paucissimis ad foelicem portum pervehatur? | For what worse can be expected after this, [than] that [ut] there should be so many [things] of instrument [instrumenti] toward the end to which it was born, and such difficult motions, and [that], where it is common to all the others which we experience for the most part to reach through [pertingere] to the proposed end, [and] in few to deviate, our race [genus] not only should go astray [deerret] in most, but scarcely, or not even scarcely, in a few (if we look at the force of nature alone), nay [immo] in very few, should be carried through to the happy [foelicem] harbour? |
| Quod ex eo video necessario contingere, quod tres sunt in homine gradus omnes contingentia laborantes, quorum singuli priores praerequirant ad finem quem petimus obtinendum. | Which I see to happen [contingere] necessarily from this, that there are three grades [gradus] in man, all labouring with contingency [contingentia], of which each [singuli] pre-require [praerequirant] the prior [ones] for obtaining the end which we seek. |
| Intueamur itaque primo ipsam naturae humanae fabricam seu partem ut vocant vegetativam: nonne statim constat maiori artificio? atque adeo maiori contingentiae subiecta est, quam corpus cuiusvis animalis? sicut & animalia plantis, plantae mixtis, mixta elementis quae tamen singula, eorum ex quibus componuntur contingentiam pati necessum est. Maximae itaque contingentiae opus est homo, etiamsi solum corpus aspicias, nisi vero corpori recte sit, animo non potest esse: unde quid miraculi est, si stultorum plena sunt omnia, cum a plurimarum causarum ministerio fieri necesse sit, ut recte a passionibus sit dispositus? | Let us look therefore first at the very fabric [fabricam] of human nature, or the part, as they call [it], vegetative: does it not at once consist [constat] of greater artifice [artificio]? and so [atque adeo] is [it not] subject to greater contingency than the body of any animal whatever? just as also animals [are, than] plants, plants [than] mixed [bodies] [mixtis], mixed [bodies than] elements, each of which [singula] nevertheless must necessarily [necessum est] suffer the contingency of those from which they are composed. Man therefore is a work of the greatest contingency, even if you look at the body alone; but unless it is well [recte sit] with the body, it cannot be [well] with the mind [animo]: whence what [is there] of a marvel [miraculi], if all things are full of fools, since it must come about from the ministry of very many causes that [a man] be rightly disposed on the side of [a] the passions [passionibus]? |
| Sed esto homo mediocriter compositus ut possit sibi providere & societati aliorum hominum non esse valde incommodus, quae secunda est statio, superest corpore perfecto, animus quoque erudiendus aliqua morum probitate. Quantum enim cultae nationes a feris & barbaris hominum coetibus distent, legentibus historias navigationum modernarum satis constabit. | But let a man be [esto] moderately composed, so that he can provide for himself and not be very troublesome [incommodus] to the society of other men, which is the second station [statio]; there remains, the body having been perfected, the mind [animus] also to be instructed [erudiendus] with some uprightness [probitate] of morals. For how much cultivated nations stand apart from the wild and barbarous gatherings [coetibus] of men will be sufficiently evident to those reading the histories of modern navigations. |
| Ponamus itaque Graecis & Romanis cultiorem aliquam nationem; adhuc quantum distat a summa beatitate? cum eorum mens non nisi ad quietam & commodis huius vitae redundantem vitam erudita fuerit, a quibus omnibus abstrahenda est, ut in futura vita sit beata. Constat enim interire cum corpore quae hic foelicem praestant vitam secundum corpus: neque posse in futura vita sine amore illorum quae tunc animae secundum naturam futura sunt, consistere beatitudo. | Let us posit therefore some nation more cultivated than the Greeks and Romans; still how much does it stand apart from the highest blessedness [beatitate]? since their mind [mens] has been instructed for nothing but a life quiet and overflowing with the advantages [commodis] of this life, from all which it must be drawn away, that in the future life it may be blessed [beata]. For it is evident [constat] that those things perish with the body which here furnish [praestant] a happy [foelicem] life according to the body: nor [that] beatitude can stand [consistere] in the future life without love of those things which then will be according to nature for the soul. |
| Vides itaque novam contingentiam caeterisque omnibus maiorem sese homini ingerere, quae eum a beatitudine distineat. Cum enim dediscenda & aversanda sint omnia quibus prius inhaesimus & insuevimus, & quasi nova creatura exituri simus, vides, duplum nobis superesse & utrumque difficillimum laborem, ut & nota discutiamus & alienemus, & incognitis inhaereamus & violentia quadam insuescamus. | You see therefore a new contingency, and greater than all the others, thrust itself [sese ingerere] upon man, which may keep [distineat] him apart from beatitude. For since all things are to be unlearned and turned away from, to which we formerly clung and to which we became accustomed, and we are going to come out as it were a new creature, you see that a double labour, and each [part] most difficult, remains for us: that we both shake off [discutiamus] and estrange [alienemus] the known [things], and cling to the unknown [things] and by a certain violence become accustomed [to them]. |
| Vides itaque si misericordia quicquam misericordissimum saeculorum Patrem movere possit, longe nos propiores beneficiis omni reliqua creaturae suae parte futuros. Vides rursus non aliam de eo querimoniam institui posse, nisi quare nos condiderit: si enim existere debuimus, certe ex his elementis & partibus, cum his miseriis & contingentia existere necesse fuit. Ad hoc itaque, nisi fallor, devoluta est quaestio: Utrum praestiterit nos non esse quam vel sic esse genitos, ut inter tot miserias viventes ad ultimum beatitudo destituat. | You see therefore, if mercy can move the most merciful Father of the ages at all [quicquam], that we will be far nearer to [his] benefits than every remaining part of his creature [creaturae]. You see again that no other complaint can be set up [institui] about him, except why he founded [condiderit] us: for if we had to [debuimus] exist, certainly it was necessary [for us] to exist out of these elements and parts, with these miseries and contingency. To this therefore, unless I am mistaken, the question has rolled down [devoluta est]: whether it would have been better [praestiterit] for us not to be, than to have been begotten even [vel] thus, that beatitude should forsake [destituat] [us], living among so many miseries, at the last. |
| Asphal. Tantumdem mihi quoque videtur; & hoc si declaraveris, caetera tibi crimina ignosco. | Asphal. Just so much [tantumdem] seems [so] to me also; and if you shall have made this clear [declaraveris], I pardon you the other charges [crimina]. |
| Ereu. Immo tu, Asphali, vide, num quaestio de hoc ulla possit esse: cum enim non de eo quod Deo conveniens sit quem diximus a creatis bonis ex nulla parte pendere, quaestio exerceatur, conficitur hoc unum quaeri, an hominibus melius sit creari, etiam si non usque ad summum apicem beatitatis perducantur, quam neque hanc quam sortiuntur qualemcumque perfectionem possidere? Hoc autem num vel quaeri possit vide. | Ereu. Nay rather [immo] you, Asphalius, see whether there can be any question about this: for since the question is being exercised [exerceatur] not about that which is fitting for God, whom we said to depend in no part [ex nulla parte] on created goods, it is concluded [conficitur] that this one thing is asked: whether it is better for men to be created, even if they are not led through up to the highest summit of blessedness [beatitatis], than not even [neque] to possess this perfection, of whatever kind [qualemcumque], which they obtain [sortiuntur]? But see whether this can even [vel] be asked. |
| Asphal. Verum ubi nunc illa sapiens gubernatio, quae ipsum finem sine fine esse permittat? | Asphal. But where now [is] that wise governance [gubernatio], which should permit the end itself to be without end [sine fine]? |
| Ereu. Neque hoc dixerim, sed ad Theologos remitto, quibus quae supra naturam Deus adhibuerit, remedia docenda sunt. Huic mundo satis est hominum massam & caeteris omnibus perfectiorem, & eorum finem esse, & qualis qualis sit, talem constitutam esse, qualis secundum naturam fieri potuit. | Ereu. Neither would I say [dixerim] this; but I remit [it] to the Theologians, by whom the remedies which God may have applied above nature are to be taught. For this world it is enough that the mass [massam] of men is both more perfect than all the others and their end, and, of whatever sort it be [qualis qualis sit], [that] it has been constituted such as it could come about according to nature. |
| Neque si plura circa eandem beatitudinem tanquam ultimum finem versentur, crediderim aequaliter omnia ad illum perventura: Sed quorum natura incertitudini, & inconstantiae magis patet, ea rarius quae minus frequentiora evolabunt, etsi semel contingentiae locus factus fuerit, ultimum quoque gradum in summa contingentia, hoc est ut in paucissimis scopum foelicitatis attingat, collocari consequens est. | Nor, if several [things] are occupied [versentur] about the same beatitude as [their] ultimate end, would I believe [crediderim] that all will reach through [perventura] to it equally: but [those] whose nature lies open [patet] more to uncertainty and inconstancy, those [will fly out] more rarely; those which [lie open] less, [being] more frequent, will fly out [evolabunt]; although, once a place has been made for contingency, it is consequent that the last grade also be placed in the highest contingency, that is, that in very few it reaches [attingat] the target [scopum] of happiness [foelicitatis]. |
| Andab. Risum non continerem, Asphali, videns quomodo de repente illaqueatus manseris, nisi altior cordi dolor inhaereret. Ad te itaque, Ereuni, convertor non sine gemitu. Quo enim tandem evaserunt Philosophicae tuae demonstrationes? quo nos perduxisti? Equidem in ea semper fui sententia Philosophicas in utramque partem velitationes semper serena Theologiae & fidei pace stabiliendas & moderandas esse: alioqui in perniciem magis quam scientiam erupturas. Ecce quale in ipso, quod aiunt, portu naufragium passus es. | Andab. I would not hold back laughter, Asphalius, seeing how suddenly you have remained [manseris] ensnared [illaqueatus], unless a deeper grief clung to [my] heart. To you therefore, Ereunius, I turn, not without a groan. For whither at length have your Philosophical demonstrations come out [evaserunt]? whither have you led us? I indeed have always been of that opinion, that Philosophical skirmishes [velitationes] on either side are always to be stabilized and moderated by the serene peace of Theology and of faith: otherwise [they are] going to break out into ruin [perniciem] rather than into science. Behold what a shipwreck you have suffered in the very harbour, as they say. |
| Tormenta enim & cruciatus qui animabus impiorum in futuro saeculo parati sunt, tantos esse, ut praestet omnino nihil esse, indubitatum in Theologia est. Neque disputationi locum dat qui ipse suo ore enunciavit, melius fuisse traditori ut non fuisset natus ille homo. | For it is undoubted in Theology that the torments and tortures [cruciatus] which have been prepared for the souls of the impious in the future age are so great that it would be better [praestet] to be altogether nothing. Nor does he give room to disputation who himself with his own mouth pronounced [enunciavit] that it would have been better for the traitor that that man had not been born. |
| Tu vero, Ereuni, qui supra nos docuisti omnes actus nostros ab ipsa aeternitate catenatis a causarum necessitate passibus usque ad ipsum liberi arbitrii exercitium alligatos & perductos, quid habes respondere? Cur egregius ille, qualem eum facis, gubernator, tot hominum myriadum myriadas non aeternis & horrendis & ineffabilibus poenis gratis ante omne aevum addixerit, viam muniverit, gressus coegerit, fatalem poenam praestruxerit: quorsum posthac cum gemitu fundemus preces? quorsum per aspera carnem exercitia domabimus? quorsum sollicitudinem & extentum ad futura animum vel ipsi colere, vel aliis commendare valebimus? Heu, heu, heu, quo nos perduxisti, Ereuni, quo nos compegisti? | But you, Ereunius, who above taught us that all our acts, from eternity itself, by steps [passibus] chained [catenatis] by the necessity of causes, are bound and led through right up to the very exercise of free choice [liberi arbitrii], what have you to reply? Why should that excellent governor [gubernator], such as you make him, not [non] have consigned [addixerit] so many myriads of myriads of men gratuitously [gratis], before every age [aevum], to eternal and horrendous and ineffable punishments, [not have] fortified [muniverit] the way, compelled the steps, built in advance [praestruxerit] the fated [fatalem] punishment? to what end shall we hereafter pour out prayers with a groan? to what end shall we tame the flesh through harsh exercises? to what end shall we be able either ourselves to cultivate, or to commend to others, solicitude and a mind [animum] stretched out toward future things? Alas, alas, alas, whither have you led us, Ereunius, whither have you fastened [compegisti] us? |
| Ereu. Magna luges, Andabata, neque minus Oedipo Colonaeo. [Margin: An praestet non esse quam beatitudine excludi?] Noli tamen Philosophiae exprobrare, priusquam quid pro se allegare valeat audiveris. Tormenta etiam futuri saeculi agnoscit Philosophia: Neque, si his quae praesens vita patitur ea compares, nostra haec prae iis ludicra, flores, & rosas, & delicias ipsa Philosophia iurare dubitaverit. | Ereu. Great things you mourn, Andabata, and not less than Oedipus at Colonus [Colonaeo]. [Margin: Whether it is better [praestet] not to be than to be shut out from beatitude?] Do not, however, reproach Philosophy before you have heard what she is able to allege for herself. Philosophy also acknowledges the torments of the future age: nor, if you compare them with those which the present life suffers, would Philosophy herself hesitate [dubitaverit] to swear that these [things] of ours are, beside them [prae iis], playthings [ludicra], flowers, and roses, and delights. |
| Novit enim animae post mortem potentiam cum ea quam in materia habet, non pati proportionem; novit affectus quos hic colligit, illic ad proportionem status & existentiae excreturos: Novit itaque in immensum & bona & mala, non per hyperbolen, sed ipsa re esse augenda. Verum haec omnia cum nos doceat Philosophia, huc usque non attingit, ut melius illis non fuisse censeat. Si hoc tam certum est, ut ais, in Theologia, ex iis quae naturam superant, contigisse puta. Nos autem impraesentiarum Philosophiae meditata perdiscamus. | For she knows that the power [potentiam] of the soul after death does not suffer [non pati] proportion with that which it has in matter; she knows that the affections [affectus] which it gathers here will grow out [excreturos] there to the proportion of [its] state and existence: she knows therefore that both goods and evils are to be increased into the immense, not through hyperbole, but in the thing itself [ipsa re]. But although [cum] Philosophy teaches us all these things, she does not reach [attingit] thus far, that she should judge [censeat] it to have been better for them not to have been. If this is so certain, as you say, in Theology, consider [puta] it to have come about from those things which surpass nature. But let us at present [impraesentiarum] learn thoroughly the meditations [meditata] of Philosophy. |
| Video itaque hoc iudicium quod melius ipsis fuerit non esse, vel ex natura rei vel ex opinione personarum esse pensitandum; personas vero duas video examinandas: ipsum illum qui poenis irretitus manet, & prudentem aliquem qui rem humanitus existimet. Age itaque si naturam consulamus, quid facit aliud alio melius in natura? seu in qua ratione melioratio consideranda est? | I see therefore that this judgment, that it would have been [fuerit] better for them not to be, is to be weighed [pensitandum] either from the nature of the thing or from the opinion of persons; and I see two persons to be examined: that very one who remains entangled [irretitus] in punishments, and some prudent [man] who would estimate [existimet] the matter humanly [humanitus]. Come therefore, if we consult nature, what makes one thing better than another in nature? or in what account [ratione] is betterment [melioratio] to be considered? |
| Andab. Melioratio in ratione entis est, & melius in ratione entis est, quod plus de ente habet. | Andab. Betterment is in the account [ratione] of being [entis], and that is better in the account of being which has more of being. |
| Ereu. Et potest Andabata dubitare, num quod est, & quantum homo est, & plus quam homo est, & ita plusquam homo est, ut comparatio inter ipsum & hominem nulla maneat, plus habet de ente quam nihil, quam quod non existit. | Ereu. And can Andabata doubt whether that which is, and [is] as much as a man is, and [is] more than a man is, and is more than a man so [much] that no comparison remains between it and a man, has more of being than nothing, than that which does not exist? |
| Asphal. Si me irrisisses, Andabata, nunc parem tibi gratiam retulissem. Non video enim quam responsionem vel fingere probabilem possis? | Asphal. If you had laughed at me, Andabata, I would now have returned you equal thanks [parem gratiam]. For I do not see what answer you could even feign [as] probable? |
| Ereu. Verum quoniam malis pressos videmus mortem invocare, consulamus ipsos infoelices. Tormenta quae natura nos erudit in miseris inveniri, affectus ipsorummet sunt invidia, de qua Poeta ait Siculos non invenisse Tyrannos tormentum maius. Dolorem omnes sciunt quam grave sit malum, eum dico quam tristitiam dicimus: Desiderium praeterea Narcissi fabula & experimenta plurima quantum habeat mali testantur: Metu mortuos non rarum esse meminisse; Sed hoc volo a te discere, num unquam audiveris de invido qui non ipsam invidiam {?amaverit}? | Ereu. But since we see those pressed by evils invoke death, let us consult the unhappy [infoelices] themselves. The torments which nature teaches [erudit] us to be found in the wretched are affections [affectus] of these very [men] themselves [ipsorummet]: envy, about which the Poet says the Sicilian Tyrants did not find a greater torment. Grief [dolorem], all know how grave an evil it is, I mean that which we call sadness [tristitiam]: longing [desiderium], moreover, how much of evil it has, the fable of Narcissus and very many experiments [experimenta] testify: that [men] dead from fear are not rare, [one may] remember [meminisse]. But this I wish to learn from you, whether you have ever heard of an envious man who did not {?love} envy itself? |
| Desiderium & Zelotypiam celebrant Poetae, quasi nullus citra ea sit amantium fructus, moestos tenebras & omnia quae malum augent quaerere cottidie experimur. Malum fugiunt meticulosi, sed ipsum timorem adamant: Haec si vera sint, vide num hi miseri sine his poenis esse velint? Et si non velint iis cedere, cur credas eos malle non esse quam illas sustinere? Omnis spirituum poena voluntaria est, & non potest (quoniam ex voluntate proficiscitur) non voli, & si volitur ipsa miseria, credine potest eos non esse malle quam haec pati? | Longing and jealousy [Zelotypiam] the Poets celebrate, as if there were no fruit of lovers without [citra] them; we experience daily that the sorrowful seek darkness and all things which increase the evil. The fearful [meticulosi] flee evil, but they dote upon [adamant] fear itself: if these things are true, see whether these wretches would wish to be without these punishments? And if they do not wish to yield them up [iis cedere], why would you believe them to prefer not to be rather than to sustain them? Every punishment of spirits is voluntary, and cannot (since it proceeds from the will) not be willed [voli]; and if the misery itself is willed [volitur], can it be believed [credine potest] that they prefer not to be than to suffer these things? |
| Andab. Non contentiose obstiterim quin haec illi velint; sic tamen ut causas eorum nolint, & quod ex his conficitur, absolute ea nolint, velintque tantummodo si obiecta sint, quod est non simpliciter, sed secundum quid haec illis esse voluntaria, ut ipse optime nosti. | Andab. I would not contentiously resist [obstiterim] that they will these things; yet so that they do not will [nolint] their causes, and — what is concluded [conficitur] from these — absolutely do not will them, and will [them] only if they are set before [them] [obiecta sint]; which is [to say] that these things are voluntary to them not simply [simpliciter], but in a certain respect [secundum quid], as you yourself know best. |
| Ereu. Ego? Immo oppositum novi. Quoniam enim malum ipsorum non in obiectis, sed in affectione inest, affectum autem pertinaciter & sine causa retinent; absit ut ego vel etiam tu affectus hos involuntarios & coactos credamus; cum ex opposito sint contra ipsam rationem perverse voluntarii, & non ex alia necessitate quam propriae inclinationis. An autem praestet non esse quam eos affectus habere, tu videris qui Theologica magnifice tractas. Scio tamen similia interdum quasi proverbialiter dicta ad exprimendam poenae gravitatem intelligi posse. | Ereu. I? Nay [immo], I have known the opposite. For since their evil is present not in the objects [obiectis] but in the affection [affectione], and they retain the affection [affectum] pertinaciously and without cause; far be it that I, or even you, should believe these affections involuntary and coerced [coactos]; since on the contrary [ex opposito] they are perversely voluntary against reason itself, and from no other necessity than that of [their] own inclination. But whether it be better [praestet] not to be than to have those affections, you will see to it [tu videris], who treat Theological things magnificently. I know, however, that similar [things], said at times as if proverbially, can be understood [as] for expressing the gravity of the punishment. |
| Nisi forte signate expressum sit natum non esse ad significandum fructus, seu voluptates huius vitae non esse pretium tantis poenis dignum; sive simpliciter melius fuisse, esse abortivum, & de utero translatum ad tumulum, quod non significat non esse; si quidem abortivorum animae supersunt, sicut caeterorum hominum. Sed vita mortali non esse perfruitum. | Unless perhaps 'not to have been born' [natum non esse] has been pointedly [signate] expressed for signifying that the fruits, or pleasures, of this life are not a price worthy of such great punishments; or simply [that] it would have been better to be an untimely birth [abortivum], and carried over from the womb to the tomb, which does not signify not to be, since indeed the souls of untimely births survive, just as [those] of the other men; but not to have enjoyed [perfruitum] mortal life. |
| His positis, hoc etiam intuere in quo possis primae causae administrationem calumniari? Num quia non plus dedit quam patiebatur natura? An quia constanter & ex praevisione egit? Si enim ita rexit humanam libertatem ut quantum boni ex natura advenire poterat, haberet; Nonne gratiis & non maledictis incessenda erit? poenas vero quomodo Theologi intelligant, habes quos consulas: si Philosophum consulas, invenies eas non nisi fructus perversae libertatis esse. | These things being posited, look also at this: in what could you calumniate [calumniari] the administration [administrationem] of the first cause? Is it because he gave no more than nature allowed [patiebatur]? Or because he acted constantly and out of foresight [praevisione]? For if he so ruled human liberty that it should have as much of good as could come to [it] from nature, must it [the administration] not be assailed [incessenda] with thanks and not with curses? but how the Theologians understand the punishments, you have [men] whom you may consult: if you consult the Philosopher, you will find them to be nothing but the fruits of perverse liberty. |
| Necessum enim est & bonum hominem quo magis crescit, eo etiam maiores pietatis fructus percipere: & perversum, quo magis exercet malitiam, eo semper in peius incrementis non imparibus proficere. Quo item tragica tua desperatio? num propterea minus ad virtutem conandum est, quia a Deo nostrae operae curantur & gubernantur? at nullus hoc causatur quo agricultura, mercatura, navigatione desistat. Cur magis virtutem deserant, nisi quia minus est animi & affectus? | For it is necessary both that the good man, the more he grows, by so much [eo] also gathers [percipere] greater fruits of piety: and [that] the perverse [man], the more he exercises malice, by so much always advances [proficere] toward the worse by increments not unequal. Whither likewise [goes] your tragic despair? surely [num] one is not on that account less to strive toward virtue, because our works [operae] are cared for and governed by God? but no one pleads [causatur] this so as to desist from agriculture, merchandise, navigation. Why should they rather desert virtue, unless because there is less of spirit [animi] and affection [affectus]? |
| Tandem de Philosophia, quod eam Theologiae subditam cupis, merito facis. Sed alia est subiectio medii ad finem, alia discipuli ad magistrum. [Margin: An Theologia Philosophis praescribere sententias debeat?] Neque est Philosophiae suprema & optima pars aliud quam via & praestructio itineris ad Theologicas veritates. Quomodo vero eam Philosophiae magistram optes nescio, cum Philosophica neque scire, neque docere profiteatur. | Finally, concerning Philosophy, that you desire her subject to Theology, you do [it] deservedly. But one is the subjection of the means [medii] to the end, another of the disciple to the master. [Margin: Whether Theology ought to prescribe opinions [sententias] to Philosophers?] Nor is the supreme and best part of Philosophy anything other than the way and the advance-building [praestructio] of the road to Theological truths. But how you would wish her [Theology] the mistress [magistram] of Philosophy, I do not know, since she professes neither to know nor to teach Philosophical things. |
| Liceat autem causari & Theologorum aliquod vitium non Theologiae. Volunt enim aliqui priusquam ipsa Theologiae scita intellexerint, ex puris & aequivocis vocibus Philosophiae praescribere quid doceat. Sed hi ignotum per ignotius ostendere conantur. Et plerumque ex Philosophia petendum est quid voces in Theologia valeant; e natura enim voces Theologicae desumuntur, & naturae illustratrix est Philosophia. | Let it be permitted, however, to plead [causari] also some vice of the Theologians, not of Theology. For some wish, before they have understood the very decrees [scita] of Theology, out of bare [puris] and equivocal words to prescribe to Philosophy what she should teach. But these attempt to show the unknown through the more unknown. And for the most part it must be sought from Philosophy what words are worth [valeant] in Theology; for Theological words are taken from nature, and the illuminatrix [illustratrix] of nature is Philosophy. |
| Praeterea si Philosophia scientia est, non ipsi intimandum est quaenam sequenda sit, cum certa via & fixo demonstrationis tramite incedat. Iis itaque praescribendae sunt sententiae qui Philosophiam prae se ferunt, vere tantummodo Logicam. h. e. in utrumque disserendi facultatem in materia Philosophica exercent. Horum mens, quoniam ex sese dubia est, recte Theologiae numine gubernatur, & ad unum flectitur. Vera Philosophia inflexilis est. | Moreover, if Philosophy is a science [scientia], it is not to be intimated [intimandum] to her which [opinion] is to be followed, since she advances by a certain way and a fixed path [tramite] of demonstration. To those therefore opinions [sententiae] are to be prescribed who display [prae se ferunt] Philosophy, [but] truly only Logic, that is [h. e.], exercise the faculty of arguing [disserendi] to either side in Philosophical matter. The mind [mens] of these, since of itself it is doubtful, is rightly governed by the divine power [numine] of Theology, and bent to one [side]. True Philosophy is inflexible. |
| [Margin: Elementa Metaphysica.] Asphal. Haec Philosophia, Ereuni, ubi quaerenda est? tentavisse te aliquid accepi in parte Metaphysica, quomodo successit? | [Margin: Metaphysical Elements.] Asphal. This Philosophy, Ereunius, where is it to be sought? I have heard [accepi] that you attempted something in the Metaphysical part; how did it succeed? |
| Ereu. Tentasse me non inficior, & tentavisse fere totam incipientem a communibus corporis notionibus, per partes substantiae & species, ad hominem & animam & spiritualia, Deum videlicet & Angelos penetravisse quinque elementis; sed quo successu, id aliorum iudicio desero, me certe non poenituit operis. Nam quantumlibet non tam immemor mei sim, ut nusquam peccavisse me arbitrer, pleraque tamen, nisi philautia me decipiat, ultra spem, sub pedibus mihi nata esse visa sunt. | Ereu. I do not deny that I attempted [it], and that, having attempted almost the whole [totam], beginning from the common notions of body, through the parts of substance and the species, I penetrated to man and the soul and spiritual things, namely God and Angels, in five elements [elementis]; but with what success, that I leave [desero] to the judgment of others; I certainly have not repented of the work. For however much I am not so unmindful of myself as to judge that I have nowhere sinned [peccavisse], yet most things, unless self-love [philautia] deceives me, beyond hope, seemed to me to have been born under [my] feet. |
| Asphal. Vide quam liberalis sim, Ereuni, ea conditione ut haec nobis elementa, alia vice stipuleris communia nobis facere, hodie te dimitto, cum nondum sit concubia nox. | Asphal. See how liberal I am, Ereunius: on this condition, that you stipulate [stipuleris] to make these elements common to us another time, I dismiss you today, since it is not yet the dead of night [concubia nox]. |
| Ereu. Mirum, ni Andabata te statua donet pro tanta liberalitate. Elementa vero nondum matura sunt, ut in Andabatae descendant iudicis aures & vestras. Memento item quomodo moneamur, ut decimum serventur in annum scripta non tantae rea censurae quam quae petis elementa. Ego vero rudem iure postulo, quam, si hanc ultimam quae te urebat curam demerem, pollicitus es, nunc autem ea vacuus ad alia converteris. | Ereu. [It is] a marvel if Andabata does not present you with a statue for so great liberality. But the elements are not yet ripe to descend into the ears of Andabata, the judge, and yours. Remember likewise how we are warned that writings liable [rea] to not so great a censure as the elements which you ask [for] be kept to the tenth year. I indeed demand by right the wooden sword [rudem], which you promised if I should remove this last care which was burning [urebat] you; but now, emptied [vacuus] of it, you turn to other things. |
| Andab. Ast intercedo ego, neque puto tam parum mentis Asphalio esse, ut cum exordium mundi & continuationem audiverit, sine fine velit discedere. Audiendus itaque es quid de exitio huius tam magnificae fabricae sentias. | Andab. But I interpose [intercedo], nor do I think there is so little mind in Asphalius that, when he has heard the beginning [exordium] of the world and [its] continuation, he would wish to depart without the end. You are therefore to be heard [as to] what you think about the destruction [exitio] of this so magnificent fabric [fabricae]. |
| Asphal. Agnosco insipientiam meam, Andabata, & iungo tecum manus. | Asphal. I acknowledge my folly [insipientiam], Andabata, and join hands with you. |
| Ereu. Immo mea qui duobus me Tyrannis & tortoribus vivens vidensque ultro subieci, & compedes laetus indui: Sed hoc saltem solatium est, quod de fine quaesiturus sum, cuius cogitatio suaviorem elaborabit laborem. Age itaque, Andabata: Quid ais esse motum? | Ereu. Nay [immo], mine [is the folly], who, living and seeing, of my own accord [ultro] subjected myself to two Tyrants and torturers, and gladly put on shackles: but this at least is a solace, that I am going to inquire about the end, the thought of which will work out [elaborabit] a sweeter labour. Come then, Andabata: what do you say motion is? |

## Words and phrases with alternative or special meanings

- ***contingentia*** — rendered “contingency [contingentia]”. chance-liability; the possibility of failing; also 'touching' in geometry. White's key notion in this Knot: the more complex a nature, the more points at which it can fail; man, the most complex, reaches his end most rarely. Scholastic sense (what can be otherwise), not 'chance event'.
- ***natio (hominum)*** — rendered “nation [nationem]”. race, kind, breed (of men). Here mankind as a species among 'knowing creatures', as gens in D3-N16; elsewhere in the same Knot natio = a people (cultae nationes).
- ***cognoscentes creaturae*** — rendered “knowing [cognoscentium] creatures”. cognitive creatures; creatures endowed with knowledge. Scholastic hierarchy: man lowest of the intellectual creatures (below angels), hence 'lowest seat'.
- ***gradus (tres in homine)*** — rendered “grades [gradus]”. degrees, steps, stages. The three stages: bodily constitution (vegetative part), moral civility, and the re-orientation of love to the life to come.
- ***pars vegetativa*** — rendered “the vegetative part”. the nutritive soul/faculty. Aristotelian De anima division of the soul (vegetative, sensitive, rational).
- ***mixta*** — rendered “mixed [bodies] [mixtis]”. compounds; elemental mixtures. As in glossary (D3-N13): mixta between elements and plants in the scale of composition.
- ***statio*** — rendered “station [statio]”. post, stopping-place, stage. Military/nautical metaphor continuing 'harbour' (portus).
- ***beatitas / beatitudo*** — rendered “blessedness [beatitas] / beatitude [beatitudo]”. synonyms; beatitas is Ciceronian (De nat. deor. I.95). Kept apart in English to show White's alternation.
- ***foelicitas / foelix*** — rendered “happiness [foelicitas] / happy [foelicem]”. felicity, good fortune. As in glossary: this-worldly happiness as against beatitude, though 'scopum foelicitatis' here is the final end.
- ***nova creatura*** — rendered “a new creature”. new creation. 2 Cor. 5:17 (si qua ergo in Christo nova creatura).
- ***gubernatio / gubernator*** — rendered “governance / governor”. government, steering; helmsman. Providential government (glossary D3-N09, D3-N16 p3); nautical overtone with portus/naufragium.
- ***sine fine*** — rendered “without end [sine fine]”. endless; without attaining its end. Asphalius' pun: governance that lets the 'end' (finis = purpose) be 'without end' (unattained, or everlasting).
- ***massa (hominum)*** — rendered “the mass [massam] of men”. lump, aggregate. Augustinian overtone of massa damnata/perditionis, which White sidesteps: mankind as a whole is the end of the lower creation.
- ***qualis qualis*** — rendered “of whatever sort [qualis qualis]”. such as it is. Colloquial doubled relative (Plautine/Ciceronian).
- ***evolare*** — rendered “fly out [evolabunt]”. escape, fly away, rise. Image of souls 'flying out' to beatitude; ellipsis in 'ea rarius, quae minus frequentiora evolabunt' read as correlative.
- ***velitationes*** — rendered “skirmishes [velitationes]”. light-armed sparring; verbal wrangling. Plautine word (velites); disputation in utramque partem.
- ***in ipso portu naufragium*** — rendered “shipwreck in the very harbour”. failure at the last moment. Proverb of failure at the last moment (cf. Erasmus, Adagia, 'In portu impingere'); continues the harbour (portus) image of p. 424.
- ***melius fuisse ... ut non fuisset natus ille homo*** — rendered “better ... that that man had not been born”. — Matt. 26:24 / Mark 14:21, on Judas (traditor).
- ***catenati passus*** — rendered “steps chained [catenatis passibus]”. linked steps. Andabata recalls Ereunius' doctrine of the causal chain (D3-N13–N15) and draws the predestinarian consequence.
- ***addicere*** — rendered “consign [addixerit]”. adjudge, sentence, devote. Legal term (the praetor's addictio); cf. glossary 'addictus' (D3-N16).
- ***Oedipus Colonaeus*** — rendered “Oedipus at Colonus”. — Sophocles, OC 1224–5 (mē phynai ton hapanta nika logon): 'not to be born is best', the very thesis at issue.
- ***prae iis ludicra*** — rendered “playthings beside them”. trifles in comparison. Comparative prae + abl.
- ***impraesentiarum*** — rendered “at present”. for the time being. Archaic adverb (in praesentia rerum).
- ***meditata*** — rendered “meditations [meditata]”. things thought out, studied positions. Neuter plural participle used as noun.
- ***melioratio / ratio entis*** — rendered “betterment / account of being [ratione entis]”. improvement; 'in respect of being'. Scholastic: goodness convertible with being (glossary D3-N11); more being = better, so existing damned soul > nothing.
- ***invidia ... Siculi tyranni*** — rendered “envy ... Sicilian Tyrants”. — Horace Ep. I.2.58–59: invidia Siculi non invenere tyranni / maius tormentum.
- ***Zelotypia*** — rendered “jealousy [Zelotypiam]”. — Greek loan (Cicero Tusc. IV.18).
- ***voli / volitur*** — rendered “be willed [voli] / is willed [volitur]”. passive of volo. Scholastic coinage (non-classical passive of velle); White's point: the punishment of spirits is their own willed affection.
- ***simpliciter / secundum quid*** — rendered “simply / in a certain respect”. absolutely / qualifiedly. As in glossary (D3-N10 p2); Aristotle's mixed actions (NE III.1).
- ***obiecta*** — rendered “set before [them] [obiecta]”. presented; objects (of the will). Pun with obiectis in Ereunius' reply: the evil lies not in the objects but in the affection.
- ***tu videris*** — rendered “you will see to it”. that is your concern. Idiomatic future perfect of deferral.
- ***abortivum ... de utero translatum ad tumulum*** — rendered “untimely birth ... carried from the womb to the tomb”. miscarriage, stillborn. Job 10:19 (de utero translatus ad tumulum) and Job 3:16, Eccl. 6:3 (abortivus).
- ***praevisio*** — rendered “foresight [praevisione]”. prevision, foreknowledge. Cf. providentia; God's acting 'constantly and from foresight'.
- ***incessere (gratiis)*** — rendered “assail [incessenda] with thanks”. attack; 'heap with'. Paradoxical: incessere normally with reproaches (maledictis).
- ***causari*** — rendered “plead [causatur]”. allege as excuse. As in glossary (D3-N16).
- ***praestructio / praestruere*** — rendered “advance-building [praestructio] / built in advance [praestruxerit]”. preparing, laying a foundation beforehand; obstructing. Used both of God's pre-arranged punishment (Andabata) and of philosophy as road-builder to theology (Ereunius).
- ***scita (Theologiae)*** — rendered “decrees [scita]”. ordinances, tenets. Legal word (plebiscitum); the established tenets of theology.
- ***ignotum per ignotius*** — rendered “the unknown through the more unknown”. — Scholastic logical fault (obscurum per obscurius).
- ***illustratrix*** — rendered “illuminatrix [illustratrix]”. that which explains, sheds light. Rare feminine agent noun.
- ***intimare*** — rendered “intimate [intimandum]”. announce, make known officially, enjoin. Late/legal Latin: to notify by authority.
- ***in utrumque disserere / Logica*** — rendered “argue to either side / Logic”. dialectic in utramque partem. Academic/dialectical method, which White contrasts with demonstrative philosophy (scientia).
- ***numen (Theologiae)*** — rendered “divine power [numine]”. nod, will, divinity. Theology's authority rightly 'bends' probabilist dialectic but not demonstrative science.
- ***Elementa Metaphysica / quinque elementis*** — rendered “Metaphysical Elements / five elements”. books, treatises (stoicheia). Self-reference: White's metaphysics in five books, later printed as Euclides metaphysicus (1658).
- ***philautia*** — rendered “self-love [philautia]”. self-regard, vanity. Greek loan (Cicero Att. XIII.13; Erasmus).
- ***sub pedibus nata*** — rendered “born under [my] feet”. came easily, sprang up underfoot. Proverbial image of things found without labour.
- ***concubia nox*** — rendered “dead of night [concubia nox]”. the hour of first sleep. Classical time-term (Livy, Cicero).
- ***statua donare*** — rendered “present with a statue”. — Ironic: public honour for a benefactor.
- ***decimum in annum servari*** — rendered “be kept to the tenth year”. withheld from publication. Horace AP 388 (nonumque prematur in annum), here with 'tenth'.
- ***rea censurae*** — rendered “liable [rea] to censure”. answerable to, subject to. Legal reus + gen.
- ***rudis*** — rendered “wooden sword [rudem]”. the foil given to a discharged gladiator. Horace Ep. I.1.2 (donatum iam rude): Ereunius asks to be released from the contest; ties to 'Andabata' (a blind gladiator).
- ***intercedere*** — rendered “interpose [intercedo]”. intervene, veto (as a tribune). Roman constitutional sense: Andabata vetoes Ereunius' discharge.
- ***iungere manus*** — rendered “join hands”. make common cause. Gesture of alliance.

## Translator's notes

- Unit: Dialogue III, Knot xvii, complete. PDF pp. 435–446 = printed pp. 424–435, from Ereunius' 'Ego vero nihil polliceor…' (p. 424) to Ereunius' question '…Quid ais esse motum?' (p. 435). The margin 'Nodus XVII. Cur plerique hominum a beatitudine excidant.' stands on p. 424 beside 'suppono nationem'; following the convention of earlier runs, the Knot starts at the speaker break. The margin 'Nodus XVIII. An mundus sit finiendus?' stands on p. 435 beside Andabata's answer 'Actum entis in potentia…'; the Knot is therefore ended at the preceding speaker break, though Ereunius' question 'Quid ais esse motum?' already opens the topic of Knot XVIII. Two inner marginal headings are placed in their rows: 'An praestet non esse quam beatitudine excludi?' (p. 429) and 'An Theologia Philosophis praescribere sententias debeat?' (p. 433), plus 'Elementa Metaphysica.' (p. 434). Offset +11 holds (running heads 424–435; signatures Hhh, Hhh ij, Hhh iij, Iii, Iii ij). At about 11 printed pages of short (c. 22-line) pages, the Knot was done in one part.
- Uncertain readings: (1) p. 430 last line '{?amaverit}' — the ink is heavy; the word could be read 'ornaverit', but 'amaverit' fits the following 'adamant' and the argument (the wretched love their own affections). (2) p. 424 'tot sint instrumenti' — kept as printed; one would expect instrumenta; rendered literally as a partitive genitive. (3) p. 427 'ea rarius quae minus frequentiora evolabunt' — elliptical; read as correlative 'ea rarius [evolabunt]; quae minus [patent], frequentiora evolabunt'. (4) p. 430 'Metu mortuos non rarum esse meminisse' — the infinitive has no governing verb; perhaps 'testantur' is understood, or a word has dropped; rendered with '[one may] remember'. (5) p. 428 'Cur … non … addixerit' — read as a rhetorical 'why should he not have consigned…' (i.e., on your principles he has); the non could alternatively be a misprint. (6) p. 427 'manseris' is split man-|seris across a damaged line; reading secure. (7) p. 430 'erudit' in 'natura nos erudit' is smudged.
- Verification pass: the Latin was re-read sentence by sentence against the page images and the English. Corrections made: 5 — (a) p. 427 'Huic mundo satis est hominum massam & caeteris omnibus perfectiorem, & eorum finem esse' first rendered '…and that their end exists'; corrected to 'more perfect than all the others and their end' (eorum = the other creatures, finem predicative); (b) p. 434 'tentavisse fere totam incipientem…' first taken with incipientem as 'me beginning'; corrected: the feminine accusative agrees with totam [partem Metaphysicam]; (c) p. 435 'scripta non tantae rea censurae' first rendered 'not liable to so great a censure as…' with the negative misplaced; adjusted so non goes with tantae; (d) p. 426 'propiores beneficiis omni reliqua … parte' — omni … parte confirmed as ablative of comparison, not 'in every part'; (e) p. 431 'si non velint iis cedere' first rendered 'yield to them'; changed to 'yield them up' (cedere + dat. = give way, but here = give up the punishments) with the Latin bracketed. Checked: negatives non pati, non attingit, non invenisse, non … amaverit, nolint, non simpliciter, neque scire neque docere, nondum; comparatives deterius, maiori, cultiorem, propiores, rarius, frequentiora, altior, ignotius, suaviorem; subjunctives praestiterit, destituat, exerceatur, permittat, adhibuerit, crediderim, obstiterim, credamus, videris (fut. perf.).
- Conventions: as in the glossary — long s, ligatures and accents normalised; consonantal u → v; speaker abbreviations kept (Ereu., Asphal., Andab.); supplied words in [square brackets]; Latin of technical or ambiguous words in [brackets] after the English; marginal notes as [Margin: …] in their place. Long speeches are divided into several rows at sentence breaks, so each row's English matches that row's Latin.
