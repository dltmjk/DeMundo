# De Mundo — Dialogue II (De forma mundi, seu de motu magnorum corporum), Knot iii, part 2

*Latin title:* Nodus III. Mare a litoribus et ad littora moveri  
*English title:* Knot III. That the sea is moved from the shores and to the shores  
*Pages:* PDF pp. 164–172 = printed pp. 153–161 (from ‘Ereu. Non factum nego’ on p. 153 to the end of Asphalius’ speech ‘…humani ingenii explicare.’ on p. 161, where Knot iv begins).

## Dialogue II, Knot iii, part 2 (printed pp. 153–161)

| Latin | English |
|---|---|
| [Margin: Cur in Oceano appareat aqua in Orientem redire.] Ereu. Non factum nego, sed qui fiat declaro: & hoc aio manifestum, minus resistere aquam propter littus quam in medio alveo. Vide remiges nostros in Thamesi: sive surgat mare sive resideat, in principio (si adversus aestum viam faciant) littus urgentes. Hic primos accessus & decessus sentiunt, hic levior est contra torrentem labor. Inundet itaque mare, constat ad littora magis versus Occidentem sentiri motum; deponat aquas, quoniam hoc in medio alveo incipiet, quantum sese littoribus occurrentibus attollent aquae, [p. 154] tantum refluere apparebunt in Orientem, quae prius margines soli radebant. Contigit mihi aliquando molam a rivo non magno agitatam notare qui infra molam ripas leviter declives lavabat, & oculos testes iuro, ipsum torrentem a mola descendentem aquas littoreas molam versus manifestarie pepulisse, ubi iunctae torrenti novis supervenientibus cedebant, ut imaginem refluxus in brevi depictam aspicerem. | [Margin: Why in the Ocean the water appears to return into the East.] Ereu. I do not deny the fact, but I declare how [qui] it comes about: and this I say [is] manifest, that the water resists less near the shore than in the middle of the channel [alveo]. See our rowers on the Thames: whether the sea rise or settle, at the beginning (if they make [their] way against the tide) [they are] pressing the shore [littus urgentes]. Here they feel the first comings and goings [accessus & decessus], here the labour against the torrent is lighter. Let the sea therefore flood: it is agreed [constat] that at the shores the motion is felt more towards the West; let it lay down [its] waters: since this will begin in the middle of the channel, as much as the waters raise themselves, the shores running against [them] [littoribus occurrentibus], [p. 154] so much will they appear to flow back into the East, [the waters] which before were grazing the margins of the ground [margines soli]. It befell me once to note a mill driven [agitatam] by a not great stream, which below the mill washed gently sloping banks; and I swear [by my] eyes [as] witnesses, that the torrent itself, descending from the mill, manifestly drove the shore-waters [aquas littoreas] towards the mill, where, joined to the torrent, they gave way to new [waters] coming on above, so that I beheld an image of the reflux painted in brief. |
| [Margin: Error cuiusdam circa sequelam aestus ad lunam.] Andab. Nihil inter haec de luna, Ereuni, cuius tamen hoc opus esse testem omni exceptione maiorem affero, quem tu pro Cometarum materia tanto cum sonitu adduxisti, ut iterum negare non possis; Et ait idem omnes notare a calvo ad calvum, remque in Oceano Flandrico esse compertissimam. Attollente se supra horizontem lunari sydere, mare paulatim intumescere, donec pervenerit ad meridianum coeli fastigium, tunc vero toto ad horizontem usque occiduum descensu residere: Ubi statim iterum inflari incipit. | [Margin: The error of a certain [man] about the following [sequelam] of the tide upon the moon.] Andab. Nothing among these [things] about the moon, Ereunius, whose work [opus] nevertheless this is — [for which] I bring a witness greater than every exception [omni exceptione maiorem], whom you brought in with so much noise for the matter of the Comets, so that you cannot deny [him] a second time. And the same [man] says that all note [it] from bald [man] to bald [man] [a calvo ad calvum], and that the thing is most thoroughly ascertained [compertissimam] in the Flemish Ocean: [that,] the lunar star raising itself above the horizon, the sea little by little swells, until it has arrived at the meridian summit of the heaven, but then in the whole descent as far as the western horizon settles: where immediately it begins again to be inflated. |
| Ereu. Authorem affers & diligentem, & pro illo scribendi genere, quo in utramque partem Dialectice disputatur, non sordidum: & cui ego oculato testi credam: sed cum a calvis testimonium emendicat, hoc cupiam examinare num calvis illis memoria cum pilis non defluxerit. Quale enim est, quod vult [p. 155] tollente se supra horizontem luna, fluxum incipere, cum in aliquibus plagis per plures dies vel continue vel non omnino videatur? Cum in ipsa Flandria aliquando octodecim circiter horas supra horizontem sit, ut impossibile sit flumen, si cum oriente luna inceperit, non tres saltem horas ante meridianam potentiam defluere coepisse. Quid caeteris horizontibus fiet? Num cum meridiana est Lovanii luna horizon cui incipit allucescere, & a quo tum primo recedit, contrarios maris motus sentient? incipiente utrobique aestu dum ad unum terminum accedit luna, ab altero recedit. Necessum quoque erit lunam secum aestum circumferre & ingeniosa illa Authoris de Antiselene disputatio in fumos abiverit. | Ereu. You bring an author both diligent and, for that kind of writing in which one disputes Dialectically on either side [in utramque partem], not mean [sordidum]: and one whom I would believe as an eye-witness [oculato testi]: but since he begs testimony from bald [men], this I should wish to examine, whether from those bald [men] memory has not flowed off together with [their] hairs. For of what sort is [it], what he will have [vult], [p. 155] [that,] the moon raising itself above the horizon, the flux [fluxum] begins, when in some regions [plagis] through several days it [the moon] is seen either continually or not at all? Since in Flanders itself it is sometimes about eighteen hours above the horizon, so that it is impossible that the flood [flumen], if it began with the moon rising, did not begin to flow down at least three hours before the meridian power [potentiam]. What will happen at the other horizons? Will, when the moon is meridian at Louvain, the horizon to which it begins to shine, and [that] from which it then first withdraws, feel contrary motions of the sea? the tide beginning in both places while the moon approaches the one term [terminum], [and] withdraws from the other. It will be necessary [necessum] also that the moon carry the tide around with itself, and that ingenious disputation of the Author concerning the Antiselene will have gone off into smoke. |
| Asphal. Mirum, unde viro ingenioso & diligenti tam obesus error potuit obrepere! | Asphal. [A] wonder, whence upon a man ingenious and diligent so gross [obesus] an error could creep! |
| [Margin: Error cuiusdam de linea qua gravia descendunt.] Ereu. Nihil mirum, Asphali, si quam levibus momentis memoria & attentio nostra agitetur, consideremus. Duo tibi exempla propono, alterum viri & Geometriae peritissimi & scriptoris exactissimi, qui describens lineam qua gravia descendunt, supposito telluris motu, ait probabiliter esse circularem; & hoc schemate subiuncto exponit, at vide quantam probabilitatem habeat. [Margin: Quaere in Tabula figuram tertiam.] Cadat a [p. 156] puncto C, eodem momento aliud grave motu recto paulo celeriori, ita ut ad lineam B, I, pertingat cum punctum C, pervenit ad lineam A, L, & vide num portionem circuli descripserit suo casu? | [Margin: The error of a certain [man] about the line by which heavy [things] descend.] Ereu. Nothing wonderful, Asphalius, if we consider by how light moments [momentis] our memory and attention are driven [agitetur]. Two examples I set before you, the one of a man both most skilled in Geometry and a most exact writer, who, describing the line by which heavy [things] descend, the motion of the earth [telluris] being supposed, says it is probably circular; and he expounds [it] by this diagram [schemate] subjoined; but see how much probability it has. [Margin: Look for the third figure in the Table.] Let [a heavy body] fall from [p. 156] the point C, [and] at the same moment another heavy [body] with a straight motion a little quicker, so that it reaches the line B, I when the point C arrives at the line A, L; and see whether it has described a portion of a circle by its fall? |
| Andab. Quid enim obstat? | Andab. For what stands in the way? |
| Ereu. Non vides ab uno centro & spatio eodem non nisi unum circulum duci? Cum itaque cursus omnium E, sit centrum, spatium vero E, C, facile vides unum tantum circulo decurrere posse. Deinde vero colligit ex hoc Theoremate aliud quod mihi videtur debuisse monere, hoc prius fuisse impossibile; arguit enim grave cadens, conversa terra casu suo nihil amplius moveri, quam si quievisset tellus: Cum enim duas ipsemet motus causas ponat, cum una sola fuisset tellure quiescente, non aperte ponitur causa actu agens sine effectu? Utraque enim causa velocitatem affert & tamen non est velocitas nisi quantam una causa facit. [Margin: Error Opticorum de loco imaginis.] Alterum exemplum est optices principum plerorumque, Alhazenis, Vitellionis, perspectivae communis & Magini, qui omnes affirmant in speculo concavo posse accidere ut locus imaginis sit retro oculum, quod quomodo in cogitationem habentis oculos potuerit incidere, prorsus non capio. | Ereu. Do you not see that from one centre and the same space [spatio] not [any] but one circle is drawn? Since therefore of the courses of all E is the centre, but the space E, C, you easily see that only one can run down by a circle. But then he gathers from this Theorem another, which, it seems to me, ought to have warned [him] that this former [one] was impossible; for he argues that a heavy [body] falling, the earth [terra] being turned, is moved by its fall nothing more than if the earth [tellus] had rested: for since he himself posits two causes of the motion, whereas there would have been one only with the earth [tellure] resting, is not a cause acting in act [actu] without effect openly posited? For each cause brings velocity, and yet there is not velocity except as much as one cause makes. [Margin: The error of the Opticians about the place of the image.] The other example is of most of the princes of optics, Alhazen, Vitellio, the common perspective [perspectivae communis] and Magini, who all affirm that in a concave mirror it can happen that the place of the image is behind the eye; which, how it could fall into the thought of one having eyes, I altogether do not grasp. |
| Asphal. Obstupesco prorsus, Ereuni, haec in tantos viros cadere potuisse. | Asphal. I am altogether astounded, Ereunius, that these [things] could fall upon such great men. |
| [p. 157] Ereu. Immerito, Asphali, neque enim errores hi sunt, etiamsi errata vocari mereantur. Sed ut memini accidisse aliquando nostro Andabatae, ut alium sibi assidentem oblitus de eo loqui, non coram dicenda, consessu ridente inciperet; & mihi simile quid pluries intervenit, & lanio quaerenti cultrum qui in ore sibi erat: sic haec per inadvertentiam non per ignorantiam magnis viris eveniunt. Et quot qualesque errores in hodierno vel hesterno colloquio intercidisse poterunt, quos non ego solus qui loquor; sed vos etiam dum ad alia intenti estis, non advertitis? Recte profatus est qui dixit, ubi plura nitent in carmine, non ego paucis offendar maculis: & per calumniam agunt qui magnorum virorum authoritatem ex aliquo uno errore elevare contendunt. | [p. 157] Ereu. Undeservedly, Asphalius; for neither are these errors [errores], even if they deserve to be called slips [errata]. But as I remember it happened once to our Andabata, that, forgetting another [man] sitting beside him, he began to speak about him [things] not to be said in [his] presence, the assembly [consessu] laughing; and something similar has befallen me several times, and [as] to the butcher seeking the knife which was in his [own] mouth: so these [things] happen to great men through inadvertence, not through ignorance. And how many and of what kind [of] errors will have been able to slip in [intercidisse] in today's or yesterday's colloquy, which not I alone who speak, but you also, while you are intent on other [things], do not notice? Rightly spoke [profatus est] he who said: where more [things] shine in a poem, I shall not be offended by a few spots: and they act by cavil [per calumniam] who strive to lighten [elevare] the authority of great men out of some one error. |
| Asphal. Hoc itaque si placet examinemus, Ereuni, an accidentia aestus tuae speculationi testimonium dicant. | Asphal. Let us therefore, if it pleases, examine this, Ereunius: whether the accidents [accidentia] of the tide speak testimony for your speculation. |
| [Margin: Cur circa aequinoctia aestus sunt maiores.] Ereu. Quoniam sic vultis & iubetis, hoc primum quaero, cur statis temporibus maiores sunt aestus? Dic itaque, Andabata, postquam sol est causa aestus, quando putas maiores aestus accidere debere? | [Margin: Why about the equinoxes the tides are greater.] Ereu. Since thus you will and order [vultis & iubetis], this I ask first: why at fixed [statis] times the tides are greater? Say therefore, Andabata, since [postquam] the sun is the cause of the tide, when do you think greater tides ought to happen? |
| Andab. In singulis plagis secundum quod sol magis imminet. | Andab. In each region [plagis] according as the sun hangs more over [it] [imminet]. |
| Ereu. Ergo oblitus es ex conversione aquae [p. 158] in occasum per accidens generari aestum? Vide igitur aestum esse fortiorem cum aquae fortius in Occidentem evolvuntur: hoc autem esse cum auctior est ventus: hoc rursus cum sol magis aequatori insidet: tum enim & maior est circulus terrae quam ambit & indifferentius in aquas agit. | Ereu. Then you have forgotten that from the turning [conversione] of the water [p. 158] towards the setting the tide is generated by accident [per accidens]? See therefore that the tide is stronger when the waters are more strongly rolled out [evolvuntur] into the West: and that this is when the wind is increased: this again when the sun sits more upon the equator: for then both the circle of the earth which it goes around is greater, and it acts more indifferently [indifferentius] upon the waters. |
| Andab. Hoc dicis in Martio & Septembri prope aequinoctia vehementissimos esse aestus. Neque in hoc male; Nautae enim non fatentur modo sed expectant & experiuntur. Sed hoc te monere habeo, tanto fortiores etiam tunc esse aestus, cum lunae coniunctio vel oppositio intervenerit, ut eam non contemnas. | Andab. This you say: that in March and September, near the equinoxes, the tides are most vehement. Nor [do you say] badly in this; for sailors not only confess [it] but expect and experience [it]. But this I have to warn you: that the tides are then also so much stronger when a conjunction or opposition of the moon has intervened, that you should not despise her. |
| Ereu. Ut ego eam despiciam a qua tantum nobis commodi affluere videam? Non fiet. Sed hoc potius aspiciamus quid ea conferat huic vento, quem immediatam pulsus aquae causam esse exposuimus. | Ereu. That I should look down on [despiciam] her, from whom I see so much of advantage flowing to us? It shall not be. But let us rather look at [aspiciamus] this: what she contributes to this wind, which we have set out to be the immediate cause of the driving [pulsus] of the water. |
| [Margin: Cur luna censeatur humida.] Andab. Non legi ventosam esse lunam, etsi ventorum aliquando index esse possit: sed aquosam esse nihil incredibile crediderim. Nam & Hippocrates plenilunia humidiora dixit & plurimae observationes hortulanorum Sylvicidarum & Medicorum nescio quid simile insinuant. Unde esse lunarem quandam influentiam quae dominatrix aquarum sit, fere certus sum, unde aquas trahere possit & aestus elevare. | [Margin: Why the moon is reckoned moist.] Andab. I have not read that the moon is windy, although it can sometimes be an index of winds: but that it is watery I should believe [crediderim] [to be] nothing incredible. For both Hippocrates said full moons [plenilunia] [are] more humid, and very many observations of gardeners, Wood-cutters [Sylvicidarum] and Physicians insinuate something similar. Whence I am almost certain that there is a certain lunar influence [influentiam] which is mistress [dominatrix] of the waters, whence it can draw waters and raise tides. |
| [p. 159] Ereu. Influentias tuas, Andabata, cum constiterint, libenter acceptabo. Interea quod constat, est lunam nobis lumen affundere, & in plenilunio maximum, lumen autem ignem seu igneae naturae esse; &, quod sequitur, pro suo modulo vapores & ventos excitare qui licet cum luna sola est, insensibiles sint, tamen cum alteri causae potentiori copulata est, insignem possint habere effectum. Multos enim quos sol elicuit, sed decedens sustinere non potuit luna quae elicere non potuisset, sustentare potis erit. Et sic vides quomodo luna cum satura est, concitatiorem aquam reddat, sine occultae alicuius qualitatis adminiculo. Et credo dictum Hippocratis & caetera experimenta quae in tanto proborum & suarum artium peritorum consensu non possunt nulla existimari, huc pertinere, ut lunae lumen & calor, cum modicus sit, vapores a sole collectos elevare non possit, sed neque a nocturno frigore congelari & arescere permittat; ex quo fit ut humiditate redundent eae noctes, & magis plantas irrigent, & corpora etiam humana, eosque effectus habeant quos lunae in similibus attribuere solemus. | [p. 159] Ereu. Your influences, Andabata, when they have been established [constiterint], I shall gladly accept. Meanwhile, what is agreed [constat] is that the moon pours light upon us, and at full moon [plenilunio] the greatest, and that light is fire or of a fiery nature; and, what follows, [that] according to its little measure [modulo] it stirs up vapours and winds, which, although when the moon is alone they are insensible, yet when it is coupled to another more powerful cause, can have a notable effect. For many [vapours] which the sun drew out, but departing could not sustain, the moon, which could not have drawn [them] out, will be able [potis erit] to sustain. And thus you see how the moon, when she is sated [satura], renders the water more stirred [concitatiorem], without the prop [adminiculo] of any occult quality. And I believe that the saying of Hippocrates, and the other experiments [experimenta] which, in so great a consensus of upright men and of [men] skilled in their arts, cannot be reckoned null, pertain here: that the light and heat of the moon, since it is moderate, cannot raise the vapours collected by the sun, but neither permits [them] to be congealed and to dry up from the nocturnal cold; from which it comes about that those nights overflow with humidity, and water plants more, and human bodies also, and have those effects which we are accustomed to attribute to the moon in similar [cases]. |
| [Margin: Cur circa plenilunia & interlunia fortiores sunt aestus.] Sed quid de luna sitiente, cuius oppositos esse effectus convenit, dicemus; cum eosdem videamus? Primo non esse pares siticulosae lunae aestus & satiatae, nisi fallant me nautae, [p. 160] quibus inter parvas differentias fidere periculosum est. Aliud est lunare lumen fortius in coitione esse, cum tota sui orbis diametro soli propior sit. Unde licet minus sit, non desinit notabilem effectum habere. Praeterea circa hoc tempus illustrior est a terra reflexio. Tandem quarta fere luna & interdum quinta vehementissimum esse aestum, adeo ut nautae licet circa novilunium, fiat, haereant tamen quando maximus sit. De utroque autem huius syderis statu notandum, lunam tum temporis eundem parallelum vel certe vicinum aliquem solari incedere, quoties hunc motum in mari ciet, ut videas operas lunae soli accessarias esse, neque per sese efficaces. | [Margin: Why about full moons and inter-lunes [interlunia] the tides are stronger.] But what shall we say about the thirsty [sitiente] moon, whose effects it is fitting [convenit] should be opposite, when we see [them] the same? First, that the tides of the thirsty [siticulosae] moon and of the sated [satiatae] are not equal, unless the sailors deceive me, [p. 160] whom it is dangerous to trust among small differences. Another [point] is that the lunar light is stronger in the coition [coitione], since it is nearer to the sun by the whole diameter of its orb. Whence, although it be less, it does not cease to have a notable effect. Besides, about this time the reflexion from the earth is brighter. Finally, that about the fourth moon [day], and sometimes the fifth, the tide is most vehement, so much so that the sailors, although it happens about the new moon [novilunium], are yet at a loss [haereant] when it is greatest. But concerning each state of this star it is to be noted that the moon at that time proceeds along the same parallel as the solar, or certainly some neighbouring [one], as often as it stirs this motion in the sea; so that you may see that the works [operas] of the moon are accessory [accessarias] to the sun, and not efficacious through themselves. |
| [Margin: Cur aestus singulis diebus una hora veniat tardior.] Andab. Ne dixis, Ereuni, manifestissimus enim est effectus lunae in horario illo detrimento, quod in mari cottidie experimur, tanta cum lunae motu consonantia, ut clarissime se inde natum profiteatur. | [Margin: Why the tide comes each day one hour later.] Andab. Do not say [so] [Ne dixis], Ereunius; for most manifest is the effect of the moon in that hourly loss [detrimento] which we experience daily in the sea, with so great a consonance with the motion of the moon, that it most clearly professes itself born from there. |
| Ereu. Verum hoc imprimis unde constat, magis e lunae defectu maris defectum, quam e contra nasci, vel certe utrumque ab una eademque causa proficisci, vel, si non hoc, a paribus & coniunctis? Denique hoc tecum pensita, deficere singulis diebus Oceanum non aliunde posse nasci, quam ex hoc quod motus solis celerior est motu aquae: in quo quid [p. 161] novi ut quod ab alio accipit motum, tardius eo moveatur a quo motum accipit? quod cum in certa proportione contingat, certum defectum quotidie efficit: Et hoc vides cum ratione contingere. At hic effectus ab luna si foret, a sitiente vel satura luna, a recte vel ad latus spectata, differentia aliqua proderetur, quae cum nulla appareat, permitte me ad aquae morosius obedientis indolem potius quam in lunae negotiationem non tam effectum quam defectum referre. | Ereu. But this first of all: whence is it agreed [constat] that the defect [defectum] of the sea is born from the defect of the moon rather than the contrary, or certainly that both proceed from one and the same cause, or, if not this, from equal and conjoined [causes]? Finally weigh this with yourself: that the Ocean's falling short [deficere] each day can be born from nowhere else than from this, that the motion of the sun is quicker than the motion of the water: in which what [is there] [p. 161] new, that what receives motion from another is moved more slowly than that from which it receives the motion? which, since it happens in a certain proportion, effects a certain defect daily: and this you see happens with reason [cum ratione]. But if this effect were from the moon, from the thirsty or the sated moon, from [the moon] looked at straight or to the side, some difference would be betrayed [proderetur]; which, since none appears, permit me to refer not so much the effect as the defect to the disposition [indolem] of the water obeying rather sluggishly [morosius], rather than to the dealing [negotiationem] of the moon. |
| Asphal. Fateor symptomata haec non male tuae oeconomiae congruere, si non alia impediant, quorum rationem qui reddere potis sis haereo. Certe tuus Galilaeus desperavit nisi mota terra rationem horum reddi posse. Sunt autem quod mare in medio currere, in extremis elevari percipiatur; quorum figuram in vase aqua pleno, & agitato pulcherrime depingit, at aliter quomodo possint contingere, non putat esse humani ingenii explicare. | Asphal. I confess that these symptoms [symptomata] agree not badly with your economy [oeconomiae], if other [things] do not hinder, of which how [qui] you may be able to render the reason [rationem] I am at a loss [haereo]. Certainly your Galileo despaired that the reason of these could be rendered except with the earth moved. And they are: that the sea is perceived to run in the middle, [and] to be raised at the extremes; whose figure he most beautifully depicts in a vessel full of water and agitated; but how they could happen otherwise he does not think it is [a matter] of human wit [ingenii] to explain. |

## Words and phrases with alternative or special meanings

- ***Mare a litoribus et ad littora moveri*** — rendered “That the sea is moved from the shores and to the shores”. Title of Knot iii (see part 1). Part 2 completes White's case: the apparent eastward ebb is a shore effect; lunar correlations are accessory to the sun.
- ***accessus & decessus*** — rendered “comings and goings”. flow and ebb; approach and departure Standard pair for tidal flood and ebb; also used of the moon's approach and withdrawal from a horizon.
- ***margines soli*** — rendered “the margins of the ground [soli]”. ‘soli’ could be read as ‘alone’, but ‘quae’ refers to the (feminine) waters, so ‘soli’ is taken as genitive of solum.
- ***littus urgere*** — rendered “press the shore”. hug the shore Rowers keeping close inshore where the counter-current is weaker.
- ***oculos testes iuro*** — rendered “I swear [by my] eyes [as] witnesses”. I call my eyes to witness
- ***testis omni exceptione maior*** — rendered “a witness greater than every exception”. a witness beyond objection Roman-law formula (exceptio = a plea against a witness). The witness is the author whom Ereunius cited ‘for the matter of the Comets’ — evidently the Louvain professor Libertus Fromondus (cf. D1-N09), whose Meteorologica treats the tides.
- ***a calvo ad calvum*** — rendered “from bald [man] to bald [man]”. uncertain idiom: perhaps ‘from one old man to another’, i.e. by long tradition Ereunius takes it up as calvi = bald (old) men whose memory may have ‘flowed off with their hairs’.
- ***sequela (aestus ad lunam)*** — rendered “following”. consequence; correspondence The tide's supposed following of the moon; cf. glossary sequela = consequence.
- ***in utramque partem Dialectice disputare*** — rendered “dispute Dialectically on either side”. argue pro and contra Characterises the author's genre as probable (dialectical), not demonstrative; cf. glossary ‘Logice’.
- ***oculatus testis*** — rendered “eye-witness”.  Plautine/legal phrase.
- ***meridiana potentia*** — rendered “meridian power”. power (influence) at the meridian; possibly a misprint for ‘positionem’ Kept as printed: the moon's strongest position at culmination.
- ***Antiselene*** — rendered “Antiselene”. the point of the heaven opposite the moon; or a mock-moon (paraselene) Probably the author's account of the second daily tide by the influence of the moon's opposite point below the horizon; uncertain.
- ***in fumos abire*** — rendered “go off into smoke”. come to nothing ‘abiverit’ = abierit.
- ***obesus (error)*** — rendered “gross”. fat, dull
- ***momentum*** — rendered “moment”. weight, impulse, trifle ‘quam levibus momentis’ — by how light impulses; glossary momentum = moment.
- ***vir Geometriae peritissimus*** — rendered “a man most skilled in Geometry”.  Galileo, Dialogo (Day 2), who argued that a body falling from a tower on the rotating earth probably describes a semicircle and that its true motion is no greater than if the earth rested.
- ***schema*** — rendered “diagram”. figure, scheme Referred to the third figure of the book's Tabula figurarum (see FM2).
- ***causa actu agens sine effectu*** — rendered “a cause acting in act without effect”.  Scholastic axiom that a cause in act must produce its effect; turned against Galileo's two causes of motion.
- ***optices principes; perspectiva communis*** — rendered “princes of optics; the common perspective”.  Alhazen, Witelo (Vitellio), John Pecham's Perspectiva communis, and G. A. Magini (Breve instruttione sopra l'apparenze… dello specchio concavo, 1611), on the image ‘behind the eye’ in a concave mirror.
- ***errores / errata*** — rendered “errors / slips”.  White distinguishes real errors (of ignorance) from inadvertent slips.
- ***lanius quaerens cultrum qui in ore sibi erat*** — rendered “the butcher seeking the knife which was in his mouth”.  Proverb of looking for what one already holds.
- ***ubi plura nitent in carmine, non ego paucis offendar maculis*** — rendered “where more [things] shine in a poem, I shall not be offended by a few spots”.  Horace, Ars poetica 351–2.
- ***calumnia*** — rendered “cavil”. false accusation Glossary rendering.
- ***elevare (authoritatem)*** — rendered “lighten”. disparage, make light of Plays against elevare = raise (tides) elsewhere in this part.
- ***accidentia (aestus)*** — rendered “accidents”. attendant phenomena, properties Aristotelian accidents; here the observed features of the tide.
- ***sic vultis & iubetis*** — rendered “thus you will and order”.  Echo of the Roman legislative formula ‘velitis iubeatis’.
- ***stata tempora*** — rendered “fixed times”. appointed seasons
- ***postquam (causal)*** — rendered “since [postquam]”. after Late/neo-Latin causal use.
- ***per accidens*** — rendered “by accident”. incidentally, not essentially Scholastic: the tide is a per accidens effect of the westward turning of the waters.
- ***indifferentius*** — rendered “more indifferently”. more evenly, without preference The equinoctial sun acts evenly on both hemispheres.
- ***despicere / aspicere*** — rendered “look down on / look at”. despise / consider Wordplay in Ereunius' reply.
- ***pulsus (aquae)*** — rendered “driving”. impulse, beat
- ***Sylvicidae; hortulani*** — rendered “Wood-cutters; gardeners”.  Lore that timber and crops depend on the moon's phase.
- ***influentia*** — rendered “influence”.  Astrological occult influence; Ereunius accepts it only ‘when established’.
- ***dominatrix aquarum*** — rendered “mistress of the waters”.  The moon as ruler of moisture (cf. Pliny, NH II.221–3).
- ***modulus*** — rendered “little measure”. proportion, scale
- ***luna satura / satiata; luna sitiens / siticulosa*** — rendered “the sated moon; the thirsty moon”. full moon; new moon White's own paired metaphors (cf. Pliny's ‘sitiens luna’).
- ***adminiculum*** — rendered “prop”. support, aid
- ***qualitas occulta*** — rendered “occult quality”.  Scholastic hidden quality, rejected as explanation.
- ***experimentum*** — rendered “experiment”. experience, observation Glossary rendering.
- ***plenilunium / interlunium / novilunium / coitio*** — rendered “full moon / inter-lune / new moon / coition”.  Coitio = conjunction of sun and moon.
- ***operae (lunae) accessariae*** — rendered “works … accessory”. auxiliary operations The moon's role is accessory to the sun's; cf. White's whole tidal theory.
- ***detrimentum horarium*** — rendered “hourly loss”. daily retardation of the tide by about an hour
- ***defectus / deficere*** — rendered “defect / fall short”. lag, retardation; (of the moon) also eclipse Here the daily lag of the moon's and of the sea's times, not eclipse.
- ***Ne dixis*** — rendered “Do not say [so]”.  Archaic perfect subjunctive = ne dixeris.
- ***negotiatio (lunae)*** — rendered “dealing”. business, agency
- ***morosius obediens*** — rendered “obeying rather sluggishly”. peevishly, slowly Inertia of water lagging behind its mover.
- ***indoles*** — rendered “disposition [indolem]”.  Glossary rendering.
- ***symptomata; oeconomia*** — rendered “symptoms; economy”. phenomena; system, arrangement Medical and rhetorical terms applied to the tidal theory.
- ***Galilaeus … in vase aqua pleno*** — rendered “Galileo … in a vessel full of water”.  Dialogo, Day 4: water in a moving basin runs in the middle and rises at the ends.
