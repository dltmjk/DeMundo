# De Mundo — Dialogue II (De forma mundi, seu de motu magnorum corporum), Knot viii, part 2

*Latin title:* Nodus VIII. Aerem usque ad confinia lunae a terra circumferri  
*English title:* Knot VIII. That the air as far as the borders of the moon is carried round by the earth [terra]  
*Pages:* PDF pp. 219–223 = printed pp. 208–212 (from ‘Ereu. Andabatae quatenus possumus’ on p. 208, beside the marginal ‘Phaenomena motum aëris testantia’, to Asphalius' ‘…postmodum quiescente terra.’ on p. 212; Knot ix begins on p. 212).

## Dialogue II, Knot viii, part 2 (printed pp. 208–212)

| Latin | English |
|---|---|
| [p. 208] Ereu. [Margin: Phaenomena motum aëris testantia.] Andabatae quatenus possumus, gerendus est mos. Et quoniam ipsa rerum facies & montium {?longi} toto terrarum orbe nexus & cottidiana documenta hoc persuadent, aërem qui intra montes continetur, cum tellure versari, primum testimonium ab Ixione mutuor. Vide proinde, Andabata, has nubes quas ita altas conspicis ut montes qui eos superant tanto intervallo dissepiantur, quantum est mare pacificum: pauci enim sunt toto orbe qui altiores cacuminibus suis vincant. Cogita itaque has omnes, non consistentes vel hac illacve versatas pro ventorum libitu apparituras esse, si proximus montibus aër telluris agitationem non sequeretur, sed concitatissimo & constantissimo adversum terrae in occasum contendentes. Ab his erige te ulterius ad leves illas nebulas crepusculinas, quas quinquagesimo a superficie terrae milliari [p. 209] collocant Meteorologi, cum altissimae sunt; & vide etiam in iis tantumdem contingere debere. Galilaeus & caeteri post eum Astronomi vapores lunae montibus sublimiores & extrema eorum fastigia coronantes, cum luna vectas & immania spatia peragrantes ipsis oculis conspiciunt & sine stupore confitentur. Tandem caudam Fromondianam proxime lunam collocatam non contradicendum ipsius experimentum circulo cum tellure vectam convincit. A sole enim discedere & ad eundem redire necessario confitendum est. Vel itaque corpus hoc nebulosum suo proprio actu immensum spatium 24. horis confecisse asserendum est, quod aeque creditu arduum concipies, aut toto simul aëre gestatum, atque proinde telluris comitem eo usque esse aërem. | [p. 208] Ereu. [Margin: Phenomena [Phaenomena] testifying to the motion of the air.] To Andabata, as far as we can, compliance must be borne [gerendus est mos]. And since the very face of things, and the {?long} [longi] linkage [nexus] of the mountains in the whole round of the lands [toto terrarum orbe], and daily proofs [documenta] persuade this, that the air which is contained within the mountains is turned [versari] with the earth [tellure], I borrow the first testimony from Ixion. See accordingly, Andabata, these clouds, which you behold so high that the mountains which overtop them [eos] are fenced apart [dissepiantur] by as great an interval as is the Pacific sea: for there are few in the whole world [orbe] which, [being] higher, overcome [them] with their summits. Think therefore that all these would appear [apparituras esse] not standing still, or turned [versatas] this way or that at the pleasure of the winds, if the air nearest to the mountains did not follow the agitation of the earth [telluris], but hastening [contendentes] towards the setting [in occasum] with a most impetuous [concitatissimo] and most constant [course] against [adversum] the earth [terrae]. From these raise yourself further to those light twilight clouds [nebulas crepusculinas] which the Meteorologists place at the fiftieth mile from the surface of the earth [terrae] [p. 209], when they are highest; and see that in them too just as much [tantumdem] must happen. Galileo and the other Astronomers after him behold with their very eyes vapours higher than the mountains of the moon and crowning their extreme tops, carried with the moon and travelling over huge spaces, and confess [it] without amazement [stupore]. Finally, the Fromondian tail, placed very near the moon, his own experiment [experimentum], not to be contradicted, proves [convincit] [to have been] carried in a circle with the earth [tellure]. For that it departs from the sun and returns to the same must necessarily be confessed. Either therefore it must be asserted that this cloudy body by its own proper act accomplished an immense space in 24 hours, which you will conceive [to be] equally hard of belief [creditu arduum], or [that it was] carried by the whole air together, and accordingly that the air is the companion of the earth [telluris] as far as that. |
| Asphal. Cometam, si altior fuerit cauda, ad lunam pertinuisse fortasse dixeris, at caudam quomodo tantum extuleris nescio: cum intra telluris umbram constitisse certum sit, atque proinde crepusculinis non incommode vaporibus annumerari mage digna sit. | Asphal. The comet, if it was higher than the tail [cauda], you may perhaps say reached to the moon; but how you have raised [extuleris] the tail so much I do not know: since it is certain [that it] stood within the shadow of the earth [telluris], and accordingly it is rather [mage] worthy to be numbered, not unfittingly, among the twilight vapours [crepusculinis … vaporibus]. |
| Ereu. [Margin: Caudam Cometae anni 1618. prope lunam fuisse.] Cogita tecum solem medio Decembri Lovanii septem circiter horarum esse, solem Perigaeum, Phaenomenon apparuisse sole ad ortum incipiente repedare. Lunam [p. 210] ipsam umbra terrestri a sole Perigaeo defendi: & vide quantum lunari altitudini decessisse putandi sint hi cometici pili. Si exactior fuisset narratio, coniecturae quoque certiori locus fuisset. Nunc altitudinem ingentem assignandum est: quantam? coniiciendum magis quam asserendum est. | Ereu. [Margin: That the tail of the Comet of the year 1618 was near the moon.] Consider with yourself that the sun in mid-December at Louvain is of about seven hours; that the sun is Perigee [Perigaeum]; that the Phenomenon appeared when the sun was beginning to step back [repedare] towards its rising. [Consider] that the moon [p. 210] itself is defended by the earthly [terrestri] shadow from the Perigee sun: and see how much these cometic hairs [pili] are to be thought to have fallen short [decessisse] of the lunar height. If the narration had been more exact, there would also have been place for a more certain conjecture. Now a huge height must be assigned [assignandum est]: how great? it is to be conjectured more than asserted. |
| Andab. Quid etiam supra lunam, at hoc etiam tuis dictis adversum est. | Andab. What [if it was] even above the moon? But this too is adverse to your sayings [dictis]. |
| Ereu. Ego, Cometa sub luna, an supra fuerit non litigo; sed quando altior luna fuerit dictis meis nihil officerit, quod meminerim. Si enim usque lunae confinia motum prius protraxerim, hoc non facit ut ulterius non possit produci. | Ereu. I do not dispute whether the Comet was below the moon or above; but when it was higher than the moon, it will have hindered [officerit] my sayings in nothing, that I remember [quod meminerim]. For if I first shall have drawn out [protraxerim] the motion as far as the borders of the moon, this does not make [it] that it cannot be drawn forth [produci] further. |
| Andab. Poene me fidei tuae addixim, si scirem quomodo tam ingens impetus, quem a tellure corpori huic imprimi necessum est, in tam fragili natura subsistere valeret. Fac enim tellurem aliquo casu subsistere, non aër immani impetu omnia terrae haerentia, arbores, castra, montes ipsos everreret? habet itaque eundem impetum etiamnum, etsi non spectabilem, & quomodo tam vehemens impetus sine totius aëris concussione & quasi pulverisatione permanere valeat prorsus ignoro. | Andab. Almost I would have bound [addixim] myself to your faith [fidei], if I knew how so huge an impetus, which must needs [necessum est] be impressed on this body by the earth [tellure], could [valeret] subsist in so fragile a nature. For suppose [Fac] the earth [tellurem] by some chance stopped [subsistere]: would not the air with a monstrous impetus sweep away [everreret] all things clinging to the earth [terrae], trees, castles [castra], the mountains themselves? It has therefore the same impetus even now, although not visible [spectabilem]; and how so vehement an impetus can remain without a shaking and as it were a pulverization of the whole air, I am wholly ignorant. |
| Ereu. Quid enim impetum appellas? ipsumne motum, an inclinationem ad motum? | Ereu. For what do you call impetus? motion itself, or the inclination to motion? |
| [p. 211] Andab. Utrumvis, modo id sit quod in corporibus experimur, tum solidis, tum fluidis. | [p. 211] Andab. Whichever you will, provided that it be that which we experience in bodies, both solid and fluid. |
| Ereu. Si motum esse facis, & tellus subito subsistat, aërem quoque subsistere eodem facto facis. Causa enim effectus, qui in esse a causa pendet, sublata, clarum est effectum quoad esse tolli. Motum autem quoniam continue mutatur, palam est esse non habere, nisi dum actu fit, & quod hinc sequitur a movente, quoad esse, pendere. Fit itaque sublato terrae motu qui unica & totalis est causa huius motus aëris, etiam ipsum motum cessaturum & quieturum aërem, neque ullas iras in arbores, castra, montes, exerciturum. | Ereu. If you make it to be motion, and the earth [tellus] suddenly stops [subsistat], you make the air also to stop by the same deed [eodem facto]. For the cause of an effect which in being [in esse] hangs on its cause being taken away, it is clear that the effect is taken away as to being [quoad esse]. But motion, since it is continually changed, it is plain does not have being except while it is in act being done [actu fit], and, what follows from this, depends, as to being, on the mover [a movente]. It comes about therefore that, the motion of the earth [terrae] being taken away, which is the unique and total cause of this motion of the air, the motion itself also will cease, and the air will be at rest [quieturum], and will not exercise any wraths [iras] upon trees, castles, mountains. |
| Andab. Aliter in aqua fieri videmus. | Andab. Otherwise we see it happen in water. |
| Ereu. In his motibus quos quotidie experimur, aliter quidem accidit, sed causa quoque manifesta est, quia & aqua supra aequilibrium elevata est, & cadit in communem causam, nempe gravitatem; cuius postmodum {?exercetur} motibus; sic & pendulum, quod sua gravitate cietur, videmus paulatim ad quietem restitui, quia manet motus causa, ipsa nempe gravitas, & fortassis omnis motus qui cessante motore non interit, gravitas aut similis aliqua causa communis parens comperietur, si penitius inspiciamus. Unde si terra quiesceret subito, non propterea un[p. 212]daturum mare crediderim, ut etiam prius dicturiebam. Sin impetum non aliud vis quam inclinationem ad motum, vide quomodo tellure volutata, ipsamet gravitas & connexio aëris cum tellure ipso facto ad motum inclinatio erit: stante vero tellure, rursus ipso facto haec eadem faciet inclinationem ad quietem. Unde neque ex uno, neque ex alio capite fundamentum apparet, cur aër quiescente terra motum continuaret. | Ereu. In these motions which we daily experience it happens otherwise indeed, but the cause also is manifest: because both the water has been raised above equilibrium, and falls under [cadit in] a common cause, namely heaviness [gravitatem]; by whose motions it is afterwards exercised [{?exercetur}]; so also the pendulum, which is stirred [cietur] by its own heaviness, we see restored little by little to rest, because the cause of the motion remains, namely heaviness itself; and perhaps every motion which does not perish when the mover [motore] ceases — heaviness or some similar cause will be found [comperietur] [its] common parent, if we look into it more deeply. Whence, if the earth [terra] should suddenly be at rest, I would not on that account believe that the sea would [p. 212] surge [undaturum], as I was wanting to say [dicturiebam] before too. But if by impetus you mean [vis] nothing other than inclination to motion, see how, the earth [tellure] being rolled round [volutata], the very heaviness and connexion of the air with the earth [tellure] will by that very fact [ipso facto] be an inclination to motion; but the earth [tellure] standing, again by that very fact this same will make an inclination to rest. Whence neither from the one nor from the other head [capite] does a foundation appear why the air, the earth [terra] being at rest, should continue its motion. |
| Asphal. Andabata suo ardore etiam me in scopulum impegit, & ignorare mihi videor, quorum prius me peritum existimabam. Dic itaque, Ereuni, quodcumque loco movetur, nonne causam habet cur loco moveatur? Causa autem est virtus loco movens, & virtus loco movens ut loco moveat, aliquid in ipsum mobile imprimere debet. Hoc sive impetum sive alio nomine vis indigitare, susdeque habeo, modo constet aliquid in omni re mota esse, ex quo sequatur eam loco in quo est, excedere. Aër itaque ut excedat loco in quo est, aliquam impressionem a tellure habeat necesse est. Hanc volo scire cur eum non moveat postmodum quiescente terra. | Asphal. Andabata by his ardour has dashed even me upon a rock [in scopulum impegit], and I seem to myself to be ignorant of things in which [quorum] I formerly thought myself skilled. Tell [me] therefore, Ereunius: whatever is moved in place [loco], does it not have a cause why it is moved in place? But the cause is a virtue moving in place, and the virtue moving in place, in order that it may move in place, must impress something upon the mobile itself. Whether you will call [indigitare] this impetus or by another name, I hold it all one [susdeque habeo], provided it be agreed that there is something in every moved thing from which it follows that it goes out of [excedere] the place in which it is. The air therefore, in order that it may go out of the place in which it is, must needs [necesse est] have some impression from the earth [tellure]. This [impression] I wish to know why it does not move it [the air] afterwards, the earth [terra] being at rest. |

## Words and phrases with alternative or special meanings

- ***gerendus est mos (morem gerere)*** — rendered “compliance must be borne [gerendus est mos]”. to humour, gratify, comply with someone. Classical idiom (Plautus, Terence); kept literal with the Latin bracketed.
- ***ab Ixione mutuor*** — rendered “I borrow from Ixion”. i.e. from the clouds. Classical allusion: Ixion embraced a cloud (Nephele) shaped like Hera; White's witty periphrasis for 'testimony from clouds'.
- ***nexus (montium)*** — rendered “linkage [nexus]”. chain, connection, bond. The mountain-chains girdling the globe; the air enclosed among them is argued to turn with the earth.
- ***dissepire*** — rendered “fence apart [dissepiantur]”. separate by a hedge/partition (dis-saepire). Rare verb; the mountains that top the clouds are as widely spaced as the Pacific.
- ***adversum (… terrae)*** — rendered “against [adversum] the earth”. adverb/preposition 'opposite, against'; possibly a noun 'opposing [course]'. Elliptical: a noun such as cursu must be supplied with concitatissimo & constantissimo.
- ***in occasum*** — rendered “towards the setting”. westward. Air left behind by an eastward-turning earth would seem to stream west (the Galilean east-wind argument).
- ***Meteorologi*** — rendered “the Meteorologists”. writers on meteora (Aristotle's Meteorologica tradition). Scholastic discipline of sublunary 'meteors'; the twilight-height estimate of c. 50 miles.
- ***cauda Fromondiana*** — rendered “the Fromondian tail”. the tail of the comet of 1618 as observed by Libertus Fromondus (Froidmont) at Louvain. Fromondus, De cometa anni 1618 (1619); White uses Fromondus' own observation (ipsius experimentum) against the anti-Copernican Fromondus.
- ***experimentum*** — rendered “experiment [experimentum]”. experience, observation, trial. Early-modern sense is chiefly 'observation/experience', not a contrived test.
- ***convincere*** — rendered “prove [convincit]”. convict, refute, demonstrate irrefutably. Forensic sense: the observation 'convicts' the tail of moving with the earth.
- ***creditu arduum*** — rendered “hard of belief [creditu arduum]”. hard to believe. Supine in -u.
- ***mage*** — rendered “rather [mage]”. archaic/poetic form of magis.
- ***Perigaeus (sol)*** — rendered “Perigee [Perigaeum] sun”. the sun at (or near) its least distance from the earth. Pre-Keplerian solar theory: perigee near the winter solstice (actually early January); a nearer sun shortens the earth's shadow-cone.
- ***repedare*** — rendered “step back [repedare]”. to go back, return. Archaic verb (Pacuvius, Lucilius); the sun returning toward its rising, i.e. the small hours before dawn.
- ***pili (cometici)*** — rendered “cometic hairs [pili]”. the rays of the comet's tail. Plays on coma/cometa (Gk κομήτης, 'long-haired').
- ***assignandum est (+ acc.)*** — rendered “must be assigned [assignandum est]”. impersonal gerundive with a direct object. Archaic construction (Lucretius, Varro); White uses it again in coniiciendum … asserendum.
- ***quod meminerim*** — rendered “that I remember [quod meminerim]”. so far as I remember. Restrictive subjunctive.
- ***addixim*** — rendered “would have bound [addixim] myself”. archaic perfect subjunctive = addixerim; addicere fidei, to devote/bind to someone's credit. Archaism of the sigmatic type (cf. faxim, ausim).
- ***impetus*** — rendered “impetus”. impressed force; or the motion itself; or inclination to motion. Late-scholastic impetus theory (Buridan) vs Galilean conservation of motion; Ereunius distinguishes motion from inclination.
- ***inclinatio ad motum / ad quietem*** — rendered “inclination to motion / to rest”. tendency, propensity. White/Digby: no intrinsic impetus; heaviness and the air's connexion with the earth constitute the inclination.
- ***in esse / quoad esse*** — rendered “in being / as to being”. in respect of existence. Scholastic: causes in esse (on which the effect's continued being depends) vs in fieri.
- ***actu fit*** — rendered “is in act being done [actu fit]”. actually comes to be. Motion as actus entis in potentia (Aristotle, Phys. III.1): it exists only in its actual becoming.
- ***eodem facto / ipso facto*** — rendered “by the same deed / by that very fact”. thereby, automatically.
- ***dicturire*** — rendered “be wanting to say [dicturiebam]”. desiderative of dicere. Rare desiderative verb.
- ***undare*** — rendered “surge [undaturum]”. to rise in waves, to billow.
- ***caput*** — rendered “head [capite]”. heading, point of argument. Rhetorical/logical division.
- ***in scopulum impingere*** — rendered “dash upon a rock”. to wreck, run aground. Nautical metaphor for being brought to a perplexity.
- ***susque deque habere (printed susdeque)*** — rendered “hold it all one [susdeque habeo]”. to be indifferent, not care whether up or down. Plautine/Gellian idiom; printed form 'susdéque'.
- ***indigitare*** — rendered “call [indigitare]”. to name, invoke by name. Archaic religious term (indigitamenta); cf. indigitatio in glossary.
- ***virtus loco movens*** — rendered “virtue moving in place”. motive power producing local motion. Aristotelian principle omne quod movetur ab alio movetur (Phys. VII.1).
- ***impressio*** — rendered “impression”. what is impressed by the mover on the mobile. Asphalius' formulation of impetus theory, the question for Knot ix.

## Translator's notes

- Unit: Dialogue II, Knot viii, part 2 of 2 (end of the knot). PDF pp. 219–223 = printed pp. 208–212, from ‘Ereu. Andabatae quatenus possumus, gerendus est mos’ (p. 208, beside the marginal ‘Phaenomena motum aëris testantia’) to Asphalius' ‘…postmodum quiescente terra.’ (p. 212). Knot ix begins on p. 212 (PDF 223) with ‘Ereu. Permitte ut paulo altius rem incipiam’, beside the marginal ‘Nodus IX. Corpus loco moveri’.
- Offset +11 holds (running heads 208–212; signature Dd p. 209, Dd ij p. 211).
- Uncertain or noted readings: (1) p. 208 ‘montium longi … nexus’ (checked on a 300-dpi crop: clearly ‘longi’) — does not agree with nexus; probably a misprint for ‘longus’ (or ‘longe’); translated ‘long’ and marked {?longi}. (2) p. 208 ‘montes qui eos superant’ — eos (masc.) cannot refer to nubes (fem.); taken as a slip for ‘eas’ (the clouds) and so translated. (3) p. 208 ‘concitatissimo & constantissimo adversum terrae’ — elliptical; a noun such as ‘cursu’ supplied. (4) p. 211 ‘exereetur’ as printed (300-dpi crop) — read as ‘exercetur’ and marked {?exercetur}; the clause ‘cuius postmodum exercetur motibus’ is itself awkward. (5) p. 211 ‘omnis motus … gravitas aut similis aliqua causa communis parens comperietur’ — anacoluthon kept, with ‘[its]’ supplied. (6) p. 212 ‘susdéque’ as printed, for ‘susque deque’. (7) p. 209 ‘solem … septem circiter horarum esse’ — taken as the length of the day (about seven hours of daylight) at Louvain in mid-December.
- Verification pass: each Latin sentence re-read against the page images (150 dpi; 300-dpi crops of p. 208 ‘longi’ and p. 211 ‘exereetur’) and against the English; nothing found omitted or added. 3 corrections: (1) ‘extuleris’ — perfect subjunctive in indirect question rendered ‘how you have raised’ (draft: ‘how you would raise’); (2) ‘protraxerim’ — future perfect/perfect subjunctive kept as ‘shall have drawn out’ (draft: ‘draw out’); (3) ‘Lunam ipsam … defendi’ — recognised as still dependent on ‘Cogita’, ‘[Consider]’ supplied (draft had made it a main clause ‘The moon is defended’).
- Conventions: as in the glossary — tellus ‘earth [tellus]’ and terra ‘earth [terra]’ bracketed throughout; supplied words in square brackets; page breaks marked [p. n] inside the row where they fall; ‘gravitas’ = ‘heaviness’, ‘impetus’ = ‘impetus’, ‘virtus’ = ‘virtue’, ‘mobile’ = ‘the mobile’.
