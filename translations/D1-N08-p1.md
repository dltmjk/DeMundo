## Dialogue I, Knot viii, part 1 (printed pp. 55–65)

| Latin | English |
|---|---|
| [p. 55] [Margin: Nodus VIII. In Coelis esse corpora Analoga nostris.] Andab. Ergo te demonstravisse dicis quae apud nos sunt, eadem in Luna et caeteris Astris nasci et denasci? Quando plurimum poscas, hoc tantum tibi petendum est: Coelos non esse, neque Astra corruptibilitate esse exempta. | [p. 55] [Margin: Knot VIII. That in the Heavens there are bodies Analogous [Analoga] to ours.] Andab. Therefore you say you have demonstrated that the things which are among us, the same are born and un-born [denasci] in the Moon and the other Stars? When you demand the most, this only is to be claimed [petendum] by you: that the Heavens are not, nor are the Stars exempt from corruptibility. |
| Esse vero seriem generationum et corru- [p. 56] ptionum nostris persimilem, clarum est humano maius esse captu, ut quicquam {?cum} ratione afferatur. | But that there is a series of generations and corru- [p. 56] ptions very like ours, it is clear [that this] is greater than human grasp [captu], [too great] that anything should be brought forward [about it] with reason [ratione]. |
| Cum enim scientia nostra quanta quanta est, a sensibus pendet, necessum est, primos lapides et fundamenti structuram experimento authore et Architecto dirigi. | For since our science [scientia], however great it is [quanta quanta est], hangs from the senses, it is necessary that the first stones and the structure of the foundation be directed with experiment [experimento] as author and Architect. |
| Porro de rebus quae in Luna geruntur (et haec est citima Astrorum et nostris observationibus maxime obnoxia) ipsamet plena lucidius est nos experimenta particularia neque habere, neque colligere ullis machinis posse. Nedum ut aliis Astris litem intendamus. | Further, concerning the things which are carried on in the Moon (and this is the nearest [citima] of the Stars and the most exposed [obnoxia] to our observations), it is clearer than [the Moon] herself when full [ipsamet plena] that we neither have particular experiments [experimenta particularia], nor can collect [them] by any machines [machinis]. Much less [nedum] that we should stretch the suit [litem intendamus] to the other Stars. |
| Ereu. Nihil tam propere, nosti enim diverbium, quod de Cane festina solet iaci; Tu vero si libellum quemdam aureum (quem ego avide pervolutavi) de immortalitate animae legisses, aliud sentires. [Margin: Libellus de immortalitate animae.] | Ereu. Nothing so hastily; for you know the saying [diverbium] which is wont to be thrown out about the hasty Bitch [de Cane festina]. But you, if you had read a certain golden little book (which I eagerly rolled through [pervolutavi]) on the immortality of the soul [animae], would think otherwise. [Margin: A little book on the immortality of the soul.] |
| Et ut hoc saltem videas, quibus scalis Arx Coeli superetur, et ad notitiam eorum quae ibi geruntur, qualemcumque ascendatur, totius libri argumentum tibi expono. | And that you may see at least this, by what ladders the Citadel of the Heaven is surmounted, and [by what ladders] one ascends to a notice [notitiam], of whatever sort, of the things which are carried on there, I set out for you the argument [argumentum] of the whole book. |
| Videns itaque vir ingeniosissimus in eo maximam vim positam ut animam hominis spiritualem esse convinceret, quo immortalem esse consequeretur, neque posse immaterialitatem ipsius dignosci nisi sepositis corporis et spiritus rationibus, eo sibi collimandum existimavit, ut quo corpus pervenire posset et in quo spiritibus cederet, expli- [p. 57] catum faceret. | Seeing therefore, [that] most ingenious man, that the greatest force [vim] was placed in this, that he should prove [convinceret] the soul of man to be spiritual, whereby it would follow [that it] is immortal, and that its immateriality cannot be discerned unless the reasons [rationibus] of body and of spirit are set apart, he judged that he must aim [collimandum] at this: that he should make expli- [p. 57] cated [explicatum faceret] whither body could reach and in what it yielded to spirits. |
| Sed cum naturam corpoream desidia humana undique spiritualium operationum modo, in scholis explicari videret, nihil se effecturum praevidit, nisi ea fere omnia quae in natura difficillima explicatu forent, et propterea per Deum ἀπὸ μηχανῆς a vulgaribus Philosophis expedirentur, quam posset brevissime tangeret, et quomodo corporum implexis texturis perficerentur, declararet. | But since he saw that bodily nature, through human sloth, was on all sides explicated in the schools after the mode of spiritual operations, he foresaw that he would effect nothing, unless he touched, as briefly as he could, almost all those things which in nature would be most difficult to explicate, and for that reason were disposed of [expedirentur] by common Philosophers through a God ἀπὸ μηχανῆς [from the machine], and declared how they were perfected by the interwoven textures [implexis texturis] of bodies. |
| Quid ergo fecit? Cum corpus idem esse quod magnum a pueris aeque ac senibus intellectum videret, inde exorsus quantitatem per rarum et densum quasi primis differentiis scindi delineat. | What therefore did he do? Since he saw that body is understood by boys equally as by old men to be the same as [what is] great [magnum], having begun from there, he delineates [delineat] that quantity is cut by rare and dense as it were by first differences [primis differentiis]. |
| Ubi vero rarum sit et densum, illic adiecto, quem manibus tangimus, universi situ, ex necessitate gravitatem quoque et levitatem nasci. Ubi haec essent, primas quatuor qualitates, calorem et frigus, humorem et siccitatem abesse non posse: | But where there is rare and dense, there, the site [situ] of the universe, which we touch with our hands, being added, [he shows that] out of necessity heaviness also and lightness are born. Where these were, [that] the first four qualities, heat and cold, moisture and dryness, cannot be absent: |
| Et his positis, Elementorum rationem esse necessariam, quorum singula duas ex his qualitatibus Aristotelis ad iussum sortita forent; Elementa vero magna mole et suis quasi latifundiis non posse extare, particulis autem minimis continuo permisceri. | And these being posited, [that] the account [rationem] of the Elements is necessary, of which each would have obtained [sortita] two of these qualities at the bidding of Aristotle; but [that] the Elements cannot stand out [extare] in great mass and as it were in their own broad estates [latifundiis], but are continually mixed together in smallest particles. |
| Hinc mixtorum communes rationes subiungit, variorum motuum causas, et ortus exponit; ad plantas et animalia se porrigit. | Hence he subjoins the common reasons [rationes] of mixed [bodies]; he sets out the causes and the origins [ortus] of various motions; he stretches himself to plants and animals. |
| Ubique ostendit ea quae praeter hominem agantur, talia esse ut quasi filis ex Aranei cavo emis- [p. 58] sis et intertextis, sic e naturae visceribus et densi rarique complexionibus necessario nexu consequantur. | Everywhere he shows that the things which are done besides [praeter] man are such that, as it were by threads sent [p. 58] out and interwoven from the hollow of a Spider, so from the bowels of nature and the complexions [complexionibus] of dense and rare they follow by a necessary bond [nexu]. |
| Postea animi quoque lustrat germina, eaque alterius esse potentiae neque his instrumentis confieri posse docet. Tum demum immortalem animam, et brevem de statu separationis commentationem adiungit. | Afterwards he also surveys the seeds [germina] of the mind [animi], and teaches that they are of another power [potentiae] and cannot be brought about [confieri] by these instruments. Then at last he adjoins the immortal soul [animam], and a brief commentation on the state of separation. |
| Habes, Andabata, expetendi illius libelli summam, quam utinam communis semel iuris faceret, neque virginem chartam durus pater sterilitate senili condemnaret. Sperarem non minorem Christianae rei fructum quam meritissimo viro gloriam inde surrecturam esse. | You have, Andabata, the sum of that little book which is to be sought after; which would that he would once make of common right [communis iuris], and that the hard father would not condemn the virgin paper to senile barrenness [sterilitate senili]. I would hope that no less fruit for the Christian cause [rei] than glory for the most deserving man would rise up from it. |
| [Margin: Knelmus eques Digbaeus.] Asphal. Suspicor? an etiam quid inaudivi de libello quem memoras, et authore ipsius amico tuo, Ereuni; sed ecquando putas ausurum in solem? | [Margin: Kenelm [Knelmus] Digby, knight [eques].] Asphal. Do I suspect? or have I also heard something of the little book which you mention, and of its author, your friend, Ereunius? But when, do you think, will it dare [to come out] into the sun? |
| Ereu. Non meo, Asphali, sed nostrum omnium. Meo, si vis ita, sed patrono, neque enim tantis me dignor amicis, viro ad omnia nato, et Marti grato et Musis, sed (ut hodie res sunt) et caetera occupatissimo, et nondum Berzellai annos indepto. | Ereu. Not my [friend], Asphalius, but [the friend] of us all. Mine, if you will [have it] so, but [as] patron, for I do not deem myself worthy of such great friends [amicis]: a man born for all things, and pleasing both to Mars and to the Muses, but (as things are today) both in the rest most occupied, and not yet having reached the years of Barzillai [Berzellai]. |
| De operis tamen natali laete auguror, adeo verum est ventum nullum adeo infoelicem, ut nemini emolumentum afflet. Credo enim non nisi obstetricantibus infortuniis nasciturum. | Of the birthday [natali] of the work, however, I augur gladly; so true is it that no wind is so unhappy that it blows emolument to no one. For I believe [that it] will be born only with misfortunes acting as midwives [obstetricantibus]. |
| Andab. Gratulor equidem viro dignissi- [p. 59] mo omnem laudis occasionem; sed verene quae dicis probata existimas, an ad plausum populumque ingeniose commenta? | Andab. I indeed congratulate the most worthy [p. 59] man on every occasion of praise; but do you truly judge the things which you say to be proved, or ingeniously devised [commenta] for applause and the people? |
| Ereu. Tu vide. Si posito motu raritatem et densitatem, his cum motu notissimo coniunctis gravitatem et levitatem, his rursus cum raro et denso maritatis elementa, et ex his rursum obstetricante motu mixta ex necessitate parta et in lucem edita seu edenda ex ipsarum definitionum receptarum compositione concludit; Quid hoc vocas? | Ereu. You see [to it]. If, motion being posited, [he concludes] rarity and density; these being joined with a most known motion, heaviness and lightness; these again being married with rare and dense, the elements; and from these again, motion acting as midwife, [that] mixed [bodies] are of necessity brought forth and given out, or to be given out, into the light — [if] he concludes [this] from the composition of the received definitions themselves: What do you call this? |
| Ego certe si non demonstrationem (neque enim singula tam accurate dicta ausim affirmare) perfectam vocitem, hac non possum ei invidere gloriam, ut solam formam deesse confirmem. | I certainly, if I do not call [it] a perfect demonstration (for I would not dare to affirm that each [thing] is said so accurately), cannot begrudge it this glory: that I affirm [confirmem] that only the form [formam] is lacking. |
| Adeo sese ostendit veritas confessa (ut loquitur Poeta) Deam, qualisque videri Mathematicis et quanta solet. | So much does truth show herself, confessed [confessa] (as the Poet speaks) a Goddess, both of what sort and how great she is wont to be seen by Mathematicians. |
| Tantum igitur non licet in dubium vocare esse elementa et mixta, mixtorum vero alia solida, alia humida et fluentia, et horum (quoniam gradatim distinguuntur et iuxta elementorum dominium) alia saxis, alia terris, alia metallis, alia concretis liquoribus analoga, quantum si in scriniis Archimedeis reperisses. | So much [tantum], therefore, is it not permitted to call into doubt that there are elements and mixed [bodies], and of the mixed some solid, others humid and flowing, and of these (since they are distinguished by degrees and according to the dominion of the elements) some analogous to stones, others to earths, others to metals, others to congealed liquors, as much as [quantum] if you had found [it] in the book-chests [scriniis] of Archimedes. |
| De plantis et animalibus sicut affirmare nihil possum, sic nullam video vel exilem positis, quae iam asseruimus, ambigendi ansam. | Of plants and animals, as I can affirm nothing, so, the things which we have now asserted being posited, I see no handle, even a slender one, for doubting [ambigendi]. |
| Unum confidenter dico non esse aliud aliquod entium in universo genus, praeter ea [p. 60] [Margin: Genera rerum quae in universo sunt.] quae numeravimus. Siquidem scissa corporum per contradictionem specie, his partibus omnia tenentur. | One thing I say confidently: that there is not any other kind [genus] of beings [entium] in the universe besides those [p. 60] [Margin: The kinds of things which are in the universe.] which we have numbered. Since indeed, the species [specie] of bodies being cut by contradiction, all things are held in these parts. |
| Cum enim omne corpus aut inter rara sit vel densa, hoc primo quaerendum est, an unico raritatis sive densitatis gradu contentum sit, an plura quasi membra possideat. Hoc si est, iam mixti se natura alligat: si illud, clare elementum est, cum incompositum sit et mixti potestativa pars. | For since every body is either among the rare or the dense, this is first to be asked: whether it is content with a single degree of rarity or density, or possesses more [than one] [plura] as it were members. If this is [so], it now binds itself to the nature of a mixed [body]; if that, it is clearly an element, since it is uncompounded and a potestative part [potestativa pars] of a mixed [body]. |
| Mixtum modo vel augetur, quod plerisque experimur contingere, vel (quod gemmis fortassis et si quibus aliis durissimis ex natura esse potest) primam quantitatem naturaliter non mutat. | The mixed [body] now either is increased, which we experience to happen to most, or (which can be the case [esse] with gems perhaps and with any other most hard [things] by nature) does not naturally change its first quantity. |
| Hoc pure mixtum est, illud rursus ita scissile, ut aut pure ab extrinseco augeatur, aut habeat in sese organa quibus augmentum perficiat; hoc iam plantarum gradum occupat, relicto inter pura mixta divisionis consorte. | This is purely mixed; that again [is] so cuttable [scissile] that either it is increased purely from outside [ab extrinseco], or it has in itself organs by which it accomplishes increase; this now occupies the grade of plants, the partner [consorte] of the division being left among the pure mixed [bodies]. |
| Plantarum modo, quae nutritionem tantummodo patitur, ea planta est pura; si qua vero sese ad alimentum convertit et activum in sese motus principium ostendit, Animalis nomine insignitur, perfecti quidem, si motus perfectus est, hoc est si toto animali translato, loci mutatio fiat, sin aliqua tantum parte, Ζωοφύτων in classe numeratur. | Of plants now, that which only undergoes [patitur] nutrition, that is a pure plant; but if any turns itself to its food and shows in itself an active principle of motion, it is marked with the name of Animal — of a perfect [animal] indeed, if the motion is perfect, that is, if, the whole animal being transferred, a change of place takes place; but if [it is moved] by some part only, it is numbered in the class of Ζωοφύτων [Zoophytes]. |
| Et quoniam immediatae contradictionis necessitate singula membra coarctari vides, qualiscumque sit coniectura, haec in Coelis reperiri, hoc certe indubitatum [p. 61] relinquitur, alia sub corporis natura latere genera nulla posse. | And since you see that the single members are constrained [coarctari] by the necessity of immediate contradiction, of whatever sort the conjecture may be that these are found in the Heavens, this certainly is left undoubted [p. 61]: that no other kinds can lie hidden under the nature of body. |
| Hoc itaque confectum vides universum hoc quod contiguitate quanti unicum esse diximus his sui partibus adaequate et commode esse dispertitum. | This therefore you see accomplished: that this universe, which we said is one [unicum] by the contiguity of quantum, is divided adequately [adaequate] and fitly into these its parts. |
| [Margin: An in Luna possibile sit esse animalia.] Asphal. Agnosco equidem dictorum evidentiam et negationum veritatem; sed quod tam leviter superasti iugum illud non facile, ut animalia in reliquo mundo, etsi non affirmares, nullum tamen negandi argumentum te novisse dixeris, submiratus sum. | [Margin: Whether it is possible that there are animals in the Moon.] Asphal. I acknowledge indeed the evidence [evidentiam] of the things said and the truth of the denials; but that you surmounted so lightly that not easy ridge [iugum], so that you said, of animals in the rest of the world, although you did not affirm [them], yet that you knew no argument for denying [them], I have somewhat wondered [submiratus sum]. |
| Cum magni te Galilaei (authoris et laboriosissimi et cui debet Astronomia quicquid hoc saeculo profecit) et scripta te legisse et non neglexisse certus sim. Qui tamen oppositum saltem in Luna et diserte opinatur, et productis rationibus suadet. | Since I am certain that you have both read and not neglected the writings of the great Galileo (an author both most laborious and to whom Astronomy owes whatever it has advanced in this age). Who yet both expressly [diserte] opines the opposite, at least in the Moon, and persuades [of it] with reasons brought forward. |
| Ereu. Non tam rigide hoc Galilaeus expressit, ut per dicta necesse mihi sit ab authoritate ipsius discedere, si non ab ipsomet doctus essem veritatem amicitiae praevertere: Non enim haec negat, sed a nostris diversissima contendit. | Ereu. Galileo did not express this so rigidly that through [what I have] said [per dicta] it is necessary for me to depart from his authority, if I had not been taught by himself to put truth before friendship: for he does not deny these [things], but contends [that they are] most different from ours. |
| Asphal. Imo globum Lunae aqua et terra conflari negat. Rursus noctis et diei frequentem permutationem generationi animalium necessariam putat: et solem totis quindecim diebus exusturum plantas credit, si rectus tanto tempore verticem feriat. | Asphal. Nay, he denies that the globe of the Moon is compounded [conflari] of water and earth. Again, he thinks the frequent permutation of night and day necessary for the generation of animals: and he believes that the sun would burn up plants in fifteen whole days, if straight [rectus] it should strike the vertex for so great a time. |
| Adiicit his solis de- [p. 62] clinationem a nobis quadraginta septem gradus obtinere, in Luna non amplius decem. Tandem neque pluvias in Luna esse. Siquidem enim essent, aliquando visorum aliquod ab accuratissimo observatore ita fuisse occultaturas ut semet ipsas demonstrarent. | He adds to these that the de- [p. 62] clination of the sun holds forty-seven degrees with us [a nobis], in the Moon not more than ten. Finally, that neither are there rains in the Moon. For if indeed there were, [they] would at some time have hidden something of the things seen [visorum] by a most accurate observer, so that they would demonstrate themselves. |
| Ereu. Haec quidem Galilaeus, sed ut in dubiis timide. Mihi e diverso ex ea quam audivisti ex libello de immortalitate animae disputatione, tanta lux oborta est, ut non dubitem etiam homines et animalia nostratia posse in Luna foelicius quam apud nos morari. | Ereu. These [things] indeed Galileo [says], but, as in doubtful matters, timidly. To me, on the contrary [e diverso], from that disputation out of the little book on the immortality of the soul, which you have heard, so great a light has arisen that I do not doubt that even men and animals of our country [nostratia] can dwell in the Moon more happily than among us. |
| Reprehendo dictum. Non enim hoc volo sic esse, sed ex {?allegatis} nihil obstare quin ita sit. | I correct [reprehendo] the saying. For I do not mean [volo] that this is so, but that from the things alleged nothing stands in the way of its being so. |
| Et fortasse aliud aliquid mihi, et Galilaeo non provisum, vel alteri occurrat, vel sic extet, ut a nobis agnosci non possit, quod tamen rerum generationem pervertat. | And perhaps something else not foreseen by me and by Galileo may either occur to another, or may so exist that it cannot be recognized by us, which yet may overturn the generation of things. |
| Quod ergo Lunae globum tellure et aqua non constare dixit, si rationem expressisset, fortasse me quoque impulisset. | As to [the fact], therefore, that he said that the globe of the Moon does not consist of earth [tellure] and water, if he had expressed the reason [rationem], perhaps he would have driven me also [to it]. |
| Nunc quid terram vocet expecto, si enim non aliud quam mollem quandam substantiam generationi aptam, aquam vero visibilem quendam liquorem aere densiorem solidis corporibus rariorem, nescio quomodo, cum corpus solidum Lunae circumiectis sive vaporibus sive aere conclusum videamus, media corpora negare valeamus. | Now I wait [to hear] what he calls earth [terram]; for if not other than a certain soft substance apt for generation, and water a certain visible liquor denser than air, rarer than solid bodies, I do not know how, since we see the solid body of the Moon enclosed by surrounding either vapours or air, we could be able to deny the middle bodies. |
| Certe aquae [p. 63] proximum nebulosum aerem in confiniis hemisphaeriorum ipsemet sive notavit, sive collegit. | Certainly a cloudy air next [p. 63] to water, in the confines of the hemispheres, he himself either noted or gathered [collegit]. |
| Sed si vapores sunt subtiliores, etiam crassiores esse poene necessarium est. | But if there are subtler vapours, it is almost necessary that there are also grosser [ones]. |
| Noctis et diei permutationem adeo frequentem neque apud nos esse necessariam testantur regiones trimestri nocte dieque tamen habitatae. Narrant et sub ipso Polo vivere gentes, sed quoniam nostris navigationibus sunt imperviae ideoque dubiae, eas praetermitto. | That the permutation of night and day so frequent is not necessary even among us, regions attest which are nevertheless inhabited with a three-month night and day. They relate also that nations live under the Pole itself, but since they are impassable [imperviae] to our navigations and therefore doubtful, I pass them over. |
| Non taceo tamen prope Polos foeliciori et magis propitio vivi aethere quam in citerioribus partibus, etiam per navigationes esse compertum. Et tantumdem de Polari plaga rationi subolere. | I am not silent, however, [that] it has been found out, even through navigations, that near the Poles one lives [vivi] in a happier and more propitious aether [aethere] than in the nearer [citerioribus] parts. And that just so much is scented [subolere] by reason concerning the Polar region. |
| Quod autem metuit Lunari terrae a diuturno solis aspectu, in diversum prorsus abit: cum constet torridam Zonam tunc frigidissimam esse et hyemare, cum sol verticem maxime ferit. Quod inde fit quia tum sol densissimos exprimit vapores, qui quoniam gravissimi sunt, statim in terram rursus procumbunt. | But what he fears for the Lunar earth from the long-lasting aspect [aspectu] of the sun goes off altogether into the opposite: since it is established [constet] that the torrid Zone is then coldest and has winter, when the sun most strikes the vertex. Which comes about from this, that then the sun presses out the densest vapours, which, since they are heaviest, at once fall down again onto the earth. |
| Memini legisse, sed Authorem non teneo, in aliqua Zonae torridae regione singulis diebus paulo ante meridiem cogi nubes, sequi circa meridiem effusam pluviam. Inclinato iam sole serenitatem reddi. | I remember having read, but I do not hold [teneo] the Author, that in some region of the torrid Zone on each single day [singulis diebus] a little before noon clouds are gathered, [and] about noon a poured-out rain follows. The sun being now inclined, clear weather [serenitatem] is restored. |
| Neque praetereunda est non aequanda nostris montium Lunarium altitudo quae tria secum trahit, frigidissimum in cacuminibus aetherem, [p. 64] Acuti enim montes parum incalescunt, ut in Peruvianis quos, Andes appellant, constat; | Nor is to be passed over the height of the Lunar mountains, not to be equalled by ours, which draws three things with it: a most cold aether on the summits, [p. 64] for pointed mountains grow warm little, as is established [constat] in the Peruvian [mountains] which they call Andes; |
| Terras quasdam medias vitae humanae iucundissimas; valles demum ut fervidissimas, sic aquis exundantissimas ventisque perflatissimas. Nam et montium originem secundariam ex aquarum defluxu existere ipsa quotidiana experientia perspicuum est. | certain middle lands most pleasant for human life; valleys lastly, as most hot, so most overflowing with waters and most blown through by winds. For that the secondary origin of mountains, too, arises from the flowing-down of waters is clear by daily experience itself. |
| Pensandae quoque sunt longissimae umbrae ex tam altis montibus: Neque totos quatuordecim dies perpendiculariter cuiquam uni parti imminere solem, sed post triduum auctioribus laetari umbris. | To be weighed also are the longest shadows from mountains so high: and that the sun does not [neque] overhang [imminere] perpendicularly any one part for fourteen whole days, but [that each part] after three days rejoices in increased [auctioribus] shadows. |
| Neque solis tam exigua declinatio quicquam officit, cum latitudo magis ad communicandum calorem quam frigus instituta videatur. Polares enim plagae sine hac varietate non sentirent aestatem, Temperatae non haberent decoctas fruges. | Nor does so small a declination of the sun hinder anything, since the breadth [latitudo] seems to have been instituted more for communicating heat than cold. For the Polar regions without this variety would not feel summer; the Temperate would not have ripened [decoctas] crops. |
| Rursus quo minus declinat Sol, eo frigidiores ex Polis aspirant venti. Praeterquam quod brevis Solis mora et circumvolutatio circa terram qua a Sole recedit satis hoc malum solari videtur: | Again, the less the Sun declines, by so much colder winds breathe from the Poles. Besides that the short delay [mora] of the Sun, and the rolling-round [circumvolutatio] about the earth by which [the Moon] recedes from the Sun, seems sufficiently to console this evil: |
| Neque putem eum satis firmo niti ad pluvias tollendas argumento. Monet enim ipse Galilaeus nubes tonitrua ferentes non amplius tertia milliaris parte a nobis abesse, cum verticales sunt, quae autem non amplius distant neque magnae esse possunt, neque satis observationi obnoxiae. | Nor would I think that he leans on a sufficiently firm argument for removing the rains. For Galileo himself advises that clouds bearing thunders are not away from us more than the third part of a mile, when they are vertical; but those which are not more distant can neither be great, nor sufficiently exposed [obnoxiae] to observation. |

## Words and phrases with alternative or special meanings

- ***In Coelis esse corpora Analoga nostris / Corpora coelestia esse nostris analoga*** — rendered “That in the Heavens there are bodies Analogous to ours”. Margin title differs in wording from the FM2 table title ('That the heavenly bodies are analogous to ours'). 'analogus' = proportionally corresponding, not identical; White's claim is of the same kinds (elements, mixts) in the heavens.
- ***nasci et denasci*** — rendered “be born and un-born”. 'denasci' is a rare archaism (Varro, Nonius) = to die, perish. Pairs with generatio/corruptio.
- ***quanta quanta est*** — rendered “however great it is”. Reduplicated relative = quantacumque. Comic/colloquial idiom (Terence).
- ***experimentum*** — rendered “experiment”. = experience, particular observation; not necessarily a contrived test. Cf. 'experimenta particularia'; Aristotelian empeiria as the base of science.
- ***ipsamet plena lucidius*** — rendered “clearer than [the Moon] herself when full”. Pun on proverb 'luce clarius' (clearer than daylight).
- ***litem intendere*** — rendered “stretch the suit”. Legal: to bring/extend an action at law.
- ***de Cane festina*** — rendered “about the hasty Bitch”. Proverb 'canis festinans caecos parit catulos' (the hasty bitch bears blind whelps); Erasmus, Adagia II.ii.40. Warning against premature judgment.
- ***libellus aureus de immortalitate animae*** — rendered “golden little book on the immortality of the soul”. The margin on p. 58 identifies the author as Kenelm Digby. Evidently Digby's Two Treatises (Paris, 1644: Of Bodies; Of Mans Soule), which White read in manuscript; the outline (quantity → rare/dense → heavy/light → four qualities → elements → mixts → plants/animals → soul → separated state) matches it.
- ***Deus ἀπὸ μηχανῆς*** — rendered “a God from the machine”. = deus ex machina. Ironic for scholastic recourse to occult qualities or divine intervention.
- ***rarum et densum*** — rendered “rare and dense”. Digby/White's primary differences of quantity. Core of White and Digby's 'Peripatetic atomism' in which density is the basic division of body.
- ***primae differentiae*** — rendered “first differences”. Logical: the first specific differences dividing a genus. Porphyrian division.
- ***latifundia*** — rendered “broad estates”. Large Roman landed estates. Metaphor: elements do not exist pure in great tracts.
- ***complexio*** — rendered “complexion”. = combination, entwining of rare and dense. See glossary; also Galenic 'temperament'.
- ***confieri*** — rendered “be brought about”. Rare passive of conficere.
- ***status separationis*** — rendered “state of separation”. The soul's condition after death. Scholastic eschatology; Digby's second treatise ends on it.
- ***communis iuris facere*** — rendered “make of common right”. = publish. Legal metaphor.
- ***Berzellai anni*** — rendered “years of Barzillai”. Barzillai the Gileadite, aged 80 (2 Sam 19:32–35). Digby was born 1603.
- ***ventus nullus adeo infoelix*** — rendered “no wind so unhappy”. Proverb: 'it is an ill wind that blows nobody good'. Allusion to Digby's misfortunes in 1642 (imprisonment).
- ***obstetricare*** — rendered “act as midwife”. Recurs: 'obstetricante motu', 'obstetricantibus infortuniis'. Socratic maieutic image.
- ***veritas confessa Deam*** — rendered “truth confessed a Goddess”. Virgil, Aen. II.589–591 (Venus 'confessa deam qualisque videri / caelicolis et quanta solet'); White replaces 'caelicolis' with 'Mathematicis'.
- ***forma (demonstrationis)*** — rendered “form”. Logical form of the syllogism vs its matter. Cf. glossary 'materia (propositionis)'.
- ***scrinia Archimedea*** — rendered “book-chests of Archimedes”. Scrinium = case for scrolls. = as certain as a mathematical demonstration.
- ***per contradictionem scindere*** — rendered “cut by contradiction”. Dichotomous division into A / not-A. Guarantees exhaustiveness of the division.
- ***pars potestativa*** — rendered “potestative part”. Scholastic: part 'in power', virtually present in a whole (vs integral part). Elements remain virtually in the mixt.
- ***Ζωόφυτα*** — rendered “Zoophytes”. Plant-animals (sponges, sea-anemones). Aristotelian intermediate class (Hist. anim. VIII.1).
- ***contiguitas quanti*** — rendered “contiguity of quantum”. Refers back to the world's unity (D1 earlier knots).
- ***iugum*** — rendered “ridge”. Mountain ridge, or yoke. Image of a difficult pass.
- ***veritatem amicitiae praevertere*** — rendered “put truth before friendship”. 'Amicus Plato, sed magis amica veritas'; Galileo quotes it in the Dialogo (Day I).
- ***Galileo's objections*** — rendered “—”. Asphalius summarizes Galileo, Dialogo (1632), Day I: Moon not of earth and water; month-long day; sun's declination 47° vs. ~10° on the Moon; no rains (no clouds seen to hide features). White answers each point.
- ***latitudo*** — rendered “breadth”. = range of the sun's declination; not geographical latitude.
- ***lunicolae*** — rendered “Moon-dwellers”. Rare coinage (Kepler, Somnium).
- ***Teutonicae observationes*** — rendered “German observations”. Probably Kepler or Scheiner (Rosa Ursina) reporting a cloud on the Moon. Identification uncertain.
- ***Andes*** — rendered “Andes”. Peruvian mountains; cold summits, via travel literature (Acosta).
