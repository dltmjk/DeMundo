# De Mundo — Dialogue II (De forma mundi, seu de motu magnorum corporum), Knot vi

*Latin title:* Nodus VI. Cur tellus axem parallelum axi Aequatoris teneat  
*English title:* Knot VI. Why the earth holds [its] axis parallel to the axis of the Equator  
*Pages:* PDF pp. 203–207 = printed pp. 192–196 (from ‘Asphal. Sic me uno absolvis dubio’ on p. 192 to Asphalius' ‘…ostendere conatur’ on p. 196; knot vii begins on p. 196 with ‘Ereu. Ardua est haec difficultas’).

## Dialogue II, Knot vi (printed pp. 192–196)

| Latin | English |
|---|---|
| [p. 192] Asphal. Sic me uno absolvis dubio, ut altero illiges. Nosti enim terram in toto suo cursu polos suos rectos, & axem parallelum sibimet servare, & utrisque Aequatoris directionem communem esse; quomodo haec cum perpete propter Zodiacum corporis sui motu consistere valeant, impos sum. Ipsum enim diurnum motum per Zodiacum ferri necessum est, si eum sequatur annuus qui signa observat. [Margin: Nodus VI. Cur tellus axem parallelum axi Aequatoris teneat?] | [p. 192] Asphal. Thus you release me from one doubt, so as to bind [me] in another. For you know that the earth [terram] in its whole course keeps its poles upright [rectos], and [its] axis parallel to itself, and that to both [utrisque] the direction of the Equator is common; how these things can [valeant] stand together [consistere] with the perpetual [perpete] motion of its body along [propter] the Zodiac, I am unable [impos sum] [to see]. For it is necessary that the diurnal motion itself be borne [ferri] through the Zodiac, if the annual [motion], which keeps to [observat] the signs, follows it. [Margin: Knot VI. Why the earth [tellus] holds [its] axis parallel to the axis of the Equator?] |
| Ereu. Acute observasti, sed sic tecum rationes supputa. Mare pacificum caetera quae usquam sunt, immani differentia superare, & [p. 193] soli penitus obnoxium esse. Coniunctum est, vapores ex eo elevatos maiori & mole & virtute esse quam in alia orbis parte eleventur. Hinc vero necessum est motum citatiorem telluris esse, cum sol hic perpendiculo collimat. Ignosce, Asphali, cogitatio me praeter tuam interrogationem traduxit. Ad te itaque redeo: Non vides cum telluris corpus rotundum sit, posse subtus Zodiacum sic in circulum moveri, ut unum quodlibet punctum eandem semper comparationem ad situm alicuius immoti observet? | Ereu. Sharply have you observed, but reckon up [supputa] the accounts [rationes] with yourself thus. [Consider] that the pacific sea surpasses the other [seas] which are anywhere by an enormous difference, and [p. 193] is wholly exposed [obnoxium] to the sun. Joined [to this] is [the fact] that the vapours raised from it are of greater both bulk [mole] and virtue [virtute] than [those] raised in another part of the globe [orbis]. Hence truly it is necessary that the motion of the earth [telluris] be quicker [citatiorem], when the sun here aims perpendicularly [perpendiculo collimat]. Forgive [me], Asphalius, [my] thought has carried me across beyond your question. To you therefore I return: do you not see that, since the body of the earth [telluris] is round, it can be moved beneath the Zodiac in a circle in such a way that any one point keeps [observet] always the same relation [comparationem] to the site [situm] of something unmoved? |
| Asphal. Satis video, sed hoc ipsum sine novo motu vel potius concausa motus quae motum priorem nova additione cumulet & variet non comprehendo, huius autem nullum fundamentum conspicor. | Asphal. I see [it] sufficiently, but this very thing I do not comprehend without a new motion, or rather a joint-cause [concausa] of motion which would heap up and vary the prior motion by a new addition; of this, however, I perceive no foundation. |
| Ereu. Tene sphaeram rectam, & cogita telluris axi appensum pondus quod eum parallelum axi Aequatoris teneat, & vide quomodo cum sub Zodiaco movetur terra, semetipsam rectam sustineat. | Ereu. Hold the sphere upright [rectam], and think of a weight hung on the axis of the earth [telluris] which holds it parallel to the axis of the Equator, and see how, when the earth [terra] is moved under the Zodiac, it sustains itself upright [rectam]. |
| Asphal. Egregie, sed Lemniscus quem appendisti Australi Polo, vim motivam habet. Hanc itaque mihi etiam in nostra tellure ostende, & quievi. | Asphal. Excellently; but the pendant [Lemniscus] which you have hung on the Southern Pole has a motive force [vim motivam]. Show me this, therefore, also in our earth [tellure], and I have rested [quievi]. |
| Ereu. Adverte tantummodo cur additum sit telluri fictitiae momentum. Nonne propterea ne a circulis quibus sustinetur, per eorum [p. 194] motus rapiatur? Cum itaque in rerum natura, libero tellus aere pendeat, neque externis arctata sit vinculis, hoc periculo exempta non petit causam, ne moveatur; sed hoc magis quaerendum est, an sit causa quae axem telluris semel libratum a sua directione valeat deflectere. Credo enim te tantum sapientiae authoris naturae tributurum ut, & sciverit & valuerit axem, qua commodissimum erat, dirigere. Nunc itaque cogita axem tam mobilem, ut vel saltus muscae invertere eum a recta dispositione valeat, & motum per Zodiacum adde: & dicito mihi, motus hic quo niti debet, ut axem invertat? | Ereu. Only turn your mind [adverte] [to] why the moment [momentum] was added to the fictitious earth [telluri fictitiae]. Was it not for this, lest by the circles by which it is sustained it should be snatched along through their [p. 194] motions? Since therefore in the nature of things the earth [tellus] hangs in free air, and is not constrained [arctata] by external bonds, exempt from this danger it does not seek a cause [for it] not to be moved; but this rather is to be inquired, whether there be a cause which can deflect from its direction the axis of the earth [telluris] once balanced [libratum]. For I believe you will attribute so much wisdom to the author of nature that he both knew and was able to direct the axis where it was most fitting [commodissimum]. Now therefore think of the axis [as] so mobile that even the leap of a fly could turn it over [invertere] from [its] upright disposition, and add the motion through the Zodiac: and tell me, whither ought this motion to strive [niti], so as to turn over the axis? |
| Asphal. Abunde est dictum, Video enim quod quoniam motus aquae circumagentis tellurem est per conatum versus centrum, vel ad latera alvei aequaliter, idcirco nullam diversionem faciat: ad hoc enim ut diverteret, deberet a centro sic altior esse, ut terram super aliud gravitatis & molis medium libraret, nisi enim aliquid in tellure fixum intelligatur, quo impediatur recte moventis impetus, necesse erit totum axem eadem moveri & nusquam inclinari. Quare cum tumor aquae sit tantummodo in rotundum, & momentum eius versus centrum; nullatenus axem, & Polos habet immutare. | Asphal. It is abundantly said. For I see that, because the motion of the water driving round [circumagentis] the earth [tellurem] is by endeavour [conatum] toward the centre, or toward the sides of the channel [alvei] equally, for that reason it makes no diversion: for to this [end], that it might divert [it], it would have to be so much higher from the centre as to balance [libraret] the earth [terram] upon another middle [medium] of heaviness [gravitatis] and of bulk [molis]; for unless something fixed in the earth [tellure] be understood, by which the impetus of the [thing] moving straight [recte] be impeded, it will be necessary that the whole axis be moved the same way [eadem] and nowhere be inclined. Wherefore, since the swelling [tumor] of the water is only in the round [in rotundum], and its moment [momentum] toward the centre, in no way has it [the power] [habet] to change the axis and the Poles. |
| Ereu. Oh, tantum libeat tibi (Andabata) [p. 195] videre quantum instar in hac veritate sit? Quomodo noctium dierumque ratio, & anni varietas, & infinitae aliae clarissimae & iucundissimae varietates ex hac una consequantur, accuratius dispicere? Polliceor tibi non ens suppositicium, non liberum per extrinseca divinae determinationis actum, non caetera quae tanto tempore, studio, labore excoluisti, eam voluptatem tibi allatura quantam haec, etsi inferioris Indolis theoremata. | Ereu. Oh, if only it might please you (Andabata) [p. 195] to see how much worth [instar] there is in this truth? How the reckoning [ratio] of nights and days, and the variety of the year, and infinite other most clear and most delightful varieties follow from this one [truth], to discern [it] more accurately? I promise you [that] not supposititious being [ens suppositicium], not the free act of divine determination [known] through extrinsic [things] [per extrinseca], not the other [things] which with so much time, study, labour you have cultivated, will bring you that pleasure, as great as these [will], although theorems of an inferior Disposition [Indolis]. |
| Asphal. Tam paucis putas tam inolitam Andabatae consuetudinem eradicare? tuam potius cogitationem quam importunus tibi excussi, in medium confer, nisi enim me decepit, non leve aliquid sonabat. | Asphal. With so few [words] do you think to uproot so ingrown [inolitam] a habit [consuetudinem] of Andabata? Rather bring into the midst your thought, which I, untimely [importunus], shook out of you; for unless it deceived me, it was sounding something not light. |
| Ereu. Veniam precari habeo, ut videtis enim quandoque me a vobis attentior cogitatio divertit. Erat autem mens tum temporis in eo defixa, cur altera anni medietas aliquot diebus alteram excedit? Cum enim signa Zodiaci per aequinoctia divisa sint in partes prorsus aequas, invenitur sol ab Autumnali ad vernum pauciores dies impendere quam rursus a verno ad Autumnale. Et hic ipse effectus mihi testatur quae dicta sunt ex ipsa rerum natura deprompta esse. Cum enim sol ab Autumnali ad vernum Americanas partes recto spiculo insectetur, Eae autem aquam magis quam [p. 196] terram sapiant, maiores necesse est esse exhalationum moles, & quod sequitur, ventos ventisque cognatos aestus, aestuumque asseclas motus, & diurnum & annuum. Si itaque velocior est diurnus motus, necesse est tristes Hyemis horas Dei benevolentia esse breviores, & ipsam Hyemem, quod agnoscunt Astronomi, & aestus Septembres nominatiores Martiis, quod nautae quoque calculis suis firmant. Adiice in aquosis Zonae torridae plagis, Hyemem tunc esse, cum sol a vertice fulminat, eamque intemperie, & pluviis, & inundationibus componi; unde si quid flumina possunt, torrentius erit per nostram Hyemem mare. [Margin: Cur una anni medietas brevior est reliqua.] | Ereu. I have to beg pardon; for, as you see, sometimes a more attentive thought diverts me from you. Now [my] mind was at that time fixed on this: why one half [medietas] of the year exceeds the other by some days? For since the signs of the Zodiac are divided by the equinoxes into parts altogether equal, the sun is found to spend fewer days from the Autumnal to the vernal [equinox] than again from the vernal to the Autumnal. And this very effect testifies to me that the things which have been said were drawn forth [deprompta] from the very nature of things. For since the sun from the Autumnal to the vernal pursues the American parts with a straight dart [recto spiculo], and these savour [sapiant] of water more than [p. 196] of earth [terram], it is necessary that the bulks [moles] of exhalations be greater, and, what follows, the winds and the tides [aestus] akin to the winds, and the motions attendant [asseclas] on the tides, both the diurnal and the annual. If therefore the diurnal motion is swifter, it is necessary that the sad hours of Winter be by the benevolence of God shorter, and Winter itself [shorter], which Astronomers acknowledge, and the September tides [aestus] more noted [nominatiores] than the March ones, which sailors also confirm by their reckonings [calculis]. Add [that] in the watery regions [plagis] of the torrid Zone it is Winter then, when the sun flashes [fulminat] from the zenith [a vertice], and that it is made up [componi] of bad weather [intemperie], and rains, and floods; whence, if rivers can [do] anything, the sea will be more torrent-like [torrentius] during our Winter. [Margin: Why one half of the year is shorter than the rest.] |
| Asphal. Non temere conieceram, hoc enim est intactum illud Astronomis miraculum, quantum olim Philosophis inundantis Nili caput. Sed quid est illud, Ereuni, quod de Aequinoctiorum praeversione & solstitiorum inaequali declinatione narrant Astronomi, & Gilbertus noster ex magnetica telluris natura ostendere conatur | Asphal. Not rashly had I conjectured; for this is that marvel untouched by Astronomers, as great as once to Philosophers [was] the head [caput] of the flooding Nile. But what is that, Ereunius, which Astronomers relate about the outrunning [praeversione] of the Equinoxes and the unequal declination of the solstices, and [which] our Gilbert tries to show from the magnetic nature of the earth [telluris] |

## Words and phrases with alternative or special meanings

- ***propter (Zodiacum)*** — rendered “along”. prep. 'near, alongside' (old local sense of propter = prope), not causal 'on account of'. The earth's annual course runs beside/under the Zodiac; causal 'because of' would misstate White's point.
- ***impos sum*** — rendered “I am unable [impos sum]”. powerless, not master of; elliptical (supply 'to see/explain'). Rare, archaic (Plautine) adjective; Asphalius' mock-helplessness.
- ***concausa*** — rendered “joint-cause [concausa]”. co-cause, concurrent cause. Scholastic: a cause cooperating with another to one effect; Asphalius thinks parallelism of the axis needs an extra cause (cf. Copernicus' 'third motion', declinationis).
- ***Lemniscus*** — rendered “pendant [Lemniscus]”. ribbon, pendant band (classical: ribbon hanging from a garland); here a weight/bob hung on the model globe's pole. Refers to the counterweight in the demonstration globe ('sphaera recta').
- ***momentum (telluri fictitiae additum)*** — rendered “moment [momentum]”. weight, counterpoise, inclining force. Physical sense as in D1-N16; the model earth needs a weight because its supporting circles would drag it; the real earth hangs free.
- ***quievi*** — rendered “I have rested [quievi]”. perfect of quiesco: 'I am satisfied / I shall rest content'. Perfect used of an assured future consequence ('and I am done').
- ***libratum / libraret*** — rendered “balanced”. poised, weighed, held in equilibrium. Gilbertian/Galilean language of a freely poised globe; parallelism needs no cause, only absence of a deflecting one (inertial conservation of axis direction).
- ***instar*** — rendered “worth [instar]”. equivalent, weight, value ('the like of'). Normally a noun 'likeness/equal'; here 'how much value is in this truth'.
- ***ens suppositicium*** — rendered “supposititious being”. a being merely supposed/assumed; a counterfeit entity. Jibe at Andabata's scholastic metaphysics (e.g. entia rationis, imaginary entities).
- ***per extrinseca divinae determinationis actum*** — rendered “the free act of divine determination [known] through extrinsic [things]”. or: 'the act, free through extrinsic [terms], of divine determination' — liberum per extrinseca could mean God's act is free only through extrinsic denomination. Scholastic debate on divine freedom: God's free decree differs only by extrinsic terms (Jesuit/Thomist controversy de scientia media / determinatione).
- ***Indoles (inferioris Indolis theoremata)*** — rendered “Disposition [Indolis]”. nature, rank, character. Physical theorems as of a 'lower nature' than metaphysics in the scholastic hierarchy of sciences.
- ***recto spiculo*** — rendered “with a straight dart”. vertical ray (spiculum = arrow-point, a ray of the sun). Sun overhead in the American tropics during the southern-hemisphere half-year.
- ***sapiant (aquam magis quam terram)*** — rendered “savour [sapiant] of water”. have the nature of, partake of. The American (Pacific) parts are mostly water, hence more exhalations, winds, tides, motion.
- ***asseclas*** — rendered “attendant [asseclas]”. followers, hangers-on. Causal chain: sun → exhalations → winds → tides → motions of the earth (White's tidal theory of terrestrial motion).
- ***nominatiores*** — rendered “more noted [nominatiores]”. more renowned, more remarkable (comparative of nominatus). The equinoctial September tides being stronger than the March ones.
- ***a vertice fulminat*** — rendered “flashes from the zenith”. vertex = zenith (or crown of the head); fulmino = lighten, blaze. The rainy 'winter' of the tropics coincides with the sun overhead.
- ***praeversio (Aequinoctiorum)*** — rendered “outrunning [praeversione]”. anticipation, preceding: i.e. the precession of the equinoxes. Cf. 'praeventio' in the title of knot vii; kept distinct by bracketing.
- ***Gilbertus noster*** — rendered “our Gilbert”. William Gilbert, De magnete (1600), bk VI, explained precession magnetically. 'noster' = our countryman (English), as with 'Gentilis' earlier.

## Translator's notes

- Unit: Dialogue II, Knot vi (complete). PDF pp. 203–207 = printed pp. 192–196, from ‘Asphal. Sic me uno absolvis dubio’ (p. 192, beside the marginal ‘Nodus VI. Cur tellus axem parallelum axi Aequatoris teneat?’) to Asphalius' question ‘…ex magnetica telluris natura ostendere conatur’ (p. 196). Knot vii (‘Unde sit praeventio Aequinoctiorum’) begins on p. 196 with ‘Ereu. Ardua est haec difficultas, Asphali’, beside its marginal title; Asphalius' introductory question is kept with knot vi because it precedes the marginal.
- Offset +11 holds (running heads 192–196; signatures Bb p. 193, Bb ij p. 195).
- Marginal notes: p. 192 knot title (placed at the end of Asphalius' speech it stands beside); p. 195 ‘Cur una anni medietas brevior est reliqua’ (beside Ereunius' ‘Veniam precari habeo…’), placed at the end of that speech.
- Uncertain readings / interpretations: (a) p. 196 ‘Si itaque velocior est diurnus motus’ — the argument (shorter winter half-year) seems to need the annual motion to be swifter; the print has ‘diurnus’, translated as printed (White may hold that a quicker diurnal motion, caused by stronger tides, drives the annual course). (b) p. 196 ‘& ęstus Septembres nominatiores Martiis’ — e-caudata read as aestus (‘tides’); the S of ‘Septembres’ is broken in the scan. (c) p. 195 ‘liberum per extrinseca divinae determinationis actum’ — construal uncertain (see terms list). (d) p. 192 ‘utrisque Aequatoris directionem communem esse’ — utrisque (plural ‘to both’) taken as the earth's equator and the celestial Equator sharing a direction; could also be ‘both poles’. (e) p. 194 ‘eadem moveri’ read as the adverb eadem (‘the same way’). (f) p. 196 Asphalius' final question has no closing punctuation in the print; kept. (g) p. 194 ‘Oh, tantum libeat tibi … videre … ? … dispicere?’ — printed with question marks though it is a wish; kept.
- Verification pass (sentence by sentence against the Latin and the page images, with 300-dpi crops of pp. 193, 195, 196): every sentence checked for omissions, additions, case, tense, mood, agreement, negatives and comparatives; 1 correction — p. 196 ‘& ipsam Hyemem’ depends on ‘esse breviores’, so ‘[shorter]’ was supplied (‘and Winter itself [shorter]’) to make clear it is the shortness of the winter half-year that Astronomers acknowledge. Transcription re-checked against the images: no changes. Readings still uncertain are listed above.
- Conventions: as in glossary.md — long s → s, ligatures and ę expanded, accents dropped, consonantal u → v; spellings kept (authoris, Hyemem, necessum, suppositicium, caetera); supplied English words in [square brackets]; Latin of technical or ambiguous words in [brackets]; printed page changes marked [p. n]; tellus/terra distinguished by bracketing.
