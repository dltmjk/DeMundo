*English title:* Knot II. That the wind in the Torrid Zone is stirred up by the sun (Table: That the wind which holds sway in the Western Ocean is from the sun)

## Dialogue II, Knot ii (printed pp. 133–144)

| Latin | English |
|---|---|
| [p. 133] [Margin: Nodus II. Ventum in Zona torrida a sole excitari.] Ereu. A me petis, quod ego a te debueram, quid scilicet existimes in hoc globo aquae et terrae coniunctione conflato, hanc solis et luminis continuam circumvolutionem operari? | [p. 133] [Margin: Knot II. That the wind in the Torrid Zone is stirred up [excitari] by the sun.] Ereu. You seek from me what I had owed [debueram] [to seek] from you, namely what you judge this continual circumvolution of the sun and of the light to work [operari] in this globe, blown together [conflato] by the conjunction of water and earth? |
| [p. 134] Andab. Perspicuum est vapores seu exhalationes quae in nubes et ventos coguntur et difflantur, ex hoc solis itinere excitari. | [p. 134] Andab. It is clear that the vapours or exhalations which are gathered [coguntur] and blown apart [difflantur] into clouds and winds, are stirred up from this journey of the sun. |
| Ereu. His demum vaporibus et exhalationibus decedente sole quid fit? | Ereu. With these vapours and exhalations, then [demum], the sun departing, what happens? |
| Andab. Refrigescunt et iterum descendunt in terram. | Andab. They grow cold and again descend onto the earth [terram]. |
| Ereu. Dum itaque calent, leviores sunt aere terram obsidente, postquam vero refrixerunt, eodem graviores. | Ereu. While therefore they are warm, they are lighter than the air besetting [obsidente] the earth; but after they have grown cold, heavier than the same. |
| Andab. Sic est. | Andab. So it is. |
| Ereu. Modo sol cum ex una parte discedit, quid agit? | Ereu. Now the sun, when it departs from one part, what does it do? |
| Andab. Tantumdem in proxima parte agit: vapores et exhalamina excitat et elevat. | Andab. Just as much it does in the next part: it stirs up and raises vapours and exhalations [exhalamina]. |
| Ereu. Non potest excitare vapores, quin aliud in locum ipsorum succedat; quid ergo credis in eorum locum succedere? | Ereu. It cannot stir up vapours without something else succeeding into their place [locum]; what then do you believe succeeds into their place? |
| Andab. Aerem qui necessario expellitur, ex eo loco in quem elevantur vapores. | Andab. The air, which is necessarily expelled from that place into which the vapours are raised. |
| Ereu. Quid si aliud aliquod corpus maiori nixu in illum locum feratur? cedetne aeri pulso, vel aer illi cessurus est? | Ereu. What if some other body be borne into that place with greater thrust [nixu]? Will it yield to the air driven [pulso], or is the air going to yield to it? |
| Andab. Si tale corpus urgeat, cessurus est aer, et per circulum in ipsius corporis locum successurus: sed unde tale corpus? ex suppositis enim nihil inferri potest, siquidem ni[p. 135]hil praeter solis cursum, inter postulata reposuimus. | Andab. If such a body press, the air is going to yield, and by a circle [per circulum] to succeed into the place of the body itself: but whence such a body? For from the things supposed [suppositis] nothing can be inferred, since [p. 135] nothing besides the course of the sun have we laid up among the postulates [postulata]. |
| Ereu. Inspiciamus itaque conclusa. Non dixisti in priori passu solis excitatos vapores, et (quod consequens est) occupatum eorumdem locum? Eos vero postmodum condensatos graviores aere fieri, et per consequens impetu descendere; quare excutient undique vi aerem, in cuius locum decidunt. Vide itaque num non sic impulsus aer fortior sit descendente, ex ascensione atomorum sensim sine sensu elevatorum. | Ereu. Let us look into, therefore, the things concluded [conclusa]. Did you not say that at the earlier step [passu] vapours were stirred up by the sun, and (what is consequent) the place of the same occupied? But that they afterwards, condensed, become heavier than the air, and in consequence descend with impetus [impetu]; wherefore they will shake out [excutient] by force on every side the air into whose place they fall down. See therefore whether the air thus impelled be not stronger, [the vapour] descending, [than] from the ascent of atoms raised little by little without sense [sensim sine sensu]. |
| Andab. Video hoc contingere, si quod linteum vel aliud grave expansum in terram decidat, ad regulam magnitudinis et velocitatis aerem concutientis, ventum excitari: et credam hoc ipsum in nubibus si quando velociter decidant, facile contingere. | Andab. I see this to happen: if some linen cloth or other heavy [thing] spread out fall down onto the earth, a wind is stirred up, according to the rule [ad regulam] of the magnitude and speed of [the thing] shaking the air: and I will believe this very [thing] easily to happen in the clouds, if at any time they fall down swiftly. |
| Ereu. Adiice et hoc solem in latitudinem aliquam, et non in solum punctum agere: quare neque in instanti abivisse, sed recedendo prius minus et minus elevare vaporum, deinde elevatos non aeque sustinere, donec tandem decidant. Quare ipsimet descendentes vapores facilius solem versus descendent, quam ad perpendiculum; quoniam tenuior illic aer et (quod sequitur) minor est illa resistentia. Devolventur itaque super frigidiore inferius aere, in aerem vicinum calidiorem [p. 136] quasi per declive, vel (sicut de quibusdam aquis audimus, et in aqua et vino licet experiri) sustinet inferior crassior aer adhuc egelidos, donec ad aerem tepidiorem et ex solis exustione leviorem perveniant. | Ereu. Add this too, that the sun acts upon some breadth [latitudinem], and not upon a sole point: wherefore [that] neither has it gone away in an instant, but in receding [it] first raises less and less of vapours, then does not equally sustain [those] raised, until at length they fall down. Wherefore the descending vapours themselves will descend more easily towards the sun than to the perpendicular; since there the air [is] thinner and (what follows) that resistance is less. They will roll down therefore over the colder air below, into the neighbouring warmer air, [p. 136] as it were down a slope [per declive]; or (as we hear of certain waters, and it is permitted to experience [experiri] in water and wine) the lower, thicker air sustains [them] still chilly [egelidos], until they come to air more tepid and, from the burning-out [exustione] of the sun, lighter. |
| Andab. Hoc agis, Ereuni, ut fatear tibi ventum sequi cursum solis ab Oriente in Occidentem: Ast ego tibi hoc absque tantis ambagibus concessissem. Est enim doctrina summorum Philosophorum ex motu raptus coelorum talem ventum excitari, circumducto aere. | Andab. You are doing [agis] this, Ereunius, that I may confess to you that the wind follows the course of the sun from the East into the West: but I would have granted you this without such great windings [ambagibus]. For it is the doctrine of the highest Philosophers that such a wind is stirred up from the motion of rapture [raptus] of the heavens, the air being led around. |
| Ereu. Ast ego has ambages volui, ut deinceps scias, neque credas magnis his Philosophis quae fide non sunt digna. Vide enim magni Galilaei, cum otium tibi fuerit, scriptum contra Libram Philosophicam, et invenies evidenter conclusum, non nisi exiguum aliquem limbum sublimis foci (si quis est) a concavo lunae circumagi posse, si quod tale foret. Nunc vero postquam hoc etiam constat, nullos esse Planetarum orbes, quibus Astrorum disci, tanquam clavi defixi sint, etiam de raptu illo tam violento et iniusto nihil nostris elementis timendum manet. Age modo effecta venti huius investigemus, inter quae scio te merito aestimare communionem, quam cum Occidente novo hoc et superiori saeculo potimur. | Ereu. But I wanted these windings, that you may know henceforth, and not believe these great Philosophers [in things] which are not worthy of credit [fide]. For see, when you have leisure, the writing of the great Galileo against the Philosophical Balance [Libram Philosophicam], and you will find [it] evidently concluded that only some slight border [limbum] of the lofty fire [foci] (if there is any) can be carried round by the concave of the moon, if any such thing should be. But now, after this too is established, that there are no orbs [orbes] of the Planets in which the discs of the Stars are fixed down like nails, there remains also nothing to be feared for our elements from that so violent and unjust rapture [raptu]. Come now, let us investigate the effects [effecta] of this wind, among which I know you rightly esteem the communion which we enjoy [potimur] with the new West in this and the former age [saeculo]. |
| [p. 137] [Margin: Non eum nasci ex motu telluris.] Asphal. Volens an invitus praetermittis Galilaeum qui hunc ventum a volubilitate telluris nasci credit, et nescio quid etiam in mari Mediterraneo similis effecti suspicatur? | [p. 137] [Margin: That it is not born from the motion of the earth [telluris].] Asphal. Willing or unwilling do you pass over Galileo, who believes this wind to be born from the volubility [volubilitate] of the earth [telluris], and suspects I know not what of a similar effect also in the Mediterranean sea? |
| Ereu. Libens omittebam, quid enim necesse erat commemorare, cum veritas statuta sit. Quoniam autem nolo tibi aliquam suspicionem manere, non video quomodo cum aliis de volutatione terrae effatis, recte cohaereat haec sententia: Nihil enim in repellendis quae opponuntur, efficacius affirmari et demonstrari solet, quam communes motus non sentiri; si autem a motu telluris ortum habet hic ventus, ille erit aeris motus, quem communem et non sensu perceptibilem dicunt. Quomodo ergo nunc undas agit? Naves propellit? homines afflat et reficit? | Ereu. Gladly [libens] was I omitting [him], for what need was there to make mention, since the truth has been settled [statuta]? But since I do not wish any suspicion to remain to you, I do not see how this opinion [sententia] rightly coheres with the other pronouncements [effatis] about the rolling [volutatione] of the earth [terrae]: for nothing is wont to be more efficaciously affirmed and demonstrated in repelling the things which are opposed, than that common motions are not sensed; but if this wind has [its] rise from the motion of the earth [telluris], it will be that motion of the air which they call common and not perceptible by sense. How then does it now drive the waves? Propel ships? Breathe upon [afflat] and refresh men? |
| Rursus vel fortius ipsa tellure agitur, vel aequaliter, vel relinquitur. Si fortius, quomodo a tardiore efficitur? Si aequaliter, quomodo propellit? si relinquitur, euntibus pariter et redeuntibus erit infestus. Cum terra enim ingressis, quia tellus praeoccupat tardiores ipsius partes, in faciem necesse est occurrat: Contra vero terrae nitentibus suo fluxu obviabit. Utroque itaque modo itinerantibus infestus occurret. | Again, either it is driven more strongly than the earth [tellure] itself, or equally, or it is left behind. If more strongly, how is it effected by a slower [thing]? If equally, how does it propel? If it is left behind, it will be hostile [infestus] alike to those going and to those returning. For to those who have set in with the earth [terra], because the earth [tellus] pre-occupies [praeoccupat] its slower parts, it must needs meet [them] in the face: but to those striving against the earth [terrae] it will come in the way with its flux [fluxu]. In either mode therefore it will meet travellers as hostile. |
| Neque suspicio de Mediterraneo me quicquam terret, debuerat enim itinerum Alexandriam versus et aversum notavisse menses Sacratus, [p. 138] et ventos solemnes, ut iudicium inde ferret; ex his enim potest expeditioris viae ratio pendere. Denique an hic noster ventus quicquam in Mediterraneo habeat momenti quis novit? Adiice ex Euxino et Propontide continuum in Oceanum defluxum, qui non leviter conducere potest. Unde etiam quae partes itineris constantiori velocitate transmeari soleant, notandum fuit. Nunc a te desidero, Andabata, ut exponas quantum in illo immani aequore valere credas hunc ventum? | Nor does the suspicion about the Mediterranean frighten me at all; for Sacratus [= Sagredo?] ought to have noted the months of the journeys towards Alexandria and away [from it], [p. 138] and the customary [solemnes] winds, that he might pass judgement from that; for from these the reason [ratio] of the speedier [expeditioris] way can depend. Lastly, whether this wind of ours has any moment [momenti] in the Mediterranean, who has known? Add the continual flowing-down [defluxum] from the Euxine and the Propontis into the Ocean, which can conduce not lightly. Whence also it was to be noted which parts of the journey are wont to be passed over with more constant speed. Now I desire of you, Andabata, that you set out how much you believe this wind avails [valere] in that immense plain of sea [aequore]? |
| [Margin: Vis dicti venti.] Andab. Parum profecto vel nihil, ut qui tam mitis et placidus sit, ut nullum omnino motum ciere, nullos fluctus elevare possit. | [Margin: The force of the said wind.] Andab. Little, assuredly, or nothing, as [being one] which is so mild and placid that it can rouse no motion at all, raise no billows. |
| Ereu. Sic censes? cogita igitur naves illas quas Hispani Galeones appellant, quantae sint, et quam immani pondere: et has tamen vides ab hoc quem contemptui habes, ventulo moveri. Sed et hoc vide, non nisi incidentem velis ventum ipsam navim impellere. Et qua vi? cum quinquaginta leucas, i. nisi fallor ducentos mille passus 24. horis promoveat, i. horis singulis octona passuum millia, feruntur siquidem Hispani quatuor passuum millia in marinam leucam conicere. Tum demum mente percipe tanta Pristis tot millia passuum una hora secans quantum aquarum sulcum [p. 139] prae se agat, et vide quam aestimationem de vento deinde facies? | Ereu. So you think? Consider then those ships which the Spaniards call Galleons, how great they are, and of how immense a weight: and yet you see these moved by this little wind [ventulo] which you hold in contempt. But see this too, that only the wind falling on [incidentem] the sails drives the ship itself. And with what force? Since it advances [it] fifty leagues, i.e., unless I am mistaken, two hundred thousand paces, in 24 hours, i.e., eight thousand paces each in single hours; for the Spaniards are said to throw [conicere] four thousand paces into a sea league. Then at length perceive in mind how great a furrow of waters so great a Pristis [Pristis], cutting so many thousands of paces in one hour, [p. 139] drives before itself, and see what estimation you will make thereafter of the wind? |
| Andab. Est ut dicis: sed quare tum adeo placidum est mare, ut domicellarum et pacificum appelletur, cum tam ferocem dominum patiatur. | Andab. It is as you say: but why then is the sea so placid that it is called [the sea] of the little ladies [domicellarum] and pacific [pacificum], when it suffers so fierce a lord? |
| Ereu. Nonne vides iratum, si irato impingat, magnas exardescere lites? Idem si molli, et obsecundanti incumbat, summa pace consistunt omnia, sic et hic accidit: quoniam immane est mare nullis littoribus, nullis aliis ventis obluctantibus obnoxium, ideo summa quiete uni vento obedit. Neque tumescere multum fluctus, neque reiici apparent. Accedit venti ipsius constantia et lenitas, quae ad maximos effectus, non animadverso conatu, possunt. | Ereu. Do you not see that if an angry [man] dashes against an angry [one], great strifes blaze up? [That] the same, if he lean upon a soft and compliant [one], all things stand together [consistunt] in the highest peace; so too it happens here: since the sea is immense, liable [obnoxium] to no shores, to no other winds struggling against [it], therefore it obeys the one wind with the highest quiet. Neither do the billows appear to swell much, nor to be thrown back. There is added the constancy and gentleness of the wind itself, which avail [possunt] to the greatest effects, the endeavour [conatu] not being noticed. |
| Sed postquam vim venti aliquatenus examinavimus, latitudinem et longitudinem plagae in qua dominatur, exquiramus. Et ne memoriae fidentes facile erremus, ab Asphalio petamus chartam (ut appellant) quae nos quod volumus doceat, qualis et haec docti sit positura Dei. | But after we have to some extent examined the force of the wind, let us search out the breadth [latitudinem] and length [longitudinem] of the tract [plagae] in which it holds sway. And lest, trusting to memory, we easily err, let us ask from Asphalius a chart [chartam] (as they call [it]) which may teach us what we want: of what sort also this placing [positura] of the learned God may be. |
| [Margin: Magnitudo aequoris quod perflat.] Asphal. Ecce vobis in mensa illa Mercatoris Atlantem, ubi quamcumque quaeritis, invenietis. | [Margin: The magnitude of the sea-plain [aequoris] which it blows through.] Asphal. Behold for you on that table the Atlas of Mercator, where whichever [chart] you seek, you will find. |
| Ereu. Primam ipsam evolve, Andabata, et dic quid invenias. | Ereu. Unroll [evolve] the first itself, Andabata, and say what you find. |
| Andab. Invenio sub aequatore nihil fere [p. 140] praeter mare. Ab Africae littore circa gradum octuagesimum Aequatoris, ad gradum trecentesimum, perpetuum mare cerno, et alios adhuc plusquam quinquaginta gradus, inter Americae et Africae littora interceptos, ut si Aequatorem consulamus, non amplius septuaginta gradus ex trecentis sexaginta, terrestres habeamus, cetera sint Neptuni. | Andab. I find under the equator almost nothing [p. 140] besides sea. From the shore of Africa, about the eightieth degree of the Equator, to the three-hundredth degree, I discern perpetual sea, and other more than fifty degrees besides, intercepted between the shores of America and of Africa; so that, if we consult the Equator, we have not more than seventy degrees out of three hundred and sixty [that are] terrestrial; the rest are Neptune's. |
| Ereu. Latitudinem quoque nota. | Ereu. Note the breadth [latitudinem] too. |
| Andab. Ea est fere ubique quanta Zonae torridae, nisi ubi Africa obstat, per viginti circiter gradus ex una Aequatoris parte. Reliqua torrida non videt nisi coelum undique et undique Pontum, si mare indicum frequentioribus Insulis non foret perspersum. | Andab. It is almost everywhere as great as [that] of the Torrid Zone, except where Africa stands in the way, through about twenty degrees on one side of the Equator. The rest of the torrid [zone] sees nothing but heaven on every side and on every side Sea [Pontum], were the Indian sea not besprinkled with rather frequent Islands. |
| Ereu. Computa modo quantam aquae molem in Atlanticum Oceanum sit exoneraturus. | Ereu. Compute now how great a bulk [molem] of water it is going to unload into the Atlantic Ocean. |
| Andab. Ad calculos hos non nimium assuevi, et sine atramento et calamo nihil prorsus valeo. | Andab. To these reckonings [calculos] I have not over-much grown accustomed, and without ink and pen I am worth [valeo] nothing at all. |
| Ereu. Neque ego accuratus sum. Verum neque tantam diligentiam postulo; sed ut pro nostra libertate aliquid propinqui coniectes. | Ereu. Nor am I exact [accuratus]. But neither do I demand such great diligence; but that, according to our liberty [pro nostra libertate], you conjecture something near. |
| [Margin: Vis eiusdem ex continuitate.] Andab. Cum vim venti attendo et latitudinem campi in quem agit, video ingentem esse. | [Margin: The force of the same from continuity.] Andab. When I attend to the force of the wind and the breadth of the field upon which it acts, I see it is huge. |
| [p. 141] Ereu. Ego vero incredibilem aio. Versa enim tecum longitudinem aequoris quod nusquam malaciam patitur. Vide vim venti in stagnum brevissimum, in lacunas: compone ut aquam aquae, sic fluctus fluctibus. Vide ingentem perpetuitatis vim; vide multitudinis minimorum ubi congesta est, invictam potentiam: Quanti agri a muribus, quanti acervi formicatim populentur? | [p. 141] Ereu. But I say [it is] incredible. For turn over with yourself the length of the sea-plain [aequoris] which nowhere suffers a calm [malaciam]. See the force of the wind upon a very short pool [stagnum], upon puddles [lacunas]: compare [compone], as water to water, so billows to billows. See the huge force of perpetuity; see the unconquered power of a multitude of the least [things] where it is heaped together: how great fields by mice, how great heaps are laid waste [populentur] ant-fashion [formicatim]? |
| Ventos quoque robustissimos quos horremus, quantum in naves huiusmodi magis possint, in quantam altitudinem maria tollant, quomodo gemebundos aggeres flagellent, dirumpant, pessundent. Tandem longitudinem, latitudinem, continuitatem expende et compara. Nunc Andabata, cogita quem motum in Oceano Indico datura sit tanta aquarum moles superinducta? | [See] also the most robust winds which we dread: how much more they can [do] against ships of this kind, to how great a height they lift the seas, how they lash, burst asunder, [and] send to the bottom [pessundent] the groaning embankments [aggeres]. Finally weigh out [expende] and compare length, breadth, continuity. Now, Andabata, consider what motion so great a bulk of waters, brought in over [superinducta], is going to give in the Indian Ocean? |
| [Margin: Mare velocius vento moveri.] Asphal. Nihil agis nisi velocitatem etiam nobis expedieris, quid enim refert quanta sit aquae moles, si temporis ratio immanis quoque permissa fuerit? | [Margin: That the sea is moved more swiftly than the wind.] Asphal. You do nothing unless you shall have set out [expedieris] for us the speed too; for what does it matter how great the bulk of water is, if an immense reckoning [ratio] of time too shall have been allowed? |
| Ereu. Aequum postulas, modo ratio aliquo modo iusta iniri possit; Quid ergo arbitraris aquam an navim magis obedire vento? | Ereu. You demand [what is] fair, provided a reckoning [ratio] just in some mode can be entered into; what then do you judge: [that] the water or the ship obeys the wind more? |
| Asphal. Fortassis navim, video enim eam fluctus ciere, quod indicio est eam aquarum motum praevertere. Praeterea video ventum [p. 142] velis altissimis accipi et norunt Mechanici quo maior est circumferentia, eo minori vi cieri mobile, eadem citatione. Haec mihi opinabile faciunt navim velocius aqua concitari. | Asphal. Perhaps the ship; for I see it rouse billows, which is an indication that it outruns [praevertere] the motion of the waters. Besides, I see the wind [p. 142] is received by the highest sails, and the Mechanicians know that the greater the circumference is, by the less force is the mobile [mobile] roused, with the same velocity [citatione]. These things make it matter of opinion [opinabile] to me that the ship is set in motion more swiftly than the water. |
| Ereu. Neque infirmo quae dixisti, Asphali, neque prorsus assentior: nam et fluctus navium in puppim flagellantes memini sensisse, et tardiores esse quae magis onustae sint rates, experimenta cottidiana docent, ex eo, crediderim, quod inferior aqua resistat motui, etiam cum superior velocior est. Unde nasci possunt quas ante se agit ratis sive spumas sive undas, sed et ipsum roboreum navigii corpus ineptius est motui et mechanici quae eminus minantur, asserunt velocitate perdere quod viribus acquirunt. | Ereu. Neither do I weaken the things which you have said, Asphalius, nor do I altogether assent: for I both remember to have felt billows lashing into the stern [puppim] of ships, and daily experiments [experimenta] teach that the vessels [rates] which are more laden are slower — from this, I should believe, that the lower water resists the motion, even when the upper is swifter. Whence can be born the foams or waves which the vessel [ratis] drives before itself; but also the oaken body itself of the craft is more unfit for motion, and the mechanicians assert that [the engines] which threaten from afar [eminus] lose in speed what they acquire in forces. |
| Sed utcumque haec sese habeant, promitto tibi velocitatem, non dico naves, sed ipsosmet (quod mireris) ventos praevertentem; quam tamen et nautae confirment et ratio evidentem reddat. Libero enim mari nautae motu et quasi fremitu ipsius liquidi pavimenti procellae ingruentis praesensionem et augurium capiunt, et nisi vela raptim accommodent, subita fidem ruina stabiliunt. | But however these things hold themselves, I promise you a speed outrunning [praevertentem] — I do not say the ships, but (what you may wonder at) the winds themselves; which, however, both sailors may confirm and reason [ratio] may render evident. For on a free sea the sailors take, from the motion and as it were the roaring [fremitu] of the liquid pavement itself, a fore-sensing [praesensionem] and augury of the storm rushing on; and unless they adjust the sails in haste, they establish the credit [fidem] [of it] by a sudden ruin. |
| Nihil itaque opponi potest, quin undis velocius propagetur motus, quam in aere. Facit autem hoc stagni liquentis lentor: quo fit ut proximior motui pars remotiorem aegre re[p. 143]linquat: et quod sequitur, trahat eam secum, cum aeris subtilitas abrumpi magis idonea sit, et breviori proinde ambitu, concussionem consectatur; | Nothing therefore can be opposed [to prevent] motion being propagated more swiftly in the waves than in the air. And this the viscosity [lentor] of the liquid pool [stagni] makes: whence it comes about that the part nearer to the motion with difficulty [p. 143] leaves the more remote [part], and (what follows) draws it with itself; whereas the subtlety of the air is more apt to be broken off, and accordingly follows up the shock [concussionem] in a shorter compass [ambitu]; |
| Immisso itaque ad Herculeas columnas Euro, agitabitur Indicum mare voluminibus serpentinis undarum, priusquam plaudentes de coelo demissi nuncii alas senserit: et haec eadem celeritas subsequentes eiusdem anhelitus in effectu pari indefesse comitabitur. Verum quem effectum huius concitationis futurum vides? | the East wind [Euro], therefore, being sent in at the Herculean pillars, the Indian sea will be agitated with serpentine rollings [voluminibus] of waves before it shall have felt the clapping [plaudentes] wings of the messenger let down from heaven: and this same swiftness will accompany unwearyingly, in equal effect, the following breathings [anhelitus] of the same [wind]. But what effect of this setting-in-motion [concitationis] do you see will be? |
| Andab. Quem? nisi ut cum moles aquae ex pacifico eiecta in Indicum venerit, tantumdem inde expellat; et Indus rursum ex Atlantico; Atlanticus tandem reddat pacifico vicem: et sic perpes fiat aquarum ab Oriente in Occidentem volutatio. | Andab. What [effect]? Unless that, when the bulk of water thrown out from the pacific [sea] shall have come into the Indian, it expel just as much from there; and the Indian again out of the Atlantic; [and] the Atlantic at length render the turn [vicem] to the pacific: and so there become a perpetual [perpes] rolling [volutatio] of the waters from the East into the West. |
| [Margin: Cur superficies maris salsa est.] Ereu. Et, nisi fallor, versat ipsum Oceani fundum hic motus, et sequitur omnium marium commixtio. Et me movet praeter ingentem ipsam aquae evolutae massam quae superveniens descendendo, et sese miscet, et elevat eam cui miscetur, quod mare totum salsum experimur. | [Margin: Why the surface of the sea is salt.] Ereu. And, unless I am mistaken, this motion turns over [versat] the very bottom of the Ocean, and a mixing-together [commixtio] of all the seas follows. And there moves me — besides the huge mass itself of rolled-out [evolutae] water, which, coming on over [superveniens], by descending both mixes itself and raises that with which it is mixed — [the fact] that we experience the whole sea [to be] salt. |
| Dum enim aquam salsam graviorem dulci considero omnemque quae vel mare ipsum ingreditur per fluvios, vel exhalatur in pluvias, dulcem esse intueor, fit ut mirer dulcem non toto mari supernatare: et faceret, nisi haec iactatio versum funditus exci[p. 144]tantis aestum, totum misceret. Accedit quod ea quae minime hunc aestum patiuntur, dulciora sunt. | For while I consider salt water [to be] heavier than sweet, and behold all [water] which either enters the sea itself through rivers, or is exhaled into rains, to be sweet, it comes about that I wonder [that] the sweet does not swim on top over the whole sea: and it would do [so], unless this tossing [iactatio] of [a wind] stirring [p. 144] up from the bottom [funditus] a turned-over {?versum} surge [aestum] mixed the whole. There is added that those [seas] which least suffer this surge [aestum] are sweeter. |
| Modo itaque, Andabata, mecum discute quomodo et quibus gradibus haec fiant. Aqua igitur quae ex pacifico eiicitur quam pellit aquam vel potius in quam partem repellit Indicos vortices? | Now therefore, Andabata, discuss [discute] with me how and by what steps these things come about. The water then which is thrown out from the pacific — what water does it drive, or rather into what part does it drive back the Indian whirlpools [vortices]? |

## Words and phrases with alternative or special meanings

- ***Ventum in Zona torrida a sole excitari*** — rendered “That the wind in the Torrid Zone is stirred up by the sun”. Table of nodi gives a different title: ‘Ventum qui in Oceano Occidentali dominatur, esse a sole’ White's account of the easterly trade winds: produced by the sun's daily passage heating and releasing vapours, not by the earth's rotation (contra Galileo).
- ***debueram*** — rendered “I had owed [to seek]”. ‘I ought to have asked’; pluperfect of obligation Dialogue politeness formula: the question should have come from the other side.
- ***conflatus (globus)*** — rendered “blown together”. fused, compounded, formed of Terraqueous globe: earth and water as one globe, a Copernican-era commonplace.
- ***circumvolutio solis*** — rendered “circumvolution of the sun”. revolution; apparent daily course White keeps the neutral ‘phenomenal’ language agreed in Knot i (sun–earth permutation).
- ***vapores / exhalationes / exhalamina*** — rendered “vapours / exhalations / exhalations [exhalamina]”. moist vs. dry exhalations (Aristotle, Meteor. I.4, II.4) Aristotelian meteorology: wet vapour makes clouds/rain, dry exhalation makes winds.
- ***per circulum*** — rendered “by a circle”. circular displacement, antiperistasis Aristotle's antiperistasis / circular replacement (Phys. IV.8) used to explain wind without vacuum.
- ***postulata / supposita*** — rendered “postulates / things supposed”. premises, hypotheses White reasons quasi-geometrically from a single postulate (the sun's course).
- ***sensim sine sensu*** — rendered “little by little without sense”. imperceptibly Proverbial jingle (Cicero, De senectute 38).
- ***atomi*** — rendered “atoms”. minute particles Not Epicurean atomism in a strict sense; Digbean corpuscles of vapour.
- ***egelidus*** — rendered “chilly”. lukewarm (chill taken off) or cool Latin word with opposite senses; here the vapours are still cool from condensation.
- ***raptus (motus raptus coelorum)*** — rendered “rapture”. diurnal motion imparted by the primum mobile Peripatetic view that the first mover drags the lower spheres and the air east-to-west, causing the trade wind.
- ***Libra Philosophica*** — rendered “Philosophical Balance”. Orazio Grassi (‘Lotario Sarsi’), Libra astronomica ac philosophica (1619) Galileo's reply is Il Saggiatore (1623), which argues the moon's concave could not carry the sphere of fire round.
- ***limbus sublimis foci*** — rendered “border of the lofty fire”. edge of the elemental sphere of fire Aristotelian sphere of fire beneath the moon; ‘si quis est’ shows White's scepticism.
- ***orbes Planetarum / clavi defixi*** — rendered “orbs of the Planets / nails fixed down”. solid spheres carrying the stars Post-Tychonic rejection of solid orbs; rapture therefore loses its mechanism.
- ***volubilitas / volutatio telluris*** — rendered “volubility / rolling of the earth”. rotation Galileo, Dialogo, Day 4, derives the trade winds from the earth's rotation.
- ***motus communis*** — rendered “common motion”. motion shared by observer and object, hence imperceptible Galileo's key principle (Dialogo, Day 2); White turns it against Galileo's wind theory.
- ***Sacratus*** — rendered “Sacratus [= Sagredo?]”. probably Giovan Francesco Sagredo, Galileo's interlocutor (Latinised as ‘sacred’) In Dialogo Day 4 Sagredo reports that voyages from Venice to Syria are quicker than the return, as evidence for Galileo's theory.
- ***venti solemnes*** — rendered “customary winds”. regular, seasonal (etesian) winds Solemnis = recurring yearly; the Etesian winds of the eastern Mediterranean.
- ***Euxinus / Propontis*** — rendered “Euxine / Propontis”. Black Sea / Sea of Marmara Classical commonplace of the constant outflow from the Black Sea into the Mediterranean.
- ***Galeones*** — rendered “Galleons”. Spanish great ships The Manila galleons crossing the Pacific with the trade wind.
- ***leuca / passus*** — rendered “league / pace”. Spanish sea league = 4 Roman miles (4,000 paces) 50 leagues/day = 200,000 paces = c. 8,000 paces an hour.
- ***Pristis*** — rendered “Pristis”. sea-monster, whale; a ship's name Virgil, Aen. V.116, a ship named Pristis in the boat-race; the galleon as leviathan.
- ***mare domicellarum / pacificum*** — rendered “sea of the little ladies / pacific”. Spanish ‘Golfo de las Damas’ (trade-wind belt) and Magellan's ‘Mar Pacífico’ Sailors' names for the calm trade-wind seas.
- ***obnoxius*** — rendered “liable”. exposed, subject to Here: the open ocean is subject to no shores or contrary winds.
- ***charta*** — rendered “chart”. map, sea-chart ‘ut appellant’ marks the word as a modern technical term.
- ***qualis et haec docti sit positura Dei*** — rendered “of what sort also this placing of the learned God may be”. positura = arrangement, disposition An elegiac pentameter, apparently a quotation; source not identified.
- ***Mercatoris Atlas*** — rendered “the Atlas of Mercator”. Gerard Mercator's Atlas (1595, with later Hondius editions) The ‘first’ chart is the world map; degrees of longitude counted from a meridian west of Africa.
- ***coelum undique et undique Pontus*** — rendered “heaven on every side and on every side Sea”.  Virgil, Aen. III.193 (= V.9): ‘caelum undique et undique pontus’.
- ***malacia*** — rendered “calm”. Greek μαλακία, dead calm at sea Caesar, BG III.15; nautical term.
- ***formicatim*** — rendered “ant-fashion”. like ants Rare adverb (Plautine type).
- ***pessundare*** — rendered “send to the bottom”. pessum dare, ruin
- ***Mechanici / mobile / citatio*** — rendered “Mechanicians / mobile / velocity [citatione]”. practitioners of mechanics; the thing moved; speed of motion Law of the lever / wheel: larger circumference, less force for the same speed (pseudo-Aristotle, Mechanica).
- ***opinabilis*** — rendered “matter of opinion”. probable, not demonstrated Aristotelian contrast of opinion (doxa) and science.
- ***quae eminus minantur*** — rendered “[the engines] which threaten from afar”. missile engines (catapults, ballistae) Mechanical rule: what is gained in force is lost in speed.
- ***lentor*** — rendered “viscosity”. toughness, clinging White's account of why motion propagates faster in water than in air: water's cohesion carries the farther part with it.
- ***volumina (undarum)*** — rendered “rollings”. coils, swells cf. glossary ‘volumina (ignorantiae) = rolling floods’.
- ***nuncius plaudentes alas*** — rendered “clapping wings of the messenger”. the wind pictured as Mercury Virgil, Aen. IV.252 ff. (Mercury's descent); the wind as divine messenger.
- ***volutatio aquarum ab Oriente in Occidentem*** — rendered “rolling of the waters from the East into the West”. westward oceanic current White's general westward circulation, anticipating his account of tides in Knot iii.
- ***aestus*** — rendered “surge [aestum]”. tide, seething, agitation Here the general turbulence stirred by the wind rather than the tide proper (glossary: aestus maris = tide).
- ***vortices*** — rendered “whirlpools [vortices]”. eddies, vortices Not Cartesian vortices (Descartes' Principia 1644 is later).
