## Dialogue III, Knot xi (printed pp. 361–370)

| Latin | English |
|---|---|
| [Margin, p. 362: Nodus XI. Deum nullius mali esse causam.] | [Margin, p. 362: Knot XI. That God is the cause of no evil.] |
| Ereu. [continuing, p. 361] Verum me tam alienum ab hoc errore habe, ut nihil mali a prima causa ortum habere suspicari possim. Cum enim videam naturam ipsius esse eandem quae sit entis & existentiae; Ens autem & bonum converti: fit ut credam cum non Ente malum convenire, & vel ipsum esse vel de ipsius prosapia: Ens modo quod cum non Ente nihil habeat commune, non Entis causam ponere, est causae & effectus rationum esse immemorem. Certe tam dissimilem partum non agnosceret parens. | Ereu. [continuing, p. 361] But hold me so alien from this error, that I could suspect nothing of evil to have [its] rise from the first cause. For since I see his nature to be the same as [that] which is of being [entis] and existence; and Being [Ens] and good to be converted [converti]: it comes about that I believe evil to agree [convenire] with non-Being, and either to be it [ipsum] or of its lineage [prosapia]: to posit Being merely [modo], which has nothing common with non-Being, [as] cause of non-Being, is to be unmindful of the notions [rationum] of cause and effect. Certainly a parent would not acknowledge so dissimilar an offspring [partum]. |
| Andab. Belle concionaris, sed si immediatis consequentiarum nexibus, astrictus fueris, deficient te tam praeclare effata, negasne enim rerum distinctionem a Deo esse? quam si non negas, neque non ens ab ipso fluere posse inficiandum est. Si vero negare instes, memento quonam pacto intelligentem nobis Deum astruxeris. Quin etiam ipsam distinctionem in Deo collocasti, ut inde procedere quae ibi est, nullum sit incommodum. | Andab. Beautifully you harangue [concionaris], but if you shall have been bound by the immediate ties [nexibus] of consequences, so splendid pronouncements [effata] will fail you; for do you deny the distinction of things to be from God? which if you do not deny, neither is it to be denied [inficiandum] that non-being can flow from him. But if you insist on denying [it], remember in what manner you built up [astruxeris] for us an intelligent God. Nay moreover you placed the distinction itself in God, so that [for that] which is there to proceed thence is no inconvenience [incommodum]. |
| Ereu. Cum dicis rerum distinctionem a Deo sequi, intelligere velim quomodo apprehendas, an ipsam distinctionem, an distincta? & cum duas creaturarum quasi stationes agnoscamus, alteram in Deo, alteram in se: si de hac secunda loqueris, clarum mihi videtur distinctionem creaturarum priorem esse, Deum vero in hac esse tantummodo communicare, quod iuxta subiectorum diversitatem in uno magis quam in alio recipitur, & per hoc diversum fit. | Ereu. When you say the distinction of things follows from God, I would wish to understand how you apprehend [it]: whether the distinction itself, or the distinct [things]? And since we acknowledge two as it were stations [stationes] of creatures, the one in God, the other in themselves [in se]: if you speak of this second, it seems clear to me that the distinction of creatures is prior, but that God in this [station] only communicates being [esse], which according to the diversity of the subjects is received more in one than in another, and through this becomes diverse. |
| Si autem de priori loqueris, animadverte distinctionem sic in Deo esse, ut in eo vim distinctionis non habeat; sed plura sive omnia in summam simplicitatem adunet: & pro rata (ut loquuntur) in nostro intellectu tantumdem est cernere: qui etiam ipse quae inter se sunt incompossibilia, sic connectit, ut unum secum & inter se in ipso efficiant. Unde & distinctionem non a Deo, sed ab ipsis creatis esse, & Divinitati quantum in se est, repugnare, facile perspicitur. Quare si quid aliud paratum habes, profer. | But if you speak of the former, notice that distinction is in God thus, that in him it does not have the force of distinction, but unites [adunet] many or all [things] into the highest simplicity: and in proportion [pro rata] (as they say) it is [possible] to discern just as much in our intellect: which also itself so connects [things] which among themselves are incompossible, that they make one with itself and among themselves in it. Whence it is easily perceived that distinction too is not from God, but from the created [things] themselves, and is repugnant to Divinity, as much as is in it [quantum in se est]. Wherefore if you have anything else ready, bring [it] forth. |
| Andab. Hoc itaque cogita causam quae hanc vim habet ut faciat aliam facere, quicquid per aliam fit, ipsammet facere. Cum itaque hanc vim Deo negare neque possis, neque velis, necessum est ut quicquid mali creaturae patrant, in Deum recudatur. | Andab. Think therefore this: [that] a cause which has this force, that it makes another make, itself makes whatever is made through the other. Since therefore you can neither deny this force to God, nor wish [to], it is necessary [necessum est] that whatever of evil creatures perpetrate be struck back [recudatur] upon God. |
| [Margin, p. 363: Deum non esse causam mali quod creatura facit.] | [Margin, p. 363: That God is not the cause of the evil which the creature does.] |
| Ereu. Miraberis fortassis si collectionem tuam concedam, faciendum tamen est. Si enim quod coepi dicere, constanter persequar, (dixisse vero memini privationum de familia nasci malum) neque creaturam aliquid mali efficere posse fatendum est, non tam ex creaturae nobilitate, quam ex mali nequitia, quod cum ens non sit, neque effici possit. | Ereu. You will wonder perhaps if I should concede your inference [collectionem]; yet it must be done. For if I pursue constantly what I began to say (and I remember to have said that evil is born of the family of privations), it must be confessed that neither can the creature effect anything of evil, not so much from the nobility of the creature, as from the worthlessness [nequitia] of evil, which, since it is not a being [ens], cannot either be effected. |
| Efficienti enim causae praesupponitur esse perfectum, & quicquid ab efficiente progreditur, eatenus perfectum est. Malum itaque quatenus perfectioni opponitur, non ab efficiente, sed vel ex alia causa; vel si parem proli parentem quaeris, ex defectu efficientis seu non efficiente proseminandum est. Causa itaque quae faciet efficientem agere, quatenus efficiens & perfecta est, neque ullam influentiam in eandem habet; quatenus defectuosa est, non facit eam defectum & malitiam causare. | For to an efficient cause it is presupposed to be perfect, and whatever proceeds from the efficient is to that extent [eatenus] perfect. Evil therefore, insofar as it is opposed to perfection, must be sown forth [proseminandum] not from the efficient, but either from another cause; or, if you seek a parent equal to the offspring, from the defect of the efficient, or from a non-efficient. The cause therefore which will make the efficient act, insofar as it is efficient and perfect, neither has any influence on the same [eandem] — insofar as it is defective — [nor] does it make it cause [causare] the defect and malice [malitiam]. |
| Andab. Haec sine incommodo affirmarentur, si non obstaret celebre illud axioma ipso naturae lumine clarissimum, quod qui dat formam, dat consequentia ad formam, seu, quod tantumdem valet, quando duo per se connexa sunt, si quid causa unius duorum est, etiam reliqui causa ex necessitate fit: | Andab. These [things] would be affirmed without inconvenience, if that celebrated axiom, most clear by the very light of nature, did not stand in the way: that he who gives the form gives the consequents [consequentia] to the form; or, what is worth just as much, when two [things] are connected per se [per se], if something is cause of one of the two, it becomes of necessity cause of the remaining [one] too: |
| quare cum hoc notissimum sit, defectum seu negationem ex omni forma creata intrinsece sequi, & quodammodo ad ipsius rationem pertinere, vel, cum minimum dicitur, per se connexam esse omni enti creato, vel nihil Deus immediate neque mediate efficit, vel ex eius actione negatio sequetur, quae si possit a Deo esse, etiam aliqua mali ratio non repugnabit ab eo consequi, cum nihil deterius in privatione, vel malo fingi possit, quam quod in negatione reperitur. | wherefore, since this is most well known, that defect or negation follows intrinsically from every created form, and in a certain way pertains to its notion [rationem], or, when the least is said, is connected per se to every created being, either God effects nothing immediately nor mediately, or from his action negation will follow; which if it can be from God, it will not be repugnant either that some notion [ratio] of evil follow [consequi] from him, since nothing worse can be feigned in privation, or evil, than what is found in negation. |
| Ereu. Si de nostro dicendi modo & usu quaestio foret, facile assentirer, vocum in his translationem tam facilem esse, ut non modo nihil absurdi sit, si unum pro alio dicamus; sed ut vix aut ne vix quidem evitari possit; si vero de naturali vi sermo sit, affixionem eorum quae de altero dicuntur ad illud de quo dicuntur, video interdum in una quasi consequentium linea, seu ex ipsa consequentia fieri; alias ex coniunctione causarum in operando accidere. | Ereu. If the question were of our mode of speaking and usage, I would easily assent that the transference [translationem] of words in these [matters] is so easy that not only is there nothing of absurd if we say one for another, but that it can scarcely, or not even scarcely, be avoided; but if the discourse be of natural force, the affixing [affixionem] of those [things] which are said of another to that of which they are said, I see sometimes to come about in one, as it were, line of consequents [consequentium], or from the consequence [consequentia] itself; at other times to happen from the conjunction of causes in operating. |
| Unde si causae per se coniunctae sunt, etiam continget earum effecta in eo quod ab utroque procedit, etiam per se coniuncta esse: sic autem sese res habet; non enim ex eo quod est in causa positivum, nascitur, si quis est in effectu defectus, sed ex coniuncta negatione: quam si in sua linea usque ad primam causam derives; non in Deum, sed vel in materiam vel in aliam potentialitatem refundi invenies. Unde quantumvis Axiomata quae affers, quoad usum nostrae locutionis, non fallant, facilis tamen est ex iis ruina, si ad naturalem consequentiam eadem declines. | Whence, if the causes are joined per se, it will also come about that their effects, in that which proceeds from both, are also joined per se: but thus the thing stands [sic autem sese res habet]: for not from that which is positive in the cause is born the defect, if any is in the effect, but from the joined negation: which if you derive [derives] in its line up to the first cause, you will find [it] to be poured back [refundi] not into God, but either into matter or into another potentiality. Whence, however much the Axioms which you bring do not deceive as regards the usage of our locution, yet ruin from them is easy, if you turn [declines] the same to natural consequence. |
| Andab. Sed nulla est vel potentia vel potentialitas, quae non a Deo ortum & originem habeat: Tandem itaque in ipsum hae negationes referentur. | Andab. But there is no either power [potentia] or potentiality which does not have rise and origin from God: at length therefore these negations will be referred to him. |
| Ereu. Quomodo negationes in Deo sint & a Deo proficiscantur, satis tibi supra disputatum est, cum tam ferociter insilires; Non autem alio modo in potentiis quam in aliis a Deo procedunt. Unde nihil habeo quod de novo adiiciam. | Ereu. How negations are in God and proceed from God has been disputed enough for you above, when you were leaping in [insilires] so fiercely; and not in another way do they proceed from God in powers [potentiis] than in other [things]. Whence I have nothing which I may add anew. |
| Andab. Sed ego habeo: certe enim perspicuum est Deum caedes hominum & animalium suo ore praecepisse, & per Angelos hominesque effectui dedisse; non credo haec negabis a Deo profecta? | Andab. But I have: for certainly it is evident that God commanded the slaughters of men and animals with his own mouth, and gave [them] to effect [effectui dedisse] through Angels and men; I do not believe you will deny these [to have] proceeded from God? |
| Ereu. Theologica sunt illa, Andabata, a quibus me vel propter tenuitatem meam, vel propter familiaris huius discursus qualitatem, abhorrere iam dudum nosti. Reserva proinde Scholis hunc nodum. | Ereu. Those are Theological [matters], Andabata, from which you have long known me to shrink [abhorrere], either on account of my slenderness [tenuitatem] or on account of the quality of this familiar discourse. Reserve therefore for the Schools this knot [nodum]. |
| Ego enim cum iam professus fuerim, si usus verborum spectetur, recte dici malorum causas entia positiva, etiam ipsi Deitati sic attribui posse non dubitaverim: Si autem attribui possint, etiam Deum sibi ea attribuere quid vetat? Mandans autem quid aliud faciat quam testari voluntatem suam hanc esse, nescio. Unde mandare potest Deus, quicquid sibi attribuere potest, & attribuere sibi quicquid eidem licitum est nos fando largiri: sed non propterea naturalis & Physica eorum causa magis erit quam si nihil mandavisset. Verum me retraho: iam enim extra carceres elapsus eram. | For I, since I have already professed that, if the usage of words be regarded, positive beings [entia positiva] are rightly said [to be] causes of evils, would not doubt that [they] can so be attributed even to the Deity itself: but if they can be attributed, what forbids God also to attribute them to himself? But what else one commanding does than to testify his will to be this, I do not know. Whence God can command whatever he can attribute to himself, and attribute to himself whatever it is lawful for us to bestow [largiri] on him in speaking [fando]: but not on that account will he be the natural and Physical cause of them more than if he had commanded nothing. But I draw myself back: for I had already slipped outside the barriers [carceres]. |
| Andab. Non absimilia sunt quae dicis. Et iam per Asphalii veniam stabilitus est Deus, universi gubernator, non fato subiectus, sed fatorum Dominus ab ipsomet pendentium. Videor eum videre de sublimi mandantem naturae, & iura ipsius pro imperii sui ostensione modo hic, modo illic abrogantem; Quin & per lucentia illa Astra hominum mores fortunasque corporibus in utero materno inter ipsam coagulationem insinuante{?m}, {?mirabile!} tum semina iaci, non eorum tantum quae naturae infixa haerent, verum & illorum quae exterorum adminiculo post tot temporum spatia gerenda sunt. Unde, magne Deus, haec Astris virtus? Unde tam incredibilis potentia? | Andab. Not unlike are [the things] which you say. And now, by Asphalius’ leave, God has been established [as] governor of the universe, not subject to fate, but Lord of the fates hanging from himself. I seem to see him from on high commanding nature, and for the display of his empire now here, now there abrogating its laws; nay, and [him] insinuating, through those shining Stars, the manners and fortunes of men into bodies in the mother’s womb amid the very coagulation; — wonderful! — then [I seem to see] the seeds to be cast, not of those [things] only which cling implanted [infixa] in nature, but also of those which are to be carried out [gerenda] with the support of external [things] after so many spaces of times. Whence, great God, this virtue [virtus] to the Stars? Whence so incredible a power [potentia]? |
| [Margin, p. 367: Deum non immutare ordinem naturae.] | [Margin, p. 367: That God does not change [immutare] the order of nature.] |
| Ereu. Triumphas, Andabata, utinam & nos tecum (nisi tu trahas) impares tam sublimibus dogmatibus & tanta energia prolatis tantaque verborum copia ornatis. Neque enim credo Asphalium facile assensurum Deum ordinem naturae quem ipse instituit, invertere. Periti si quidem non est artificis tam defectilem machinae suae vim tribuisse, ut ad effecta quae propter nata est, novam artificis manum perpetuo desideraret; semel iura accepit, iis vivit; nisi superior aliqua vis & non expectatus finis attollat magis quam immutet leges prius positas. | Ereu. You triumph, Andabata; would that we too [might] with you (unless you drag [us]), [being] unequal to dogmas so sublime, and brought forth with such energy [energia], and adorned with such abundance of words. For I do not believe that Asphalius will easily assent that God overturns the order of nature which he himself instituted. Since indeed it is not [the part] of a skilled artificer to have attributed so deficient [defectilem] a force to his machine that, for the effects for which it was born, it should perpetually require a new hand of the artificer; once it has received laws, by them it lives; unless some superior force and an unexpected end lift up [attollat] rather than change the laws previously laid down. |
| Asphal. Et ego non male coniectare videor Ereunio nostro ingentem illam Astrorum, quam depraedicas, potentiam esse suspectam. | Asphal. And I seem to conjecture not badly that that huge power of the Stars, which you proclaim [depraedicas], is suspect to our Ereunius. |
| Andab. Ne timeas, Asphali. Rem tot saeculorum experimentis compertam, tanta ratione fundatam, tanta authoritate stabilitam, & (quod ipsi non leve est) a maximis Mathematicis non approbatam tantummodo; sed etiam libris demonstrative scriptis illustratam Ereunius negare non poterit: quae enim fundamenta? qui authores post haec supererunt quos sequatur, quibus innitatur? | Andab. Do not fear, Asphalius. A thing ascertained by the experiments [experimentis] of so many ages, founded on so great reason [ratione], established by so great authority, and (what is not light for him) not only approved by the greatest Mathematicians, but also illustrated in books written demonstratively, Ereunius will not be able to deny: for what foundations, what authors will remain after these, whom he may follow, on whom he may lean? |
| Ereu. Legistine, Andabata, Mathematicorum quos nominas libros? | Ereu. Have you read, Andabata, the books of the Mathematicians whom you name? |
| Andab. Cur interrogas? nosti enim me non nisi a te inductum omnino Mathesi operam dedisse: & nosti fere quae legerim universa. | Andab. Why do you ask? For you know that I have given attention to Mathesis [Mathesi] at all only [non nisi] when induced by you: and you know nearly all [the things] which I have read. |
| Ereu. Sic existimabam. Si enim vel semel oculo levissimo percurrisses, nunquam demonstrativos credidisses; sed hoc ex Authorum magno nomine commodavisti, quos nihil sine demonstratione afferre iurares. In his autem libris, nisi quid ex Astronomia mutuatum fuerit, nihil est quod demonstrationis umbram prae se ferat. Nihil fortioris persuasionis: nihil cum ratione pronunciatum, {?i.} ut non desinam mirari viros alioqui tam magnos sustinuisse tam putidum locum tractare. | Ereu. So I thought. For if you had run through [them] even once with the lightest eye, you would never have believed [them] demonstrative; but this you lent [commodavisti] from the great name of the Authors, whom you would swear to bring forward nothing without demonstration. But in these books, unless something has been borrowed from Astronomy, there is nothing which bears before itself a shadow of demonstration. Nothing of stronger persuasion; nothing pronounced with reason; so that I do not cease to wonder that men otherwise so great endured to treat so rotten [putidum] a topic [locum]. |
| Aliis vero viris gravibus & in aliis scientiis insigniter versatis, sicut & tibi condonandum video, si tantorum Magistrorum nomine seducti fuistis in aliena arte. Quamvis, quoniam haec ars etiam naturali scientiae commixta est, ex ea debilitatem huius disciplinae (dicam) vel circumforaneae potius nugacitatis perdiscere potuerunt, omni eos culpa liberare non possim. | But to other grave men, and [men] notably versed in other sciences, as also to you, I see [it] is to be pardoned, if you [pl.] have been seduced in an alien art by the name of so great Masters. Although, since this art is also mixed with natural science, [and] from it they were able to learn thoroughly the weakness of this discipline (shall I say [dicam]) or rather of mountebank [circumforaneae] triviality [nugacitatis], I could not free them from all blame. |
| Andab. Ἐπαῖόν τοι ἔπος φύγειν ἕρκος ὀδόντων [Greek printed thus; see notes]. Nescis fortassis viros optimos & insignes etiam hodie huic arti illaborare, quam tu plus vento plumave elevas, vel saxo potius molari magis deprimis. Si enim talis est qualem tu asseris, quomodo sibi patiuntur illudi docti? quomodo ignaris illudere patiuntur probi? Apage cum hac censura, Ereuni. | Andab. [Greek, Homeric:] “What a word has escaped the fence of [your] teeth!” You do not know perhaps that very good and distinguished men even today labour at [illaborare] this art, which you lift [elevas] more than wind or feather, or rather press down more with a millstone. For if it is such as you assert, how do the learned suffer themselves to be mocked? How do the upright suffer [themselves] to mock the ignorant? Away with this censure, Ereunius. |
| Ereu. Nisi insignis vanitas cum gravi detrimento Reipublicae Christianae coniuncta in his inesset; & nisi ortum a sacrilega illa Astronomia haberet, libens silerem. Nunc autem iniuria est generis humani in tam solemne delirium tacendo videri consensisse. Quae autem obiicis, sunt quae irritent bilem potius quam placent. | Ereu. Unless a notable vanity, joined with grave detriment to the Christian Commonwealth [Reip(ublicae)], were in these [things], and unless it had its rise from that sacrilegious Astronomy, I would willingly be silent. But now it is an injury of the human race to seem, by keeping silent, to have consented to so solemn a delirium. But [the things] which you object are such as irritate the bile rather than appease [placent]. |
| Probi hanc artem exercent, scio: sed eosdem etiam callidos & prudentes, quid inter scientiam intersit & loquacitatem discernere potes, hoc nego. Novi aliquos; sed, quantum ego aestimandis iis sim, nimium credulos. Si bolide tentes fundum, prope abesse ab aequore senties? Docti illuduntur etiam; hoc agnosco: Sed qui? in aliis videlicet artibus periti, huius ignari. | Upright [men] exercise this art, I know: but [that] the same [are] also shrewd and prudent, [and] can [potes, printed] discern what difference there is between science and loquacity, this I deny. I have known some; but, so far as I am [fit] for judging them, too credulous. If you try the bottom with a sounding-lead [bolide], will you perceive [it] to be near from the surface? The learned are mocked too; this I acknowledge: but which? Those skilled, namely, in other arts, ignorant of this. |
| Praeterea Doctorum duae sunt Classes. Alii enim eruditi sunt quasi memoria tenus docti, alii veritatum pensitatores. Duo itaque ad authoritatem petuntur ut & artis peritus sit, & ex eorum numero penes quos depositum est scientiae Tribunal. Tales vero falli vel nullos crediderim, vel paucissimos. | Besides, there are two Classes of the Learned. For some erudite [men] are learned as it were as far as memory [memoria tenus], others [are] weighers [pensitatores] of truths. Two [things] therefore are sought for authority: that [a man] both be skilled in the art, and [be] of the number of those with whom the Tribunal of science has been deposited. Such [men] I should believe to be deceived either none or very few. |
| Sunt autem vitae nostrae Tyranni spes & metus formidolosissimi, qui miseros mortales ad quidlibet credendum adigunt. Est & blanda cupido scientiae futurorum, & excellentiae supra sodales, lena & nimium & perniciose suada, quam evadere vix optimis permissum est. Haec sunt, Andabata, argumenta insolubilia demonstrationesque clarissimae, quae genus humanum in absurdam credulitatem captivant. | But there are Tyrants of our life, hope and fear, most dreadful, which drive wretched mortals to believing anything whatever. There is also the alluring desire of knowledge of future [things], and of excellence above [one’s] companions, a procuress [lena], both too much and perniciously persuasive [suada], which it has scarcely been permitted to the best to escape. These are, Andabata, the insoluble arguments and most clear demonstrations which take the human race captive into absurd credulity. |

## Words and phrases with alternative or special meanings

- ***Ens & bonum converti*** — rendered “Being and good to be converted [converti]”. ‘Convertible’, i.e. coextensive; not ‘changed into each other’. Scholastic doctrine of the transcendentals (ens et bonum convertuntur): whatever is, is good in so far as it is; hence evil must lie on the side of non-being.
- ***privatio / negatio*** — rendered “privation / negation”. A negation is a mere absence; a privation is the absence of what a subject ought to have. Augustinian–scholastic account of evil as privatio boni; White argues every created form carries a negation (its limit) and that evil derives from this negation, not from God’s positive action.
- ***causa efficiens / non efficiens; defectus efficientis*** — rendered “efficient cause / non-efficient; the defect of the efficient”. ‘Deficient cause’ (causa deficiens). Augustine, De civ. Dei XII.7: evil has no efficient but a deficient cause; White’s ‘parem proli parentem’ (a parent equal to the offspring) plays on this.
- ***ratio (causae, mali, formae)*** — rendered “notion [ratio]”. Also ‘account’, ‘definition’, ‘formal character’. Rendered ‘notion’ where it means a formal concept, as fixed in glossary (D3-N05).
- ***stationes creaturarum (in Deo / in se)*** — rendered “stations of creatures (in God / in themselves)”. ‘States’, ‘positions’, ‘modes of being’. Scholastic distinction between creatures’ ideal being in the divine intellect and their real being in themselves.
- ***pro rata*** — rendered “in proportion [pro rata]”. ‘Correspondingly’, ‘pro rata parte’. White flags it as a current idiom (‘ut loquuntur’).
- ***qui dat formam, dat consequentia ad formam*** — rendered “he who gives the form gives the consequents to the form”. ‘Gives what follows upon the form’. Scholastic axiom (cf. Aquinas, ST I q.105 a.5; I-II q.10 a.1), here used by Andabata to make God cause the negation attached to every created form.
- ***per se connexa / coniuncta*** — rendered “connected / joined per se”. ‘Essentially’, ‘of themselves’ (vs per accidens). Scholastic per se / per accidens distinction.
- ***translatio vocum; affixio*** — rendered “transference of words; affixing”. translatio = metaphorical transfer; affixio = predication (‘attaching’ a predicate to a subject). White distinguishes usage of speech (where God may loosely be called cause of evil) from natural causation.
- ***refundi (in materiam / potentialitatem)*** — rendered “to be poured back”. ‘Referred back’, ‘traced back’, ‘imputed’. Scholastic refundere: to attribute an effect back to its source.
- ***recudatur (in Deum)*** — rendered “be struck back [recudatur]”. ‘Recoil upon’, ‘be laid at the door of’; literally ‘be re-forged/hammered back’. Andabata’s claim that a first cause answers for what secondary causes do.
- ***nequitia (mali)*** — rendered “worthlessness [nequitia]”. ‘Wickedness’, ‘good-for-nothingness’ (ne-quam). Etymological sense fits: evil is ‘worth nothing’, not a being.
- ***extra carceres elapsus*** — rendered “slipped outside the barriers [carceres]”. ‘Out of the starting-gates’, ‘out of bounds’. Circus image (glossary: carceres = barriers); White keeps natural philosophy apart from theology.
- ***fando largiri*** — rendered “to bestow in speaking [fando]”. ‘By way of speech’, ‘in words’. Echoes the Varronian fatum a fando of Knot x (fate = what is spoken).
- ***Ἐπαῖόν(?) τοι ἔπος φύγειν ἕρκος ὀδόντων*** — rendered ““What a word has escaped the fence of [your] teeth!””. As printed the first word is garbled. Homeric formula ποῖόν σε ἔπος φύγεν ἕρκος ὀδόντων (Iliad 4.350, 14.83; Odyssey 1.64 etc.), a reproach for a rash word.
- ***Mathematici / Mathesis*** — rendered “Mathematicians / Mathesis”. Also ‘astrologers’ / ‘astrology’ (classical Latin mathematicus = astrologer). The ambiguity is the point: Andabata’s ‘greatest Mathematicians’ are writers of judicial astrology; Ereunius allows demonstration only to what is borrowed from Astronomy.
- ***sacrilega illa Astronomia*** — rendered “that sacrilegious Astronomy”. Here = judicial astrology (Astronomia in the broad, ancient sense). Distinguished from the mathematical astronomy just praised; cf. Sixtus V’s bull Coeli et terrae (1586) and Urban VIII’s Inscrutabilis (1631) against judicial astrology.
- ***circumforanea nugacitas*** — rendered “mountebank triviality”. ‘Market-place trifling’, ‘itinerant quackery’. Glossary: circumforaneus = mountebank; alludes to almanac-makers.
- ***bolis (bolide tentes fundum)*** — rendered “sounding-lead”. Greek βολίς, the plummet used to sound the depth. Image: the shallow see nothing of the depth; cf. Acts 27:28 (‘bolidem’ in the Vulgate).
- ***memoria tenus docti / veritatum pensitatores*** — rendered “learned as far as memory / weighers of truths”. ‘Learned by rote’ / ‘ponderers’. White’s constant contrast between erudition (authority) and science (demonstration).
- ***lena … suada*** — rendered “procuress … persuasive”. suada also ‘Persuasion’ (the goddess Suada/Peitho). Desire for knowledge of the future pictured as a bawd.
