# De Mundo — Dialogue II (De forma mundi, seu de motu magnorum corporum), Knot iv, part 1

*Latin title:* Nodus IV. Aestum maris non esse ex motu telluris  
*English title:* Knot IV. That the tide of the sea is not from the motion of the earth  
*Pages:* PDF pp. 172–180 = printed pp. 161–169 (from ‘Ereu. Novi rem apud eum sic sese habere’ on p. 161 to the end of Andabata's request ‘…non admodum peritum.’ on p. 169).

## Dialogue II, Knot iv, part 1 (printed pp. 161–169)

| Latin | English |
|---|---|
| [Margin: Nodus IV. Aestum maris non esse ex motu telluris.] Ereu. Novi rem apud eum sic sese habere; Quoniam tamen ex non occultis causis hactenus processimus, conandum est easdem in ultimos effectus, si forte alios habeant perducere. Et hoc primum arripio quod dixisti ipsummet pulcherrime hos motus in vase pleno aqua agitato describere. Duas itaque agitatio- [p. 162] nes vasis profert, quarum eosdem (nisi fallor) effectus profitetur. Nos itaque priorem amplexemur; quae est ut longi vasis alter terminus elevetur, deprimatur alius. Ex qua ipsos motus de quibus agis secuturos asserit; ut scilicet in terminis aqua crescat, in intermediis spatiis cursus vehemens, sed modica elevatio aquae comperiatur. | [Margin: Knot IV. That the tide [aestum] of the sea is not from the motion of the earth [telluris].] Ereu. I know the thing to stand thus with him; since, however, from not occult causes we have proceeded hitherto, it must be attempted to lead the same [causes] through [perducere] into the ultimate effects, if perhaps they have others. And this first I seize on, that you said he himself most beautifully describes these motions in a vessel full of water [and] agitated. Two agitations, then, [p. 162] of the vessel he puts forward, of which he professes the same effects (unless I am mistaken). Let us then embrace the former; which is that of a long vessel one end [terminus] be raised, the other be depressed. From which he asserts that the very motions of which you treat will follow; that is to say, that at the ends [terminis] the water grows, in the intermediate spaces a vehement course, but a moderate elevation of the water is found. |
| Et a te peto, Asphali, si superfundatur aqua vasi, non omnino pleno in altera extremitate, an putes non tantumdem effecturam? reddit enim rationem fluctuationis quia altior est seu remotior a centro terrae aqua, in una parte quam in alia: ideoque sese per fluctuationem ad aequilibrium agit, si autem superfundatur in altero termino; aqua manifeste altior est; quare ad aequilibrium eadem ratione sese reducet. Communes itaque Galilaeo mecum sunt difficultates & utrique solvendae, & quamcumque solutionem ipse dederit, eadem mihi inserviet. | And I ask of you, Asphalius, if water be poured over [superfundatur] into a vessel not altogether full, at one extremity, whether you think [it] will not effect just as much [tantumdem]? For he renders the reason [rationem] of the fluctuation [as being] because the water is higher, or more remote from the centre of the earth [terrae], in one part than in another: and therefore it drives itself through fluctuation to equilibrium; but if [water] be poured over at one end [termino], the water is manifestly higher; wherefore it will bring itself back to equilibrium by the same reason [ratione]. Common, therefore, to Galileo with me are the difficulties, and to be solved by both; and whatever solution he himself shall have given, the same will serve me. |
| Nunc addendum non ipsius via sed nostra ex necessitate ingrediendum esse. Quod sic declaro, neque Mediterraneo exeo. Si non ingreditur aqua ab Oceano; elevatio sic fiet in terminis ut nulla sit in medio. Constat autem toto ipso itinere a Gadibus usque Alexandriam inflatum esse in toto littore Africano mare: ex affluxu itaque aquae ab Oceano hoc contingit & non mera agitatione vasis. Quod [p. 163] autem evidens sit in latere Africano aestus, & antiquis & novis testibus satis constat; immo & in Hispanico, Gallicoque littore, etsi minus. | Now it must be added that [one] must of necessity enter [ingrediendum esse] not by his way but by ours. Which I declare thus, and I do not go out of the Mediterranean. If the water does not enter from the Ocean, the elevation will so come about at the ends [terminis] that there is none in the middle. But it is agreed [constat] that in the whole journey itself from Gades [Cadiz] as far as Alexandria the sea is swollen [inflatum] on the whole African shore: from the inflow [affluxu], therefore, of water from the Ocean this happens, and not from mere agitation of the vessel. But that [p. 163] the tide [aestus] is evident on the African side is sufficiently agreed by both ancient and new witnesses; indeed also on the Spanish and the French shore, although less. |
| Si petis cur magis in uno littore quam in alio deprehendatur hic effectus, coniecturam affero; quae verane sit oculati testes praedicanto. Certum est Mediterraneum Meotide, Euxino & Propontide ingurgitatum in Oceanum receptas aquas revomere: hoc cum nonnisi per collum illud Gaditanum efficere possit, necesse est Oceanum ingredientem non integro freti alveo ferri. Sortitus ergo fuerit oram barbaricam, reliquum littus permittet Mediterranei cursui. Magis itaque Lybico incumbet aestus, minorem in oppositis exhibebit sui speciem, in Propontide vero, Euxino & Meotide vel nullum, vel non conspicuum. | If you ask why this effect is detected more on one shore than on another, I bring a conjecture; whether it be true, let eye-witnesses [oculati testes] proclaim. It is certain that the Mediterranean, gorged [ingurgitatum] by the Maeotis, the Euxine and the Propontis, vomits back into the Ocean the waters received: since it can effect this only through that Gaditan neck, it is necessary that the Ocean entering is not carried by the entire channel [alveo] of the strait. It will therefore have obtained [sortitus fuerit] the Barbary coast [oram barbaricam]; the remaining shore it will leave [permittet] to the course of the Mediterranean. More, therefore, will the tide lie upon the Libyan [shore]; a lesser appearance [speciem] of itself will it exhibit on the opposite [shores]; but in the Propontis, the Euxine and the Maeotis either none, or not conspicuous. |
| Et eadem est causa cur rubrum non infletur neque Balthicum[.] Horum enim alterum per angustos anfractus adeundum est Oceano, Erythraeum potius plurium fluviorum coniuncti alvei quam mare dicendum est. Neque magnam credo rationem habendam quanam fluant, Occidentem dico inter & Orientem, an Meridiem & Septentrionem. In freto autem Gaditano non dissimulant peritiores nautae sese perpetuum flumen invenire quod captent & utantur, rudiores vero praetermittant. | And the same is the cause why the Red [Sea] is not swollen, nor the Baltic. For of these the one [alterum] must be approached by the Ocean through narrow windings [anfractus]; the Erythraean [Sea] rather is to be called the conjoined channel [alvei] of several rivers than a sea. Nor do I believe great account [rationem] is to be had of which way [quanam] they flow, between West, I say, and East, or South and North. But in the Gaditan strait the more skilled sailors do not dissemble that they find a perpetual stream [flumen], which they may catch and use, while the more unskilled pass it by. |
| [p. 164] [Margin: Unde aestus sit tam velox.] Asphal. Cogita tamen tecum, Ereuni, si aquam per fretum hoc Lybicum infundas, num sex horis Venetias vel Alexandriam pervenire possit, & rursus si superfundatur, quare non aeque utrique littori incumbat? | [p. 164] [Margin: Whence the tide is so swift.] Asphal. Think, however, with yourself, Ereunius: if you pour in water through this Libyan strait, whether it can arrive in six hours at Venice or Alexandria; and again, if it be poured over [superfundatur], why it does not lie equally upon each shore? |
| Ereu. Etiam tu, Asphali, credis aquas ex collo Gaditano easdem Alexandriam & Venetias pervenire? Non vides quomodo continuatione & non motu hoc fiat? quomodo nulla Tigrium vel accipitrum velocitate sit opus? sed tempore aliquantulo quo non aqua, sed motus eo feratur. Est in Septentrione nostro vallum ingens ad propulsandos Scotos olim a Romanis, ut ferunt, aggestum, inque eo canales ad vocem convehendam instituti; & per turris ad turrim distantiam, per eos interloquuntur homines. Num propterea aerem qui primus excepit vocem, totum illud intervallum credemus permeasse? an quod est reliquum tremore articulato percussisse? sic itaque aqua ab Oceano infusa tumorem Venetias vel Alexandriam pertulit, non suum corpus. | Ereu. Do you too, Asphalius, believe that the same waters from the Gaditan neck arrive at Alexandria and Venice? Do you not see how this comes about by continuation [continuatione] and not by motion? how there is no need of any speed of Tigers or of hawks? but of some little time, in which not the water, but the motion, is carried thither. There is in our North a huge rampart [vallum], heaped up once by the Romans, as they say, for driving off the Scots, and in it channels [canales] set up for conveying the voice; and through the distance of tower to tower men speak with one another through them. Shall we therefore believe that the air which first received the voice has passed through [permeasse] that whole interval? or that it has struck what is remaining [reliquum] with an articulated tremor? Thus therefore the water poured in from the Ocean has carried the swelling [tumorem] to Venice or Alexandria, not its own body. |
| Aqua itaque in uno loco superfunditur, tumor autem ad reliquas partes secundum littorum conditionem perveniet. Si in Italico littore sit maior resistentia propter Propontidos defluxum, minus illic intumescet, si in Anconitano vel Ragusiano littore velocior [p. 165] sit aquae motus ab Africa repulsae, minor & forte insensibilis elevatio, Venetiis in ipso fundo (ubi necesse est reflexionem & circuitum aquae fieri) manifestis aestus sese prodet indiciis. | The water, therefore, is poured over in one place, but the swelling will arrive at the remaining parts according to the condition of the shores. If on the Italian shore there be greater resistance because of the down-flow [defluxum] of the Propontis, less will it swell there; if on the Anconitan or Ragusan shore the motion of the water driven back from Africa be [p. 165] swifter, [there will be] a lesser and perhaps insensible elevation; at Venice, in the very bottom [fundo] [of the gulf] (where it is necessary that a reflexion and circuit of the water come about), the tide will betray itself by manifest indications. |
| Asphal. Hoc saltem memento, Galilaeum non hoc motu aquae sed eo qui ex resistentia oritur, usum esse. Et fors ita cadat, ut quantum motus quo tu uteris, tibi proprius est, tantum ipsi suus; & quod ait, arguat manifeste motum telluris a quo pendeat. | Asphal. Remember this at least, that Galileo has used not this motion of the water but that which arises from resistance. And chance [fors] may so fall out that, as much as the motion which you use is proper to you, so much is his [own] to him; and that what he says may manifestly prove [arguat] the motion of the earth [telluris], on which it depends. |
| Ereu. Trahis me, Asphali, ut video ad sententiam Galilaei impugnandam, quod ego illibentissime facio; tum quia exactissime scripsit, ideoque difficillime contra ipsum dicitur: tum quia ingenue fateor quaecumque ego in hac materia scio primitus ab ipso hausisse, neque posse contra ipsum nisi ex iis quae ab ipso didici quicquam profari. | Ereu. You drag me, Asphalius, as I see, to attacking [impugnandam] the opinion [sententiam] of Galileo, which I do most unwillingly; both because he has written most exactly, and therefore it is most difficultly spoken against him; and because I freely [ingenue] confess that whatever I know in this matter [materia] I have drawn at first [primitus] from him, and that I cannot utter [profari] anything against him except out of those things which I have learned from him. |
| Andab. Non sic elaberis, Ereuni. Adiuro enim te per ipsam veritatem quam colere profiteris, ne personarum acceptor sis; neque nos quicquam celes quod de hac re contemplatus sis. | Andab. Not so shall you slip away, Ereunius. For I adjure you by the truth itself which you profess to cultivate, that you be not an accepter of persons [personarum acceptor]; nor conceal from us anything which you have contemplated concerning this thing. |
| [Margin: Motum telluris non imprimere impetum in aquas.] Ereu. Imperiose, Andabata, sed quoniam nihil veritati negare possum, quae cogitavi, candide proponam, relicto vobis de rerum veritate iudicio. Ait itaque Galilaeus, si terra in motu suo obici impacta fuerit, iaculaturam [p. 166] quas habet aquas: & exemplo Navicularum, quibus aqua dulcis Venetias defertur, confirmat. Hoc itaque primo quaerendum est, an hoc in terra similiter usuvenire habeat? Mutuor itaque aliud ab eodem experimentum versatae pelvis aqua plenae vel scyphi aere pleni, in quo utroque, assumit idem, neque aquam, neque aerem rotari ad motum vasis, vel concipere aliquem impetum nisi forte circa ipsum limbum. | [Margin: That the motion of the earth [telluris] does not impress an impetus [impetum] upon the waters.] Ereu. Imperiously, Andabata; but since I can deny nothing to truth, the things which I have thought I will set forth candidly, the judgement concerning the truth of things being left to you. Galileo says, then, that if the earth [terra] in its motion shall have been struck against an obstacle [obici], it will hurl [iaculaturam] [p. 166] the waters which it has; and he confirms [it] by the example of the little Boats by which fresh [dulcis] water is carried down to Venice. This, therefore, must first be inquired: whether this has to happen [usuvenire habeat] similarly in the earth [terra]? I borrow, then, from the same [author] another experiment, of a basin [pelvis] full of water, or of a cup [scyphi] full of air, turned round [versatae], in each of which the same [author] assumes that neither the water nor the air is rotated at the motion of the vessel, or conceives any impetus, unless perhaps around the rim [limbum] itself. |
| Peto deinde quod horum exemplorum propius sit terrae circumferenti mare? Ego enim haec posteriora crediderim. Video enim motum telluris eum esse qui aquam a nixu versus centrum nullatenus impediat, & inde fieri ut nullum ei impetum imprimat: secus in motu naviculae contingere. Unde eveniet ut sicut in pelvi cum sistitur, nullus aquae motus sentiatur vel aeris in scypho, sic neque terra impingente mare motum aliquem persensurum. | I ask next, which of these examples is nearer to the earth [terrae] carrying the sea around [circumferenti]? For I would believe [crediderim] [it is] these latter. For I see that the motion of the earth [telluris] is such [eum] as in no way hinders the water from [its] striving [nixu] towards the centre, and that hence it comes about that it impresses no impetus on it: [and that it] happens otherwise in the motion of a little boat. Whence it will come about that, just as in the basin, when it is stopped, no motion of the water is felt, or of the air in the cup, so neither, the earth [terra] striking against [impingente], [is] the sea about to perceive [persensurum] any motion. |
| Cogita deinde terram non inaequaliter ferri, quem impetum habitura est aqua? An pars partem comprimet? vel lapsu imperceptibili mutabit locum? Non prius, cum omnes partes aequabiliter ferentur. Posterius vero, si sensus sit totam aquam mutaturam locum secundum partes respectu partium terrae, incredibile est: si respectu totius mundi simul cum terra, potius de- [p. 167] nominatio est quam motus. Motus enim nisi sit specialiter in ipso corpore, quod dicitur moveri, non habet effectus proprios. Non itaque cogitandum est aquam a telluris motu aliquem impetum concipere, si autem non concipit impetum, neque ex resistentia facta ipsi telluri mutationem aliquam habebit. | Think next [that] the earth [terram] is carried not unequally: what impetus will the water have? Will part compress part? or by an imperceptible slipping [lapsu] will it change place? Not the former, since all the parts will be carried equably. But the latter, if the sense be that the whole water will change place according to [its] parts in respect of the parts of the earth [terrae], is incredible; if in respect of the whole world together with the earth [terra], it is rather a de- [p. 167] nomination [denominatio] than a motion. For motion, unless it be specially in the very body which is said to be moved, does not have proper effects. It is not, therefore, to be thought that the water conceives any impetus from the motion of the earth [telluris]; and if it does not conceive an impetus, neither from a resistance made to the earth [telluri] itself will it have any change [mutationem]. |
| Proximum est ut mente revolvamus hoc, quod obicis loco ei tribuitur, videlicet inaequalitatem motus. Et ne hoc obicis locum habere concedam, prohibet experimentum globulorum nostrorum quos ad metam evolvimus, quos suavissime fluere experimur, cum tamen continue inaequalitatem hanc ipsam quam habet tellus circumferant, centrum enim in linea recta ferunt, cum circumferentia circa centrum perpetuo volutetur. Cogit etiam ratio, cum enim figura ad hunc motum nata videatur, unde possum existimare vim impedimenti subesse posse in eo ipso quod ad motum institutum est? Quare sicut quod Polares partes iis quae Aequatori subiectae sunt, tardius moveantur, impedimentum motui sphaerae exhibere non creditur: sic neque haec varietas volutationis obicis naturam habere. | Next is that we turn over in mind this, which is attributed to it in the place of an obstacle [obicis], namely the inequality of the motion. And that I should not grant that this has the place of an obstacle, the experiment of our little balls [globulorum] which we roll out to the mark [metam] forbids, which we find by experience [experimur] to flow most smoothly [suavissime], although they continually carry around this very inequality which the earth [tellus] has; for they carry the centre in a straight line, while the circumference is perpetually rolled around the centre. Reason [ratio] also compels: for since the figure seems born [nata] for this motion, whence can I judge that a force of impediment can underlie [subesse] in that very thing which has been instituted for motion? Wherefore, just as [the fact] that the Polar parts are moved more slowly than those which are subjected to the Equator is not believed to present an impediment to the motion of the sphere, so neither [is it believed] that this variety of rolling [volutationis] has the nature of an obstacle. |
| Sed admissa utraque suppositione, & aquam proprium concipere impetum, & obicis naturam habere dictam motus inaequalitatem. Qualis hinc secutura sit motio disquiramus. Causa autem motus est quod tardescat terra, [p. 168] mare vero suam continuet citationem. Procurret itaque mare primo & in littoribus & alto, deinde littora primo tarditatem concipient, deinde altum. Non itaque aestus per accessum & decessum ab alto versus littora vel e contra fiet; sed aequaliter utrimque & littora prius & inanientur, & turgescent, deinde mare: quod an verum sit experientia loquatur. | But each supposition being admitted, both that the water conceives a proper impetus, and that the said inequality of the motion has the nature of an obstacle. Let us inquire what sort of motion [motio] is to follow hence. Now the cause of the motion is that the earth [terra] grows slow, [p. 168] but the sea continues its velocity [citationem]. The sea, therefore, will run forward first both at the shores and on the deep [alto]; then the shores will first conceive slowness, then the deep. Not, therefore, will the tide come about through a coming and going [accessum & decessum] from the deep towards the shores, or the contrary; but equally on both sides, both the shores first will both be emptied and will swell, then the sea: whether which be true, let experience speak. |
| [Margin: Quaere in tab. figura quarta.] Rursus cum medius terrae orbis tardius procedit, reliquus magis citatur. Quantum itaque ex una parte deseritur mare a terra, videlicet, versus F, tantum ex reliqua parte versus D, mare recumbet versus G. Fluxus itaque simul, & semel erit ab utroque puncto versus G. Quare occurrent sibi aestus oppositi & patebit navigantibus versus G, & E, talis marium colluctatio. Tandem attendendum est hoc ipsum continuo fieri. i. perpetuo terram in novis partibus ex una parte acquirere citationem ex alia tarditatem, ut motus maris hinc natus, Tremor quidam & confusio partium aquae evasura sit magis, quam ordinatus fluxus vel refluxus. | [Margin: Seek in the table, figure four.] Again, when the middle of the globe [orbis] of the earth [terrae] proceeds more slowly, the remainder is more quickened [citatur]. As much, therefore, as on one side the sea is deserted by the earth [terra], namely towards F, so much on the remaining side, towards D, will the sea lie back [recumbet] towards G. The flux, therefore, will be at the same time and at once from each point towards G. Wherefore opposite tides will run against each other, and to those sailing towards G and E such a wrestling [colluctatio] of seas will be apparent. Finally, it must be attended to that this very thing comes about continuously, i.e. that the earth [terram] perpetually in new parts acquires on one side velocity [citationem], on the other slowness, so that the motion of the sea born hence will turn out [evasura sit] rather a certain Tremor and confusion of the parts of the water than an ordered flux or reflux. |
| [Margin: Aestum si a telluris motu foret, maiorem futurum.] Adiiciamus & mentem quantitati, quae quoniam motus a vase. i. a fundo & lateribus ortum habet, a tota ipsa massa maris proficiscitur, tantae autem molis quantillum motum possumus cogitare qui denos circiter pedes & non magis turres & montes adaequet? Cum proportione ad na- [p. 169] viculas Venetas & aquae in iis motum, si res agatur, credo expeditam esse. At e diverso quomodo hic ipse motus non sit extrusio non satis capio; nam & extrusio ab impetu conversione concepto est, & aqua non aequaliter ad centrum, sed a centro movetur? Vel ergo nihil potest moveri aqua, vel terrae orbem inundabit. | [Margin: That the tide, if it were from the motion of the earth [telluris], would be greater.] Let us apply [adiiciamus] our mind also to the quantity, which, since the motion has its origin from the vessel, i.e. from the bottom and the sides, sets out from the whole mass itself of the sea; but of so great a bulk [molis], how small [quantillum] a motion can we think, which would equal about ten feet each [denos] and not rather towers and mountains? With [its] proportion to the Venetian little [p. 169] boats and the motion of the water in them, if the matter be worked out [si res agatur], I believe [it] to be readily settled [expeditam]. But on the other side [e diverso], how this very motion is not an extrusion [extrusio] I do not sufficiently grasp; for both extrusion is from an impetus conceived by turning [conversione], and the water is moved not equally to the centre, but from the centre? Either, then, the water can be moved not at all [nihil], or it will inundate the globe [orbem] of the earth [terrae]. |
| Praeter haec quomodo motus communis insensibilis est (quod supra etiam urgebam) si talem effectum habet? & si aquae propter liquiditatem conceditur deficere a telluris motu, quanto magis idem aeri concedendum erit, multo liquidiori & motus ad alterius motum minus capaci? Adiice neque terminos motuum sufficienti ratione constitutos esse. Nam & senarum horarum terminum fatetur ex causis incomprehensis pendere, & secundum causas principales duodenarum esse debuisse, & menstruos accessus in causam refert quam coniicit tantum, & neque ratione certam facit & animadversione nondum notatam esse contendit. Annua sola crementa seu semestria potius in causam notam, & principiis praeiactis conformem reducit. | Besides these things, how is the common motion [motus communis] insensible (which I was urging above also) if it has such an effect? and if it is granted to the water, because of [its] liquidity, to fall short [deficere] of the motion of the earth [telluris], how much more will the same have to be granted to the air, much more liquid, and less capable of motion at the motion of another? Add that neither are the terms [terminos] of the motions established with sufficient reason [ratione]. For he both confesses that the term of six hours each [senarum horarum] depends on incomprehensible causes, and [that] according to the principal causes it ought to have been [a term] of twelve each [duodenarum]; and the monthly comings [accessus] he refers to a cause which he only conjectures, and he neither makes [it] certain by reason, and contends that [it] has not yet been noted by observation [animadversione]. The annual increments [crementa] alone, or rather [potius] semestrial ones, he reduces to a known cause, and [one] conformable to the principles laid down before [praeiactis]. |
| [Margin: Aestuum terminos in Galilaei sententia non congruere.] Andab. Attamen, nisi te taedium ceperit mei, haec ipsa paulo diffusius expone, novisti enim me doctrinae Galilaei non admodum peritum. | [Margin: That the terms [terminos] of the tides do not agree in the opinion [sententia] of Galileo.] Andab. Nevertheless, unless weariness of me has taken you, set out these very things a little more diffusely; for you know me [to be] not very skilled in the doctrine of Galileo. |

## Words and phrases with alternative or special meanings

- ***Aestum maris non esse ex motu telluris*** — rendered “That the tide of the sea is not from the motion of the earth”. Marginal title; the Syllogus (FM2) has 'non nasci' (is not born). Knot iv is White's critique of Galileo's tide theory (Dialogo, Day 4), which made the tides the chief physical proof of the earth's double motion.
- ***superfundere / superfundi*** — rendered “pour over / be poured over”. pour in on top, flood over; overflow White's alternative to Galileo's 'vessel' model: a tidal inflow at one end, not a tilting of the basin.
- ***continuatio (vs. motus)*** — rendered “continuation”. propagation, transmission White distinguishes the travel of a swelling (tumor) through water from transport of the water itself, like sound through air — a wave concept.
- ***tumor*** — rendered “swelling”. rise, bulge, wave-crest Glossary rendering kept; here the thing that travels, not the water body.
- ***canales ad vocem convehendam (in vallo)*** — rendered “channels for conveying the voice”. speaking-tubes Legend that Hadrian's Wall had speaking-tubes between towers (repeated by Camden and others); White uses it as an analogy for propagation.
- ***ingurgitatus*** — rendered “gorged”. engulfed, swallowed into; flooded The Mediterranean 'gorged' by the Black Sea outflow and disgorging (revomere) into the Ocean.
- ***sortiri (oram)*** — rendered “obtain”. get by lot, be allotted Glossary: sortiri = obtain; future perfect 'sortitus fuerit'.
- ***ora barbarica / Lybicum (littus)*** — rendered “Barbary coast / Libyan [shore]”. North African coast Classical 'Libya' = Africa; Barbary = Maghreb.
- ***Erythraeum (mare)*** — rendered “Erythraean [Sea]”. Red Sea (sometimes also the Arabian Sea / Indian Ocean) White calls it rather a joined channel of rivers than a sea.
- ***impetus*** — rendered “impetus”. impulse, force impressed, momentum Scholastic/Galilean term for force impressed on a projectile; the debate is whether the earth's rotation 'impresses' impetus on the sea.
- ***obex*** — rendered “obstacle”. bolt, barrier, obstruction Galileo's boat example: water in a barge surges when the barge is checked; White asks what plays the role of the obstacle for the earth.
- ***inaequalitas motus*** — rendered “inequality of the motion”. non-uniformity of speed Galileo's cause of the tides: composition of diurnal rotation and annual revolution makes parts of the earth alternately accelerate and decelerate.
- ***denominatio*** — rendered “denomination”. naming, extrinsic designation Scholastic: a 'motion' relative only to the whole world is a mere extrinsic denomination, with no proper effects in the body.
- ***globuli quos ad metam evolvimus*** — rendered “little balls which we roll out to the mark”. bowls (the game of bowls) English game; a rolling ball's points move unequally while its centre moves uniformly — White's analogy for the rotating, revolving earth.
- ***volutatio*** — rendered “rolling”. revolving, rotation Glossary rendering kept.
- ***citatio / citari*** — rendered “velocity / be quickened”. speed, acceleration Glossary: citatio = velocity.
- ***colluctatio*** — rendered “wrestling”. struggle, clash Glossary rendering; clash of opposite tides.
- ***extrusio*** — rendered “extrusion”. thrusting out; centrifugal ejection White argues that an impetus conceived by rotation (conversio) would throw water outward from the centre, flooding the globe — the old centrifugal objection to a moving earth.
- ***motus communis*** — rendered “common motion”. shared motion Galilean principle that motion shared by observer and object is insensible; White turns it against Galileo.
- ***senarum / duodenarum (horarum)*** — rendered “of six hours each / of twelve each”. distributive numerals Galileo admitted his mechanism predicts one tidal period of twelve hours, ascribing the six-hour period to secondary causes (shape of basins).
- ***crementa annua / semestria*** — rendered “annual / semestrial increments”. yearly / half-yearly increases Galileo's account of greater tides at the equinoxes; White grants this only.
- ***personarum acceptor*** — rendered “accepter of persons”. respecter of persons, partial judge Biblical/Vulgate phrase (acceptio personarum, Acts 10:34; Rom 2:11).
- ***usuvenire habeat*** — rendered “has to happen”. should happen, is to happen Late Latin periphrastic future with habere.
- ***fors ita cadat*** — rendered “chance may so fall out”. perhaps it may be that Archaic 'fors' (chance) as adverbial 'perhaps'.
