# De Mundo — Dialogue I (De materia mundi), Knot xii

*Latin title:* Nodus XII. Tellurem non esse magnetem  
*English title:* Knot XII. That the earth [tellurem] is not a magnet  
*Pages:* PDF pp. 112–117 = printed pp. 101–106 (from 'Asphal. Tam leviter itaque nobis praeripis' on p. 101 to '…purum consistet.' on p. 106, where the margin marks Nodus XIII)

## Dialogue I, Knot xii (printed pp. 101–106)

| Latin | English |
|---|---|
| [p. 101] [Margin: Nodus XII. Tellurem non esse magnetem.] Asphal. Tam leviter itaque nobis praeripis notionem telluris a duobus huius saeculi Philosophiae principibus traditam, Galilaeo inquam et Gilberto, qui uterque hanc [p. 102] sententiam quasi definitam et demonstratam tradit, magneticam videlicet esse terrae naturam. | [p. 101] [Margin: Knot XII. That the earth [tellurem] is not a magnet.] Asphal. So lightly therefore do you snatch away [praeripis] from us the notion of the earth [telluris] handed down by two princes of Philosophy of this age [saeculi], Galileo, I say, and Gilbert, each of whom hands down this [p. 102] opinion [sententiam] as [quasi] defined and demonstrated, namely that the nature of the earth [terrae] is magnetic. |
| Ereu. Nequaquam ego, cum e diverso nihil magis optem, quam tales ab ipsis apodixis, ut ego etiam dubitare nequeam, tum propter virorum authoritatem, tum quod Gentilis alter, alter candidissimus inventi egregii in externo homine et admirator et laudator. | Ereu. By no means I [do so], since on the contrary [e diverso] I would wish nothing more than such demonstrations [apodixis] from them that I too could not doubt, both on account of the authority of the men, and because the one [is my] countryman [Gentilis], the other the most candid both admirer and praiser of an excellent discovery [inventi] in a foreign man. |
| Sed cur iis assentiri non queam audi, magnetem vel ipsam esse mineram ferri, vel quid simile, et cognatum tradit ipsemet Gilbertus, sed ferrum ex omnibus metallis maxime in cortice telluris haerere norunt metallici; Profundius qui rimantur terram, metallica, et concretos fere succos inveniunt. | But why I cannot assent to them, hear: that the magnet is either the very ore [mineram] of iron, or something similar and cognate, Gilbert himself hands down; but that iron, of all metals, clings [haerere] most in the rind [cortice] of the earth [telluris], the metal-workers [metallici] know; those who search [rimantur] the earth [terram] more deeply find metallic [things], and almost [fere] concreted juices [succos]. |
| Neque placet argumentum, quod ex compressione saxeam circa meditullium terrae molem arguit. Neque enim saxa gravissima sunt, sed iis metalla praeponderant, et saxa quidem circa corticem terrae videmus, immo (si licet ita dicere) in aere, cum montibus scopulisque haereant, et habeo unde suspicor generationem magnetis ex iis caussis quae supra terram sunt pendere, quaeque non multum in terram desidunt. | Nor does the argument please [me] which from compression argues a stony mass [molem] about the middle [meditullium] of the earth [terrae]. For neither are stones the heaviest, but metals outweigh them; and stones indeed we see about the rind [corticem] of the earth [terrae], nay rather (if it is permitted so to say) in the air, since they cling to mountains and crags; and I have [grounds] whence I suspect that the generation of the magnet depends on those causes which are above the earth [terram], and which do not sink down [desidunt] much into the earth [terram]. |
| Praeterea aquae quam profundae sint nemo novit, cum fallant omnem bolidem. Eae autem si viscera telluris subeant, omnia alia potius quam saxa promittunt. Adde (quantum coniectandi locus est) [p. 103] potius ignem (quod Chymici divinant) quam saxum fundo claudi par esse; sic enim totam tellurem utilem fore aestimarem. | Besides, how deep the waters are no one has known, since they deceive every sounding-lead [bolidem]. And these, if they go under [subeant] the bowels [viscera] of the earth [telluris], promise all other [things] rather than stones. Add (so far as there is room for conjecturing) [p. 103] that it is fitting [par esse] that fire rather (which the Chymists divine) than stone be shut up at the bottom; for so I should judge that the whole earth [tellurem] would be useful. |
| Sicut enim in Animalibus et plantis ex corde et radice in totum corpus derivari activum calorem cernimus, sic ex terrae centro, a Demogorgone sive Archaeo Chymico in omnia terrae membra per canales, fumidos quosdam vapores emitti, ut non possum asserere, sic nec habeo cur reclamem. | For just as in Animals and plants we discern that active heat is derived from the heart and the root into the whole body, so that from the centre of the earth [terrae], from Demogorgon or the Chymical Archaeus, certain smoky vapours are emitted through channels into all the members of the earth [terrae] — as I cannot assert [it], so neither have I [reason] why I should protest [reclamem]. |
| Certe lumen solis in cavernis tam profundis telluris operari (ut non inficior, faciet enim per vapores ex mari excitatos) sic iis quae in visceribus terrae generantur hoc sufficere non affirmem. | Certainly, that the light of the sun works [operari] in caverns of the earth [telluris] so deep — as I do not deny [it] (for it will do [so] through vapours raised from the sea), so I would not affirm that this suffices for those [things] which are generated in the bowels [visceribus] of the earth [terrae]. |
| Certe montes et insulas ab imo Oceano iactatos et historiae meminerunt; et oculati testes ante paucos annos prodiderunt: Et, ne qua vi hoc fieret, ambigere valeamus, visi sunt interdiu fumi noctu flammae egestionis comites. Quis a solis afflatu hos effectus suspicetur? | Certainly mountains and islands thrown up from the lowest Ocean both histories have remembered, and eye-witnesses [oculati testes] a few years ago have reported [prodiderunt]: and, lest we might be able to waver [ambigere] by what force this was done, there were seen by day smokes, by night flames, companions of the ejection [egestionis]. Who would suspect these effects [to come] from the breath [afflatu] of the sun? |
| Sed neque autumem telluri si curiosius examinetur, magneticas qualitates competere. Respectum ad polum aliunde pendere ipsemet Galilaeus existimat. Alias ut habeat magnetis proprietates non video quomodo quadret, cum magnes respectum ad tellurem, tellus ad aliud non habeat, unde magnes dici debeat. | But neither would I declare [autumem] that magnetic qualities belong [competere] to the earth [telluri], if it be examined more curiously. That the respect [respectum] to the pole depends from elsewhere, Galileo himself judges. Otherwise, that it should have the properties of the magnet, I do not see how it squares [quadret], since the magnet has a respect [respectum] to the earth [tellurem], [but] the earth [tellus] has none to another [thing], whence it ought to be called a magnet. |
| Sed frustra mentem meam et aures vestras torqueo, cum ipsae coniecturae nostrae ad millesimam altitudinis terrenae partem [p. 104] difficulter attingant. Infra quam an solidus sit orbis, an ingentes cavernae et generationum aliae lineae, velle scire vanum est, opinari stolidum. | But in vain do I torture my mind and your ears, since our very conjectures reach with difficulty to the thousandth part of the earthly depth [altitudinis] [p. 104]. Below which, whether the globe [orbis] be solid, or [there be] huge caverns and other lines [lineae] of generations, to wish to know is vain, to opine [opinari] stupid. |
| Andab. Ergo Insulam sancti Martini, et virides homines narras, ut credulus olim Monachus; si non aliud officiat, certe rapido illic aere qui egressum non haberet, suffocatos necesse est expirare. | Andab. Therefore you tell of the Island of Saint Martin and green men, like the credulous Monk once; if nothing else should hinder [officiat], certainly, in the swift [rapido] air there which would have no egress, [they] must, suffocated, expire. |
| Ereu. Nosses modo quam brevi aere etiam nos victitare possimus, quam facile sit remedium ut pene nullo satiemur, quot diebus sine respiratione nos teneat interdum morbus, et in utero materno quantum clausi cubemus, nunquam haec diceres. | Ereu. If you only knew on how little [brevi] air even we can live [victitare], how easy is the remedy, that we be sated with almost none, for how many days a disease sometimes holds us without respiration, and how long [quantum] we lie shut up in the maternal womb, you would never say these [things]. |
| Sed Cavea cuius diameter plus quinque milliarium millia fluit, cistam vel sarcophagum cogitare angusti est animi. Cur non ab Archaeo illo, si quis est, decoqui possunt subterranea mixta in aerem nostro nihil impuriorem. Ego certe nihil quod iure opponi possit video. | But the Cavity [Cavea] whose diameter runs [fluit] [to] more [than] five thousands of miles — to think [of it as] a chest or sarcophagus is [the part] of a narrow mind. Why can subterranean mixts [mixta] not be boiled down [decoqui] by that Archaeus, if there is any, into an air nothing more impure than ours. I certainly see nothing which could rightly be opposed. |
| [Margin: Aerem esse telluris effluvium.] Andab. Ergo aerem ex terra decoqui posse credas? | [Margin: That air is an effluvium of the earth [telluris].] Andab. So you would believe that air can be boiled down [decoqui] out of earth [terra]? |
| Ereu. Immo vero, et Aristotelem habeo authorem et plerosque naturae interpretes, qui terram in aquam, aquam in aerem rarefactione transferri profitentur: Et quid aliud de undoso hoc elemento quo vescimur, vis, ut cogitem, quam effluvium esse magnae huius qua [p. 105] nitimur, molis? | Ereu. Yes indeed, and I have as author both Aristotle and most interpreters of nature, who profess that earth [terram] is transferred into water, water into air, by rarefaction: and what else do you want me to think of this wavy [undoso] element on which we feed [vescimur], than that it is an effluvium of this great mass [molis] on which [p. 105] we lean [nitimur]? |
| an externo solis adminiculo, an vi viscitus latente expressum ipsum fluidum et innatantium siccorum examinibus mixtum, non inquiro. A te autem, Andabata, hoc peto, si fluidum a principio creatum fuit, miscibile hoc putas, an immixtum perpetuo mansurum? | Whether the fluid itself [was] pressed out by the external aid [adminiculo] of the sun, or by a force lying hid inwardly [viscitus], and mixed with swarms [examinibus] of dry [things] swimming in [it] [innatantium siccorum], I do not inquire. But from you, Andabata, I ask this: if the fluid was created from the beginning, do you think this [to be] miscible, or that it would remain perpetually unmixed? |
| Andab. Cur enim non aeternum et immixtum dicam? | Andab. For why should I not call it eternal and unmixed? |
| Ereu. Adhaererene siccis in eo fluitantibus credis vel non? | Ereu. Do you believe that it adheres [adhaerere] to the dry [things] floating in it, or not? |
| Andab. Nequaquam. | Andab. By no means. |
| Ereu. Cur ergo fluidum appellas? cum fluidum terminos proprios non habeat, sed a sicco terminetur, ideoque sicco necessario adhaereat, dividaturque facilius in semetipso, quam a sicco, ut in aqua, oleo et similibus perpetuo est cernere, quae quanta difficultate deradantur a siccis, abstersio, calefactio et similes exiccandi rationes demonstrant. | Ereu. Why then do you call [it] fluid? since the fluid has not proper terms [terminos] [of its own], but is terminated by the dry, and therefore necessarily adheres to the dry, and is divided more easily in itself than from the dry, as it is perpetually [possible] to discern in water, oil and the like; with how great difficulty these are scraped off [deradantur] from dry [things], wiping [abstersio], heating and similar ways [rationes] of drying out demonstrate. |
| Andab. Etiamsi in his agnoscam sic rem habere, num propterea tantumdem in aere fatendum est? | Andab. Even if I should acknowledge [agnoscam] that the matter is so in these, must just as much [tantumdem] therefore be confessed in air? |
| Ereu. Si ex humiditate hoc accidit, et humiditas in aere potentior est, magis in aere fatendum est quam in aqua. Adde in oleo quod aereum reputatur, magis experimentis clarescere. | Ereu. If this happens from humidity, and humidity is more potent in air, it must be confessed more in air than in water. Add that in oil, which is reputed airy [aereum], it grows clear [clarescere] more by experiments. |
| Habet quoque aer sua experimenta, ut [p. 106] cum lamina vitrea, vel acus chalybea vel aliud quidlibet ita molliter aquae imponitur ut innatet: ex eo enim quod eadem si humida imponantur, descendunt, patet non ex eorum natura, sed ex natura ambientis aeris haberi ut natent: hoc autem esse non aliunde potest, quam quia aer non se permittit separari, neque hoc nisi quia fortiter adhaerescit aquae, aeri igitur communis est haec proprietas. | Air too has its own experiments, as [p. 106] when a glass plate [lamina], or a steel needle, or any other thing whatever, is placed so softly on water that it floats on [it]: for from this, that the same [things], if they are placed [on it] wet, descend, it is evident that it is had [haberi] not from their nature but from the nature of the surrounding [ambientis] air that they float; and this can be from nowhere else than because air does not permit itself to be separated, nor this except because it strongly adheres to water; to air therefore this property is common. |
| His assumptis mistile evadit fluidum quod syncerum creatum erat: quid enim aliud mixtionem appellamus, quam ut per minima sibi adhaereant, ex quibus componitur totum et sic media quaedam inter componentia evadat ratio totius? si autem natum sit misceri, statim in hac perpetua corporum apud nos colluctatione totum misceri, et nusquam circa terram in aliqua spectabili magnitudine purum consistet. | These [things] being assumed, the fluid which had been created pure [syncerum] turns out [evadit] miscible [mistile]: for what else do we call mixtion than that those [things] of which the whole is composed adhere to one another through [their] least [parts] [per minima], and so a certain middle character [ratio] of the whole turns out between the components? And if it is apt by nature [natum sit] to be mixed, at once in this perpetual wrestling [colluctatione] of bodies among us the whole [is] to be mixed [misceri], and nowhere about the earth [terram] will it subsist [consistet] pure in any visible [spectabili] magnitude. |

## Words and phrases with alternative or special meanings

- ***magnes / magneticus*** — rendered “magnet / magnetic”. lodestone; the earth-as-magnet thesis Target is William Gilbert, De magnete (1600), which held the earth a great magnet (terrella), endorsed by Galileo in the Dialogo (1632, Day 3).
- ***princeps (Philosophiae)*** — rendered “prince”. chief, leading figure Gilbert and Galileo named as the two leaders of the new natural philosophy.
- ***apodixis*** — rendered “demonstrations”. Greek ἀπόδειξις, strict demonstration Read as acc. pl. (apodixes/apodeixeis); the Aristotelian term for scientific proof, sharper than demonstratio's common use.
- ***Gentilis*** — rendered “countryman”. kinsman, of the same gens/nation; also 'gentile, pagan' Gilbert, like White, was English; Galileo, the 'other', praised the foreigner Gilbert's discovery (Dialogo, Day 3).
- ***inventum*** — rendered “discovery”. invention, finding Gilbert's magnetic philosophy.
- ***minera ferri*** — rendered “ore of iron”. mine, mineral vein Gilbert (De magnete I) calls the lodestone the best iron ore.
- ***cortex telluris*** — rendered “rind of the earth”. crust, bark Gilbert distinguished the 'cortex' of decayed matter from the true magnetic earth within; White turns his own word against him.
- ***metallici*** — rendered “metal-workers”. miners, metallurgists Practical mining knowledge (cf. Agricola).
- ***succi concreti*** — rendered “concreted juices”. solidified saps; mineral juices Renaissance mineralogy: metals and minerals form from congealed subterranean juices (succi).
- ***meditullium*** — rendered “middle”. core, centre Rare word (Varro/Gellius) for the earth's innermost part.
- ***bolis (bolidem)*** — rendered “sounding-lead”. plummet, sounding line Greek βολίς; the sea's depth eludes all sounding.
- ***par esse*** — rendered “be fitting”. be equal, be reasonable Classical idiom par est = 'it is right/proper'.
- ***Chymici / Archaeus Chymicus*** — rendered “Chymists / Chymical Archaeus”. alchemists, Paracelsians; the Archaeus = vital governing spirit Paracelsian / Helmontian notion of the central fire or archeus of the earth that 'cooks' minerals.
- ***Demogorgon*** — rendered “Demogorgon”. primordial deity of the earth Boccaccio's Genealogia deorum makes Demogorgon the ancestor of the gods dwelling in the bowels of the earth; here paired ironically with the Archaeus.
- ***calor activus*** — rendered “active heat”. vital heat, calor innatus Aristotelian-Galenic innate heat diffused from the heart (animals) and root (plants).
- ***egestio*** — rendered “ejection”. discharge, evacuation Volcanic ejecta; the 'eye-witnesses a few years ago' likely refers to the submarine eruptions off the Azores (São Miguel, 1638).
- ***respectus ad polum*** — rendered “respect to the pole”. orientation, relation to the pole Directional (polar) property of the magnet; per glossary respectus = relatio. White's point: a magnet is so called relative to the earth, but the earth has no such relation to anything further.
- ***autumare*** — rendered “declare”. affirm, opine Archaic (glossary).
- ***generationum lineae*** — rendered “lines of generations”. veins, channels where things are generated Possibly mining 'veins'; reading clear on the page, sense uncertain.
- ***Insula sancti Martini; virides homines*** — rendered “Island of Saint Martin; green men”.  The 'green children' of Woolpit in William of Newburgh (Historia rerum Anglicarum I.27), who said they came from St Martin's Land, a subterranean twilit country; the 'credulous Monk' is William (a canon).
- ***rapidus (aer)*** — rendered “swift”. consuming, scorching, violent Andabata imagines the enclosed air as violently stifling.
- ***victitare*** — rendered “live”. subsist, live on Frequentative of vivere.
- ***Cavea*** — rendered “Cavity”. cave, hollow, enclosure The supposed hollow interior of the earth.
- ***mixtum / mixtio / mistilis*** — rendered “mixt / mixtion / miscible”. compound; chemical combination Aristotelian mixtio (GC I.10): union of components 'per minima' giving a new intermediate character (ratio totius).
- ***decoquere*** — rendered “boil down”. cook, concoct, digest Alchemical / Aristotelian concoction (pepsis).
- ***effluvium*** — rendered “effluvium”. outflow, exhalation Key term of Gilbert's magnetism (effluvia) and later Digby's corpuscular physics; White makes air the earth's effluvium.
- ***fluidum / siccum*** — rendered “fluid / dry”. moist / dry (Aristotelian qualities) Aristotle GC II.2: the moist is ill-bounded by its own term but easily bounded by another; White uses this to argue air adheres to dry bodies and hence is miscible.
- ***terminus (proprius)*** — rendered “term”. boundary, limit Aristotelian definition of the moist (ὑγρόν) as not bounded by its own limit.
- ***per minima*** — rendered “through [their] least [parts]”. by minimal particles Scholastic minima naturalia; the mark of true mixtion as opposed to juxtaposition.
- ***natum esse*** — rendered “be apt by nature”. be born to, be naturally fitted Scholastic aptum natum.
- ***lamina vitrea / acus chalybea*** — rendered “glass plate / steel needle”.  Floating-bodies experiments of Galileo's Discorso intorno alle cose che stanno in su l'acqua (1612), where floating of thin bodies is ascribed to the adhering air.
- ***undosum elementum quo vescimur*** — rendered “wavy element on which we feed”.  Air as the food of breath; undosus continues the fluid imagery.
- ***viscitus*** — rendered “inwardly”. from the bowels, internally Rare adverb (from viscus).
- ***colluctatio*** — rendered “wrestling”. struggle, conflict Perpetual mutual action of sublunary bodies.

## Translator's notes

- Unit: Dialogue I, Knot xii, complete in one part. PDF pp. 112–117 = printed pp. 101–106, from 'Asphal. Tam leviter itaque nobis praeripis' (p. 101) to '…in aliqua spectabili magnitudine purum consistet.' (p. 106). The margin 'Nodus XIII. Singulis magnis solidis suum liquidum esse' stands beside the lines from 'misceri, et nusquam' to 'expectandum est' on p. 106; Knot xiii is taken to begin with the new sentence 'Et quod in terra usuvenit, idem in globis illis coelestibus…', which states its thesis. Offset +11 holds (p. 106 = PDF 117).
- Title: margin and FM2 table agree: 'Tellurem non esse magnetem'. Note that only the first half of the knot is about magnetism; from p. 104 it passes to the origin of air as an effluvium of the earth (second margin 'Aerem esse telluris effluvium') and its miscibility.
- Readings: (a) p. 102 'apodixis' as printed, taken as acc. pl. (= apodixes). (b) p. 102 'concretos … succos' as printed. (c) p. 102 'suspicor' indicative as printed after 'habeo unde'. (d) p. 104 'generationum aliae lineae' as printed (sense uncertain: veins? lines of descent?). (e) p. 104 'plus quinque milliarium millia fluit' — 'fluit' as printed {?fluit}; possibly a misprint (for 'fuit'/'sit'?); 'Cavea' is a hanging nominative. (f) p. 104 'Cur non … possunt … impuriorem.' printed with a full stop, not a question mark. (g) p. 106 'totum misceri' (infinitive) followed by 'consistet' (future) — an anacoluthon; perhaps 'necesse est' understood, or a misprint for 'miscebitur'. (h) p. 105 'deradantur' as printed. (i) p. 102 'egregij' normalised to 'egregii'.
- Verification pass (sentence-by-sentence against the Latin and the page images, with 300 dpi crops of pp. 102–103): 2 corrections — (1) 'Adhaererene … credis' first rendered 'that it would adhere', corrected to present 'that it adheres' (present infinitive); (2) 'Etiamsi … agnoscam' corrected to subjunctive 'Even if I should acknowledge'.
