# De Mundo — Dialogue III (De causis mundi), Knot xiii

*Latin title:* Nodus XIII. Terram in usus hominum esse conditam.  
*English title:* Knot XIII. That the Earth was founded for the uses of men.  
*Pages:* PDF pp. 393–401 = printed pp. 382–390 (from Asphalius’ ‘Te latet itaque hoc ipsum quod acceptas’ on p. 382 to Ereunius’ ‘…donec conquievero.’ on p. 390).

## Dialogue III, Knot xiii (printed pp. 382–390)

| Latin | English |
|---|---|
| [Margin, p. 382: Nodus XIII. Terram in usus hominum esse conditam.] | [Margin, p. 382: Knot XIII. That the Earth [terram] was founded [conditam] for the uses of men.] |
| Asphal. Te latet itaque hoc ipsum quod acceptas, a quantis ingeniis quantoque ipsorum tormento in abyssum inanitatis & fictitiarum sententiarum praecipitetur? Tu vero quasi parergon acceptandum proponis. | Asphal. Is it hidden from you then [itaque], this very [thing] which you accept, by how great wits [ingeniis] and with how great torment of theirs it is hurled headlong into an abyss of emptiness [inanitatis] and of fictitious opinions [sententiarum]? But you propose [it] as a by-work [parergon] to be accepted. |
| Dico huius quam premimus telluris servitutem, Pisces, Aves, Animalia nobis esse subiecta, quantum irrideant? Ea dico quae Sylvestria dicimus, neque aliter quam ipsa morte nobis obnoxia experimur. | I mean [dico] the servitude [servitutem] of this earth [telluris] which we press [premimus], [that] Fishes, Birds, Animals are subject to us — how much do they laugh [it] to scorn [irrideant]? I mean those which we call Wild [Sylvestria], and [which] we experience [as] liable [obnoxia] to us not otherwise than by death itself. |
| Moles deinde & massa mixtorum quam tellurem dicimus, quantula nobis parte obvia est? Quot Eremi solis feris incoluntur? Quantae per calorem & frigus vastitates hominibus imperviae, quanto minus habitabiles sunt? | Then the bulk [moles] and mass of mixed [bodies] [mixtorum] which we call the earth [tellurem], in how tiny a part is it met with [obvia est] by us? How many Deserts [Eremi] are inhabited by wild beasts alone? How great wastes [vastitates], through heat and cold impassable to men, by how much less are they habitable? |
| Deorsum vero, quam nullam ipsius partem vel coniectura colligere potes sumus quales sint? Superficies quae nos sentit, decem aut circiter pedum profunditatem non excedit. Quam leviter ergo accipitur hunc nobis orbem servire? | Downward, however, how [is it that] of no part of it are we able [potes (= potis) sumus] even by conjecture to gather of what sort they are? The surface which feels us does not exceed a depth of ten or about [ten] feet. How lightly therefore is it accepted that this orb [orbem] serves us? |
| Tantumdem de ea parte quae aquis quasi vitreo obtegitur velo, sive dulcibus, sive amaris licet videre. Pelliculam sive sulcamus, sive radimus. Ignota nobis profunda, neque caeco illo bolidis invento pertentare conceditur, | Just as much one may see concerning that part which is covered over by waters as by a glassy veil, whether sweet or bitter [waters]. The little skin [pelliculam] we either furrow or scrape. The deeps [are] unknown to us, nor is it granted [us] to probe [pertentare] [them] with that blind invention of the sounding-lead [bolidis]; |
| & tu, accipiamus (inquis) quasi tuo hoc possemus munere, utinam possemus, Ereuni, gratos profecto agnitores haberes, postquam, ne tanti beneficii repensores esse possemus, evicisses. | and you — ‘let us accept’ (you say) — as if we could [do] this by your gift [munere]; would that we could, Ereunius: you would surely have grateful acknowledgers [agnitores], after you had made it prevail [evicisses] that we could not be repayers [repensores] of so great a benefit. |
| Ereu. Non mihi, sed omnium authori acceptum referant quod habeant, si uti velint. Si nolint, certe agnoscendum erat, & non intorquendae tot machinae ad ipsam coram pedibus positam scientiam nobis extorquendam. | Ereu. Not to me, but to the author of all things let them credit [acceptum referant] what they have, if they wish to use [it]. If they are unwilling, certainly it was to be acknowledged, and not so many engines [machinae] [were] to be twisted [intorquendae] for wresting [extorquendam] from us the very knowledge [scientiam] placed before [our] feet. |
| Fecit enim noster Andabata, ne de hac veritate dubitari possit; Id dico dono Dei esse, ut ea habeamus quae quotidiana experientia certum est nostris usibus inservire. | For our Andabata has brought it about that it cannot be doubted concerning this truth; I mean this, [that] it is by the gift of God that we have those [things] which by daily experience it is certain serve [inservire] our uses. |
| Cum enim ex ipsius ore nobis constet, omnia Deum intelligere, neque quicquam esse, cui praeter intellectum, ipsam etiam voluntatem non accommodaverit, & hoc usque ad ultimas circumstantias; non ultra dubitare possumus, quin si quid in his quae tractamus, nos nutrit, vel delectat, vel aliunde commodum est ad hoc quod desideramus & molimur, ex ipsa Dei {?praenotione}, & directa volitione τὸ nutrire, delectare, commoditatem praestare accipiat. | For since it is established [constet] for us out of his mouth that God understands all things, and that there is not anything to which, besides [His] understanding [intellectum], He has not fitted [accommodaverit] also [His] will itself, and this as far as the last circumstances; we can no further doubt but that, if anything among these [things] which we handle nourishes us, or delights, or is otherwise [aliunde] advantageous [commodum] for that which we desire and labour at [molimur], it receives from the very foreknowledge [praenotione] of God, and [from His] direct volition, the [τὸ] nourishing, delighting, affording advantage [commoditatem]. |
| Quid ergo dubii reliquum est quin ipsius dono cuncta acceperimus? si tamen accepimus. Accepimus autem, si quae protulisti, non eam vim habeant, quam praeclara illa ingenia existimant. | What of doubt therefore is left but that we have received all things by His gift? if, however, we have received [them]. And we have received [them], if the [things] which you have brought forward do not have that force [vim] which those illustrious wits [ingenia] reckon. |
| Quae si ad aedificandas scientias tantum adhiberent studii, quantum ad convellendas eas collocaverunt. Iamdudum subiissent iugum nostrum, quae nunc incassum defieri conquerimur. | Which [wits] [quae], if they applied as much of zeal to building up the sciences as they have placed [collocaverunt] in tearing them up. Long since would [those things] have gone under our yoke which we now complain in vain are wanting [defieri]. |
| Sed inspiciamus quam veritatem habeant quae sunt allata. Pisces, aves, animalia subiecta nobis esse irrident. Cur ita? Num piscis aliquis est in vastissimis illis aequoribus, cuius carne si commoda est, non vescimur? si incommoda, ad ea non abutimur ad quae nata esse videtur? | But let us look into what truth the [things] have which have been brought [forward]. Fishes, birds, animals laugh to scorn [irrident] [the claim] that they are subject to us. Why so? Is there any fish in those most vast seas [aequoribus] on whose flesh, if it is suitable [commoda], we do not feed? [or,] if unsuitable, [which] we do not use up [abutimur] for those [ends] for which it seems to have been born? |
| Dentes, cornua, tergora, pelles naves nostras onerant. Oleum etiam balaenarum maiori copia, quam olearum habemus vel nucum. Quid de copia dicam? Vivitur illis a magna humani generis parte. | Teeth, horns, hides [tergora], skins load our ships. Oil also of whales we have in greater abundance than of olives or of nuts. What shall I say of the abundance? It is lived on them by a great part of the human race. |
| De captura, quam facilis est! quam varia! Ingerunt sese hamis densius quam trahendis iis sufficiant nautae; retia multitudine rumpunt. Sunt in Mediterraneo alati escae utiles qui ipsas naves praetereuntes involant. | Of the catching [captura] — how easy it is! how varied! They thrust themselves upon the hooks more thickly than the sailors are sufficient for drawing them [in]; they burst the nets by [their] multitude. There are in the Mediterranean winged [fish] useful for food [escae] which fly into the very ships passing by. |
| Apud Indos puerulus Aethyops balaenam aut balaenis non longe inferius monstrum, quasi per ludum interficit. Canes & feles, & fibros, & alites piscatores accepimus. Cicures alimus, amores eorum cantamus, neque defuit qui ad carnificinam piscium operam adhiberet. | Among the Indians a little Ethiopian boy kills a whale, or a monster not far inferior to whales, as if by way of play. Dogs and cats, and beavers [fibros], and birds [alites] [as] fishers we have received. Tame [ones] [cicures] we feed, their loves we sing, nor has there been lacking one who applied the service [operam] of fishes to the executioner’s work [carnificinam]. |
| Aves quoque quas nostro iugo liberas sentimus, Aquilas & Herodios ab inaccessis rupibus detrahimus. Accipitres ex altissimis arboribus. Neque prodest aviculae quam de paradiso devolasse fabulantur, tellurem non tangere, galeros iis vestimus, scrinia ornamus. | Birds also, which we feel [to be] free from our yoke — Eagles and Gerfalcons [Herodios] we drag down from inaccessible crags; Hawks from the highest trees. Nor does it profit the little bird which they fable to have flown down from paradise, not to touch the earth [tellurem]: with them we clothe [our] hats [galeros], we adorn caskets [scrinia]. |
| Sed quod magis attonitos reddat, cicuratas coeli sui sodalibus immittimus, & spectamus quae propius accedere non possumus praelia, fructuque victoriae potimur ipsi. Alias undis committimus. Etiam dolosas facimus & artibus nostris Anates ad alios in retia nostra perducendos instruimus. Quin & ipsam naturam immutamus & cantuum eloquiique nostri participes facimus. | But — what may render [one] more astonished — [birds] made tame [cicuratas] we send in against the companions of their own sky, and we watch battles which we cannot approach more nearly, and we ourselves get possession of the fruit of the victory. Others we commit to the waves. We even make [them] deceitful, and by our arts we instruct Ducks for leading others into our nets. Nay, we even change [their] very nature and make [them] partakers of our songs and speech [eloquii]. |
| Quid ultra desiderare possumus? Animalibus eandem legem imposuimus, lupos, ursos, leones blandos & trepidos ad humanas minas videmus. Pantheras, Elephantos & omnia ferarum genera usque ad serpentes ex aliis regionibus & antiquitate audimus. Quam horum speciem curribus famulantem non vidit triumphans Roma? | What further can we desire? On animals we have imposed the same law: wolves, bears, lions we see fawning [blandos] and trembling at human threats. Panthers, Elephants and all kinds of wild beasts, as far as serpents, we hear [of] from other regions and from antiquity. What species [speciem] of these did triumphing Rome not see serving [famulantem] [its] chariots? |
| Non frigora Septentrionis inviaeque sylvae & paludes uros, alces, bisontes tutata sunt. Non Africani aestus non torrens Indiae Zona Ignotusque orbis Elephantos, Rhinocerotas, & similia naturae prodigia. Quid memorem serpentes in praeliis pro telis, cuneis hostium immissos? Quid decocta venena in antidota esse transmissa? | The colds of the North and the pathless woods and marshes have not protected aurochs [uros], elks, bisons. Not the African heats, not the torrid Zone of India and the Unknown orb [orbis] [have protected] Elephants, Rhinoceroses, and similar prodigies of nature. Why should I record serpents sent in battles, in place of missiles, against the wedges [cuneis] of the enemy? Why [record] that poisons, boiled down, have been carried over [transmissa] into antidotes? |
| Vis ut addam paradoxum ingens; at idem omni veritate fixum certumque? Antiqui morum Magistri haec ipsa vitio & luxui nobis verterunt. Ego vero, quod desint nobis aliqua, ingenti vitio donandum arbitror, & alte clamo, si qua in parte natura nobis utilis non est, avaritia & peccato nostro fieri. | Do you wish that I add a huge paradox; but the same fixed and certain with all truth? The ancient Masters of morals turned [verterunt] these very [things] to us [as] vice and luxury. I, however, judge that [the fact] that some things are lacking to us is to be put down [donandum] to a huge vice, and I cry aloud: if in any part nature is not useful to us, it comes about [fieri] by our avarice and sin. |
| [Margin, p. 386: Ex vitio hominum esse quod tota terra non sit habitata.] | [Margin, p. 386: That it is from the vice of men that the whole earth [terra] is not inhabited.] |
| Dedit nobis excolendam naturam benignus ipsius nostrique author: dedit incolendam terram. Nos malumus invicem caedibus fameque conficere, quam examina ad alias terras exercendas emittere. | The kindly author of it and of us gave us nature to be cultivated [excolendam]: He gave the earth [terram] to be inhabited [incolendam]. We prefer to consume [conficere] one another by slaughters and hunger, rather than to send out swarms [examina] for working [exercendas] other lands. |
| De angustis finibus laboramus, pericula omnia adimus, ipsam mortem irrumpimus; Interea squallent situ latissima regna, alia a paucissimis hominibus non tam coluntur, quam deflorantur. | Over narrow borders we toil, we go into all dangers, we break into death itself; Meanwhile the widest kingdoms lie squalid with neglect [situ], others are not so much cultivated [coluntur] by very few men as deflowered [deflorantur]. |
| Agminatim gentes carpentis vectae nunc hanc, nunc illam inquinant regionem, & quasi pollutam deserentes, aliam carpunt, & una natio multorum populorum haereditatem pravo usu desertam reddit. Ferociorum gentium confinia inculta iacent, barbarie proximorum naturae foetus suffocante. | In troops [agminatim] nations carried in wagons [carpentis] pollute now this, now that region, and deserting [it] as if polluted, they pluck [carpunt] another; and one nation renders the inheritance of many peoples deserted by depraved use. The borderlands of fiercer nations lie uncultivated, the barbarism of the nearest [peoples] suffocating the offspring [foetus] of nature. |
| Feruntur Sinae culta natio etiam flumina & alluentia maria multitudine sua implere. Tantumdem ubique fieret, nisi feris quam hominibus propiores haberemus consuetudines. | The Chinese [Sinae], a cultivated [culta] nation, are reported [feruntur] to fill even the rivers and the seas washing [them] with their multitude. Just as much would happen everywhere, unless we had customs nearer to wild beasts than to men. |
| Roma cum floreret, etiam subtus terram habitata fuisse memoratur, neque nobis latentia telluris viscera deforent incolatui, si ampliando humano generi magis quam eidem concidendo studeremus. | Rome, when it was flourishing, is recorded to have been inhabited even beneath the earth [terram]; nor would the hidden bowels of the earth [telluris] be lacking to us for habitation [incolatui], if we were zealous for enlarging the human race more than for cutting the same down. |
| Hinc, si mihi credis, ferarum indomita illa indoles & avium terror & fugacitas, ut experimentis navigatorum palam fit. Hinc vastae illae solitudines quas soli frigorique nostrorum vitiorum adulatione praetendimus. | Hence, if you believe me, [comes] that untamed disposition [indoles] of wild beasts and the terror and fugacity of birds, as becomes plain by the experiences [experimentis] of navigators. Hence those vast solitudes which, by flattery of our vices, we put forward [praetendimus] [as the work] of the sun and of the cold. |
| Nihil enim natura incultum est, sed nostra desidia vel paucitate syrtes ipsae & arenarum fluctus infusis undis durescerent & colerentur. Rupes sylvis tegerentur vel intecta cederent, si in illas sedes hominum multitudo commigraret. | For nothing is uncultivated by nature, but by our idleness or fewness: the Syrtes themselves and the billows of sands would, with waters poured in, harden and be cultivated. Rocks would be covered with woods, or, uncovered [intecta], would yield, if a multitude of men migrated into those seats. |
| Sylvescunt & arent campi, quia non est qui excolat & mundus ipse iam numerabilitati hominum non necessarius abit in situm & vilitatem. Neque superficies tantum; sed & ipsa viscera. Millesimam semidiametri terreni partem metallis quaerendis nondum pertudimus. | The fields run to wood [sylvescunt] and are parched, because there is no one who may cultivate [them], and the world itself, not necessary now to the [mere] countable number [numerabilitati] of men, goes off into neglect [situm] and cheapness [vilitatem]. Nor the surface only; but also the very bowels. The thousandth part of the earth’s semidiameter we have not yet beaten through [pertudimus] in seeking metals. |
| Emicet tantummodo a centro fulgor (ut ille canit) ab auro videbis lucem datam manibus, & strata per ipsos inferos platea, utrumque mundum communicare. | Let only a gleam flash forth from the centre (as that [poet] sings) from gold: you will see light given to [our] hands, and, a street [platea] paved through the very lower regions [inferos], both worlds communicate [communicare]. |
| Interea querimur decempedam altum esse quo fruimur solum: Ignorari cava pelagi & fluminum speluncas, ibi scilicet ubi nullae sunt bacciferae conchae, vel saxosae plantae, quas nulla maris altitudo protegere potest. | Meanwhile we complain that the soil which we enjoy is a ten-foot rod [decempedam] deep: that the hollows of the open sea and the caves of rivers are unknown — there, namely, where there are no berry-bearing [bacciferae] shells, or stony plants, which no depth of the sea can protect. |
| Excogitetur tantum quomodo luxuriae serviat, & vel civitates integras in profundo consitas cito dabimus. Ut constet quicquid in hac massa est, industriae humanae patere, & paucitate vel barbarie humana a nostris vitiis defendi. | Let it only be thought out how it may serve luxury, and we shall quickly yield [dabimus] even whole cities planted in the deep. So that it may be established [constet] that whatever is in this mass lies open to human industry, and is defended from our vices [only] by human fewness or barbarism. |
| Maneat itaque a Deo nobis omnia donata, & omnia in usum etiam nostrum cedere, nusquam Deo vel natura manum substrahente ad dandum; sed ubi parcius naturam gustamus, nobismet ipsis largitati naturae modum limitemque praefigentibus. | Let it remain therefore [that] all things [have been] given to us by God, and [that] all things also go over [cedere] into our use, God or nature nowhere withdrawing the hand from giving; but where we taste nature more sparingly, [it is] we our very selves setting beforehand [praefigentibus] a measure [modum] and limit to the largess of nature. |
| Adiice his solum hominem (si solus cognitione & ratione praeditus est) usus & dominii potestatem habere: solumque dignum esse qui finis rationem habeat, cum caetera omnia non nisi instrumentorum numero habenda sint. | Add to these [that] man alone (if he alone is endowed with cognition and reason [ratione]) has the power of use and of dominion [dominii]: and [that he] alone is worthy to have the character [rationem] of an end [finis], since all the rest are to be held not otherwise than in the number of instruments. |
| Neque bonum, neque malum sentientia, neque in quem quaeque usum nata sint, dispicientia, neque arbitrii libertate sibi ipsis nedum aliis imperantia. | [Being things] neither sensing good, nor evil, nor discerning [dispicientia] for what use each are born, nor by liberty of choice [arbitrii] commanding themselves, much less [nedum] others. |
| Cum itaque solus homo dominari possit, etiam soli data sunt quibus dominetur; De hac autem hominis dignitate, etsi ipsa res satis manifestam faciat, certe inter nos convenire debet: neque lis contra naturam moveri. | Since therefore man alone can exercise dominion [dominari], to [him] alone also have been given [the things] over which he may exercise dominion; And concerning this dignity of man, although the thing itself makes [it] manifest enough, certainly it ought to be agreed among us: and no suit [lis] [ought] to be moved against nature. |
| Asphal. Habent iidem Academici vel potius sceptici multa quae contra hanc hominis dignitatem afferant. Sed ego contra naturam non ausim disputare. | Asphal. The same Academics, or rather sceptics, have many [things] which they may bring against this dignity of man. But I would not dare to dispute against nature. |
| Hoc unum tamen silere non possim: si vera sunt quae tanta copia de cura supremi numinis circa hominem, totiusque nostri orbis donatione illi facta diximus. Hoc mihi praecipuum videtur fuisse curandum ut libera illa imperii potestate in se ius haberet aeque atque in corpora sibi supposita. | This one [thing] however I could not keep silent: if the [things] are true which we have said with such abundance concerning the care of the supreme deity [numinis] about man, and concerning the donation made to him of our whole orb [orbis]. This seems to me to have been the chief [thing] to be cared for: that by that free power of command [imperii] he should have right [ius] over himself equally as over the bodies placed under him. |
| Hoc autem exclusisti prae nimia cura: Si enim ita summo Imperatori subiecta sit libertas nostra, ut singuli actus nostri usque ad minimas circumstantias non praecogniti modo; sed & praedefiniti sint, quae ultra electio, quae libertas nobis reliqua facta est? sine qua tamen nihil caeteris creaturis praestare videtur tam laudatum genus nostrum. | But this you have shut out through too much care: For if our liberty is so subject to the supreme Commander [Imperatori] that our single acts, as far as the least circumstances, are not only foreknown [praecogniti], but also predefined [praedefiniti], what choice [electio] further, what liberty has been left [reliqua facta est] to us? without which, however, our so praised race seems to excel the other creatures in nothing. |
| Ereu. Ulcus ipsum tangis, Asphali, quo plura iam saecula laboraverunt, neque nostrum caeteris minus, si non & magis. Neque ego hic chirurgicas manus applicare auderem, nisi scirem cum quibus agendum habeam, & quod Andabatam molliorem aliquantulum hodie patiar quam hesterno die. | Ereu. You touch the very ulcer, Asphalius, from which very many [plura] ages have already suffered [laboraverunt], and ours not less than the rest, if not even more. Nor would I dare here to apply surgical hands, unless I knew with whom I have to deal, and that I shall find [patiar] Andabata somewhat softer today than on yesterday’s day. |
| Precor idcirco patientiam vestram ut me meo leni passu procedere concedatis, neque leviter obturbetis, donec conquievero. | I beg therefore your patience, that you grant me to proceed at my own gentle pace, and do not lightly interrupt [obturbetis], until I shall have come to rest [conquievero]. |

## Words and phrases with alternative or special meanings

- ***parergon*** — rendered “by-work [parergon]”. Greek πάρεργον: side-issue, subsidiary work, ornament. Asphalius complains Ereunius treats a contested thesis as an incidental.
- ***tellus / terra*** — rendered “earth [tellus] / earth [terra]”. tellus = the globe, land; terra = earth, land, soil. Kept distinct per glossary; the margin and knot title use terra, the speeches mostly tellus.
- ***mixta (moles mixtorum)*** — rendered “mixed [bodies] [mixtorum]”. compounds, mixed bodies. Aristotelian/scholastic mixta: bodies compounded of the elements.
- ***potes sumus*** — rendered “are we able [potes (= potis) sumus]”. potis sumus = possumus. Archaic/poetic potis sum; printed ‘potes’.
- ***bolis, bolidis*** — rendered “sounding-lead [bolidis]”. Greek βολίς: plummet, sounding-lead. The sounding-line, ‘blind’ because it reports depth without sight.
- ***acceptum referre*** — rendered “credit [acceptum referant]”. enter as received; attribute, owe to. Roman book-keeping idiom.
- ***evincere*** — rendered “made it prevail [evicisses]”. prove conclusively; win by argument; overcome. Here Asphalius’ irony: Ereunius has so ‘proved’ the gift that it leaves no room for repayment. Glossary had ‘convict’ (D3-N12) for a different construction.
- ***praenotio*** — rendered “foreknowledge [praenotione]”. pre-notion, prior notion; foreknowledge. Partly obscured in the scan; scholastic: God’s knowledge prior to the thing. Cf. praecognitio below.
- ***τὸ nutrire, delectare, commoditatem praestare*** — rendered “the [τὸ] nourishing, delighting, affording advantage”. Greek article used to substantivise Latin infinitives. A scholastic habit (cf. ‘τὸ esse’) to make the infinitive a noun object of accipiat.
- ***commodum / commoditas*** — rendered “advantageous / advantage [commoditatem]”. convenient, suitable; convenience, utility. Distinct from utilitas (utility) in the glossary.
- ***molior*** — rendered “labour at [molimur]”. strive, contrive, set in motion.
- ***abuti*** — rendered “use up [abutimur]”. use fully, consume; misuse, abuse. Classical sense ‘use to the full’, not moral abuse.
- ***defieri*** — rendered “are wanting [defieri]”. be lacking, fail (passive of deficio). Rare archaic form (Plautus).
- ***cicur / cicurare*** — rendered “tame [ones] [cicures] / made tame [cicuratas]”. tame, domesticated. Roman fishponds kept tame fish; trained hawks.
- ***ad carnificinam piscium operam adhibere*** — rendered “applied the service of fishes to the executioner’s work”. or: ‘applied effort to the butchery of fishes’. Very likely Vedius Pollio, who threw slaves to his lampreys (Seneca, De ira 3.40; Pliny NH 9.77). See notes.
- ***herodius (Herodios)*** — rendered “Gerfalcons [Herodios]”. a large bird of prey (heron? gerfalcon). Vulgate Ps. 103:17 ‘herodii domus’; in Renaissance falconry = gerfalcon.
- ***avicula quam de paradiso devolasse fabulantur*** — rendered “the little bird which they fable to have flown down from paradise”. bird of paradise. Legend that it had no feet and never touched the ground; plumes used on hats.
- ***Anates (dolosae)*** — rendered “Ducks [made] deceitful”. decoy ducks. Duck-decoys used to lure wild fowl into nets.
- ***species*** — rendered “species [speciem]”. kind; appearance. Here logical/natural kind.
- ***urus, alces, bison*** — rendered “aurochs [uros], elks, bisons”.  Caesar, BG 6.26–28 (Hercynian forest).
- ***cuneus*** — rendered “wedges [cuneis]”. wedge-shaped battle formation. Military; the serpents allude to Hannibal’s pots of snakes thrown at Eumenes’ fleet (Nepos, Hannibal 10–11).
- ***vitio vertere*** — rendered “turned … [as] vice [vitio … verterunt]”. impute as a fault. Classical idiom; the ‘ancient masters of morals’ = Stoic/Roman moralists (Seneca, Pliny) who condemned luxury.
- ***donare (vitio)*** — rendered “put down [donandum] to a vice”. give, present; ascribe. Unusual: ‘donare’ used for ‘attribute/impute’, perhaps by analogy with vitio dare.
- ***situs*** — rendered “neglect [situ / situm]”. position, site; disuse, rust, mould, neglect. Classical ‘situs’ = decay from disuse; not ‘site’ here.
- ***carpere*** — rendered “pluck [carpunt]”. pluck, crop, graze; wear away. Pun with carpentis (wagons); nomadic peoples ‘graze’ regions.
- ***foetus (naturae)*** — rendered “offspring [foetus]”. offspring, produce, fruit. Natural produce of the land.
- ***incolatus*** — rendered “habitation [incolatui]”. dwelling, residence (as sojourner). Late/legal Latin.
- ***praetendere*** — rendered “put forward [praetendimus]”. allege as pretext; stretch before. We blame sun and cold for wastes due to our vices.
- ***syrtes*** — rendered “Syrtes”. the Syrtes (sandbanks off Libya); quicksands. Classical proverb of barren sands.
- ***numerabilitas*** — rendered “the countable number [numerabilitati]”. numerability, i.e. smallness of number. Very rare coinage; the world is more than the few men need.
- ***decempeda*** — rendered “ten-foot rod [decempedam]”. a measuring rod ten feet long. Roman surveyor’s rod; picks up Asphalius’ ‘ten feet’.
- ***bacciferae conchae / saxosae plantae*** — rendered “berry-bearing shells / stony plants”. pearl-oysters / corals. Ironical: where pearls and coral are, no depth protects them.
- ***Emicet … a centro fulgor (ut ille canit)*** — rendered “Let only a gleam flash forth from the centre (as that [poet] sings)”.  Unidentified poetic allusion; possibly a free reminiscence (cf. Ovid, Met. 1.138–140 on mining ‘ventured into the bowels of the earth’). See notes.
- ***inferi*** — rendered “lower regions [inferos]”. the underworld; the dead. Here the earth’s depths, with a classical underworld colour.
- ***ratio finis*** — rendered “the character [rationem] of an end”. the formal nature/account of an end. Scholastic: only rational beings are ends in themselves; others are instruments (cf. Aquinas, SCG III.112).
- ***dominium / dominari*** — rendered “dominion / exercise dominion”. ownership, lordship. Scholastic doctrine of dominium (Gen. 1:28) as proper to rational beings.
- ***liberum arbitrium / arbitrii libertas*** — rendered “liberty of choice [arbitrii]”. free will, free decision. Glossary: liberum arbitrium = free choice.
- ***Academici vel potius sceptici*** — rendered “Academics, or rather sceptics”.  The New Academy (Carneades) vs. Pyrrhonian sceptics; Montaigne’s Apologie (II.12) attacked human pre-eminence over animals.
- ***numen*** — rendered “deity [numinis]”. divine power, godhead.
- ***imperium / Imperator*** — rendered “command [imperii] / Commander [Imperatori]”. rule, command; emperor, general. Cf. glossary imperium (rationis) = command; God as ‘summus Imperator’.
- ***praecognitus / praedefinitus*** — rendered “foreknown / predefined”.  The Molinist/Thomist controversy on God’s foreknowledge and predetermination of free acts; glossary praedefinitio = predefinition.
- ***patior*** — rendered “find [patiar]”. suffer, endure, experience. Idiomatic: ‘I shall find him gentler’.
- ***conquiescere*** — rendered “come to rest [conquievero]”. rest, pause, finish.
