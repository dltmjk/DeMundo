# De Mundo — Dialogue II (De forma mundi, seu de motu magnorum corporum), Knot xii, part 2 of 2

*Latin title:* Nodus XII. Lunam non moveri immediate a sole  
*English title:* Knot XII. That the Moon is not moved immediately by the sun  
*Pages:* PDF pp. 259–267 = printed pp. 248–256 (from Ereunius’ ‘Et ego fortassis secus quam oportuerat locutus sum’ on p. 248 to Asphalius’ ‘…theoriam componentes.’ on p. 256, where Knot xiii begins).

## Dialogue II, Knot xii, part 2 (printed pp. 248–256)

| Latin | English |
|---|---|
| [p. 248] Ereu. Et ego fortassis secus quam oportuerat locutus sum. Rursus itaque peto num vehementiorem horum elementorum motum, non sine condensatione mobilium contingere [p. 249] percepisti? undecumque sit is effectus. | [p. 248] Ereu. And I perhaps spoke otherwise than I ought to have. Again, therefore, I ask whether you have perceived [that] a more vehement motion of these elements does not happen without condensation of the mobiles [mobilium] [p. 249], from wherever this effect may be? |
| Andab. Rem dicis. | Andab. You speak the thing [Rem dicis]. |
| Ereu. Ergo sphaerae illius ex terra compactoque aere conflatae eas partes, quae immediate sub Ecliptica sunt, esse densiores constat. | Ereu. Therefore it is established [constat] that those parts of that sphere, blown together [conflatae] out of earth and compacted air, which are immediately under the Ecliptic, are denser. |
| Andab. Non inficior. | Andab. I do not deny [it] [inficior]. |
| Ereu. Neque hoc, grave quod in densiori connaturaliter sedet, idem in leviori demergi. | Ereu. Nor [do you deny] this: [that] a heavy [thing] which connaturally sits in a denser [medium], the same is sunk down in a lighter. |
| Andab. Neque hoc. | Andab. Nor this. |
| Ereu. Agedum igitur, luna quae sub Ecliptica altior a terra a densis telluris vaporibus agitur, eadem in summis distantiis, quia minus agitatum aerem ac proinde tenuiorem invenit, demergitur, & propiore terris semita devolvitur. Novisti autem circulum quo medio propior est; eo etiam minorem esse. Cum itaque adversa lunae resistentia nihil crescat, remotior enim est a sole, immo fortassis minor evadat, quo altius deprimitur inter effluvia telluris, facile est videre unde crescat ea velocitas quae lunae attribuitur. Duas siquidem causas habes brevitatem depressioris versus centrum circuli & vehementiam motus sub Ecliptica quae velocius movens facit deficientiam esse minorem & videri lunam tardius moveri. | Ereu. Come on then: the moon, which under the Ecliptic, higher from the earth [terra], is driven [agitur] by the dense vapours of the earth [telluris], the same [moon] in the highest distances, because it finds air less agitated and therefore thinner, is sunk down, and is rolled down along a path nearer to the earths [terris]. But you know [that] a circle, by how much nearer it is to the middle [centre], by so much also is smaller. Since therefore the adverse resistance to the moon grows in nothing, for it is more remote from the sun, nay perhaps turns out smaller the more deeply [altius] it is pressed down among the effluvia of the earth [telluris], it is easy to see whence grows that speed [velocitas] which is attributed to the moon. For you have two causes: the shortness of the more depressed circle [depressioris … circuli] toward the centre, and the vehemence of the motion under the Ecliptic, which, moving [it] more swiftly, makes the deficiency [deficientiam] be smaller and the moon seem to be moved more slowly. |
| [p. 250] [Margin: Lunam in quadraturis esse depressiorem.] Andab. Ergo lunam modo plus modo minus a tellure dissitam fateris? | [p. 250] [Margin: That the moon in the quadratures is more depressed.] Andab. Therefore you confess the moon [to be] now more, now less set apart [dissitam] from the earth [tellure]? |
| Ereu. Testantur Astronomi, sed prae caeteris {?terret} me nostras Gasco, quem heri commemini, qui suo instrumento tam explicate profitetur in singulos dies diametri lunaris quam lucis in eadem incrementa notare. | Ereu. The Astronomers testify [it], but before the rest our countryman [nostras] Gasco [Gascoigne], whom I mentioned yesterday, {?alarms} [terret] me, who with his instrument professes so explicitly [explicate] to note, day by day [in singulos dies], the increments of the lunar diameter as [those] of the light in the same [moon]. |
| Andab. Astronomi tantundem de sole pronunciant. | Andab. The Astronomers pronounce just as much concerning the sun. |
| Ereu. Non video antiquiores constanter hoc asserere, & recentiorum plerumque Septentrionaliores sunt observationes: quibus timeo ne quid illudat hyemalis crassior aer. Video quoque subesse, unde de vaporibus ex Oceano sub Capricorni regno in altum copiosius sublatis aliquid suspicer: sed dubiis libens abstineo manum. | Ereu. I do not see the more ancient [astronomers] constantly assert this, and of the more recent [ones] the observations are for the most part more Northern: in which I fear lest the wintry thicker air play some trick [illudat]. I see also [that something] underlies [subesse], whence I might suspect something concerning vapours raised more copiously on high out of the Ocean under the reign of Capricorn: but from doubtful [things] I willingly withhold [my] hand. |
| Asphal. Affirmate itaque de gravitate lunae sententiam pronuncias, cum hactenus centrum quoddam suis esse effluviis ab ipso te me accepisse credidissem: nunc modo altius, modo depressius gravitatis vi ferri asserere visus es. | Asphal. Affirmatively [Affirmate], therefore, you pronounce an opinion [sententiam] concerning the heaviness [gravitate] of the moon, whereas hitherto I had believed I had received from you yourself [that] it is a sort of centre to its own effluvia: now you have seemed to assert [that] it is carried now higher, now lower [depressius] by the force [vi] of heaviness. |
| Ereu. Mentem meam attigisti. Non quod id quod prius dixi, nunc abdicem; sed quod utriusque vim astruere coner. Cum enim ut supra indicavi, gravitas non ignota quaedam naturae conditio, sed constans quidem motus [p. 251] extrinseca naturae lege coortus sit; necessum est ea omnia quae intra effluvia telluris depacta sunt, ad eandem motum hunc habere asseram, hoc est, gravitare, quo cum tamen componi potest, aliquod corpus respectu suorum effluviorum centri rationem habere. Sicut in succino & universim in Electricis fortasse contingit; quae tamen non propterea gravia esse desinunt, & ad communem terram constanter ferri. | Ereu. You have touched my mind. Not that that which I said before I now renounce [abdicem]; but that I try to build up [astruere] the force [vim] of both. For since, as I indicated above, heaviness [gravitas] is not some unknown condition of nature, but indeed a constant motion [p. 251] arisen by an extrinsic law of nature, it is necessary [necessum] — I would assert [asseram] — [that] all those things which are fixed down [depacta] within the effluvia of the earth [telluris] have this motion to the same [ad eandem] [point], that is, to gravitate [gravitare]; with which nevertheless it can be compounded [componi] [= is compatible] that some body, in respect of its own effluvia, has the nature [rationem] of a centre. As perhaps happens in amber [succino] and universally in Electric [things]; which nevertheless do not on that account cease to be heavy, and to be carried constantly to the common earth [terram]. |
| Asphal. Male me habet inolitum tibi vitium (an virtutem dicam) ut praeterea parerga colloquiis seu responsis addere non sustineas; & iniqua mente libellum quem toties polliceris, expecto. Interea vide ne teipsum graviori quam expectes vel profundiori puteo demerseris. Vide enim, si luna gravitet, & terram versus nixu suo vergat, quantam molem, quantum molarem inani suspendas, [Margin: Cur luna non cadat in terram.] quem quo aureo demisso coelitus fune catenave nexum tenebis, ne subito lapsus nos funestius quam solent abrupta Alpium cacumina obruat? Hoc te nodo quomodo liberes opperior, fateor enim me initium nullum cogitare posse. | Asphal. It sits ill with me [Male me habet], the vice grown into you (or shall I call it a virtue) that you do not bear to add by-works [parerga] besides to the colloquies or answers; and with an uneven mind I await the little book which you so often promise. Meanwhile see that you do not sink yourself in a heavier — than you expect — or a deeper well. For see: if the moon gravitates [gravitet], and inclines toward the earth by its own straining [nixu], what a bulk [molem], what a millstone [molarem] you suspend in the void [inani]; [Margin: Why the moon does not fall onto the earth.] which, bound by what golden rope or chain let down from heaven, will you hold, lest, suddenly fallen, it overwhelm us more fatally [funestius] than the broken-off summits of the Alps are wont? How you may free yourself from this knot [nodo] I wait; for I confess that I can think of no beginning [initium]. |
| Ereu. Aiunt male claudicare planum inter claudos: & experior mihi ad peritos rem geri. Eia igitur non vides lembos & naves propterea non mergi quia aerem quasi ventre conclusum [p. 252] gestant? Pisces hoc idem a natura feruntur accepisse, ut in ventriculum ad hoc ipsum a natura ipsis accommodatum tantum aeris assumant, quantum ad hoc, ut illo loci quo appetitus instigat, vel descendant vel efferantur, necesse est: & sic se moveant figantque pro libitu. Avibus in aere extra corpus est quod pisces intus continent, alis enim compressis cadunt, passis elevantur, obstetricante motu. Una omnium est ratio. Si medium quod gravitate scindendum est ad descensum, appendiculis levioribus corpori graviori fixis adaequas, stabis ubicumque, si excesseris, ad tenuiora sublevaberis. Cogita modo effluvia lunae ad ipsum lunae corpus quasi depacta firmiter esse (quod non dubitem te credere) adeo inquam firmiter ut ipsa obnitentes telluris vapores distringere non valeant. Vides statim ipsi lunae cum his vel standum esse vel movendum. Cogita nunc ex ea mole quae effluviorum terrestrium est, quanta lunarium debet esse. Certe pro corporum ratione tanto diffusiorem quanto magis obnoxiam soli, prae tellure lunam arbitramur. Vides itaque aptatas lunae alas. Si adhuc trepidas, cogita sublimes montes & depressas valles. His adiice vacuas intrinsecus cavernas quas natura montestris suspectas nobis facit. Solent enim non raro ebullire montes incendiis subterraneis [p. 253] fermentato solo. Haec dum manent, ruinam coeli ne extimescas, neque capturam alaudarum speres. | Ereu. They say an impostor [planum] limps badly among the lame: and I experience that the matter is conducted for me before the skilled. Well then [Eia], do you not see that skiffs [lembos] and ships are on that account not sunk, because they carry air shut up as if in a belly [p. 252]? Fishes are reported [feruntur] to have received this same [thing] from nature, that into a little belly [ventriculum] fitted by nature to them for this very [purpose] they take in as much air as is necessary for this: that in that [place] of place [illo loci] whither appetite urges, they either descend or are carried up: and so they move and fix themselves at pleasure. For birds, that is outside the body in the air which fishes contain inside; for with wings compressed they fall, with [wings] spread they are raised, motion acting as midwife [obstetricante]. Of all [these] the reason [ratio] is one. If you equal the medium, which is to be cut by heaviness [gravitate] for the descent, by lighter little appendages fixed to a heavier body, you will stand wherever [you are]; if you exceed [it], you will be lifted up to thinner [regions]. Think only [that] the effluvia of the moon are, as it were, firmly fixed down [depacta] to the very body of the moon (which I would not doubt that you believe), so firmly, I say, that the vapours of the earth [telluris] themselves, striving against [them], are not able to pull [them] apart [distringere]. You see at once that the moon itself must either stand or be moved with these. Think now, out of that bulk [mole] which is [that] of the terrestrial effluvia, how great [that] of the lunar ought to be. Certainly in proportion to the bodies [pro corporum ratione] we judge the moon, compared with [prae] the earth [tellure], [to be] by so much more diffuse by how much more exposed to the sun. You see therefore wings fitted to the moon. If you still tremble, think of the lofty mountains and depressed valleys. To these add the caverns empty within, which the mountainous [montestris] nature [of the moon] makes suspected by us. For not seldom mountains are wont to boil out with subterranean fires, [p. 253] the soil having been fermented. While these remain, do not dread the ruin of the heaven, nor hope for the capture of larks. |
| Asphal. Euge, euge, Ereuni, morere (ut ille Olympionicarum Patri dicebat) nunquid enim in coelum cogitas ascendere? mihi vero Poetae canticum assumo, | Asphal. Well done, well done, Ereunius, die (as that man used to say to the Father of the Olympic victors): for surely you do not [nunquid] think to ascend into heaven? But for myself I take up the Poet's song: |
| Ex illo Corydon, Corydon est tempore nobis. | From that time Corydon, Corydon is [the one] for us. |
| Ereu. Nequicquam canis, Asphali, Non sic festinanter prolatum, non maturissimo authoris sensu concoctum theorema ante solem & pulverem laude & honore dignandum est. Cum solemnis doctorum aliquot saeculorum calculus sanciverit veritatem, tunc tuas congere acclamationes. | Ereu. In vain you sing, Asphalius. Not is a theorem thus hastily brought forth, not digested [concoctum] by the ripest sense of the author, to be deemed worthy of praise and honour before the sun and the dust. When the solemn vote [calculus] of the learned of some ages has ratified [sanciverit] the truth, then heap up your acclamations. |
| Andab. Etiam tu insanis, Asphali, ut huiusmodi commentis adhibere possis fidem, quae omnes fabulas Lucianicas prodigio & miraculo superant. | Andab. You too are mad, Asphalius, that you can apply belief [fidem] to fictions [commentis] of this kind, which surpass all the Lucianic fables in prodigy and marvel? |
| Ereu. Si fidei idoneam poscis basim, Andabata, repete animo quae pressimus vestigia. Vide num ex intimis naturae penetralibus hausta sint. Vide num per gradus deducta, num non in singulis institum sit, ne quid praeterlaberetur quod meritam vocaret in totum textum calumniam? Una excepta gravitatis indole, quam deprecatus sum. Vide rursus quam ad amussim omnia lunae Phaenomenis respondeant. Quomodo causae pateant [p. 254] cur luna eundem vultum nobis offerat, cur circum tellurem versetur. Motum annuum, menstruum, latitudinem, anomaliam, depressionem & elevationem vides quasi sponte naturae exorta ab humo prodiisse. Quam facile, quam naturaliter, quam non implicite? | Ereu. If you demand a basis [basim] suitable for belief, Andabata, go back in mind over the footprints which we have pressed. See whether they are drawn out of the inmost recesses of nature. See whether [they are] led down by degrees, whether it was not insisted [institum] upon in single [points], lest anything slip past which might call a deserved cavil [calumniam] upon the whole web [textum]? One [thing] excepted, the disposition [indole] of heaviness, which I have begged off [deprecatus sum]. See again how exactly [ad amussim] all [things] answer to the Phaenomena of the moon. How the causes lie open [p. 254] why the moon offers the same face to us, why it turns about the earth [tellurem]. The annual [and] monthly motion, the latitude, the anomaly, the depression and elevation, you see as if, sprung up by the spontaneity [sponte] of nature, to have come forth from the ground [ab humo]. How easily, how naturally, how not intricately [non implicite]? |
| Andab. Serione triumphum canis? Vide quam cito difflentur quae dixisti? Terrae vapores cum graviores & fortiores sint & contra nitantur lunae vaporibus, eos penitus discutient, & implumem brevi lunam Icario lapsu novum Oceano nomen daturam expectandum est. | Andab. Do you sing a triumph in earnest? See how quickly the things you have said are blown away [difflentur]? The vapours of the earth [Terrae], since they are heavier and stronger and strive against the vapours of the moon, will thoroughly shake them apart [discutient], and it is to be expected [that] the moon, featherless in a short [time], by an Icarian fall will give a new name to the Ocean. |
| [Margin: Aerem aeri innatare.] Ereu. Olim fortasse, sed nondum te vivo & me: quantumvis enim modus rei non sit explicatu facilis, reapse tamen experimur fluores Magneticos, Electricos, Aromaticos, ipsam denique lucem inter tot aeris, & ventorum mutationes constantem motum obtinere. Adiice nihil in iis confiniis per impetum agi posse, sed constanti quodam tenore & suavi habitudine: & neque vapores terrestres prope lunae corpus accedere, neque lunares ad hanc motuum inconstantiam quam circa tellurem persentiscimus. Adde celeritatem motus lunae, & quomodo vel nostra corpora in aqua vel ingentium avium in aere, & paegnia (quae pueri miluos appellant [p. 255] & adverso vento per sublime fune conducunt) motu famulante mutatisque quasi equis sustententur, neque multum a ruina lunae formidabis. Sed totam hanc causam extra litem statuunt corpora oculis exposita. Vinum supernatare aquae molliter infusum, immo sine mixtione loca mutare per eosdem canales subtiliter docemus, oleum non nisi per vim tenuioribus miscetur, fluvii fluvios secant; immo eodem alveo immixti, & separati longo tractu feruntur, ipsum mare secant & transeunt, sibi mutuo inequitant: ut apud Cordubensem belli civilis historicum habes, | [Margin: That air swims in air.] Ereu. Some day perhaps, but not yet while you and I are alive: for however much the mode of the thing may not be easy of explication, yet in very fact [reapse] we experience [that] Magnetic, Electric, Aromatic flowings [fluores], [and] finally light itself, amid so many changes of air and of winds, obtain a constant motion. Add that nothing in those borderlands [confiniis] can be done by impetus [impetum], but by a certain constant tenor and gentle habitude [habitudine]: and that neither do the terrestrial vapours approach near the body of the moon, nor the lunar [ones] to this inconstancy of motions which we perceive thoroughly [persentiscimus] about the earth [tellurem]. Add the swiftness of the moon's motion, and how either our bodies in water or [those] of huge birds in the air, and the toys [paegnia] (which boys call kites [miluos] [p. 255], and lead through the lofty [air] by a cord against the wind) are sustained by motion serving [famulante] and as it were by changed horses; and you will not much dread the ruin of the moon. But bodies exposed to the eyes set this whole case outside the lawsuit [extra litem]. [That] wine, softly poured in, swims on top of water, nay without mixtion [mixtione] changes places through the same channels, we teach subtly; oil is not mixed with thinner [liquids] except by force; rivers cut rivers; nay, unmixed in the same bed [alveo], and separated, they are carried over a long tract, they cut the sea itself and pass across, they ride upon one another: as you have [it] in the Cordovan historian of the civil war: |
| Solus, in alterius nomen cum venerit undae, | Alone, when it has come into the name of another water, |
| Defendit Titaresus aquas, lapsusque superne, | Titaresus defends [its] waters, and gliding above, |
| Gurgite Penei pro siccis utitur arvis. | Uses the flood of Peneus in place of dry fields. |
| Quid regeri potest quo minus aer aeri & vapores vaporibus innatent, aequali se privilegio ab inquinatione protegentes? | What can be retorted [regeri], to prevent air swimming in air and vapours in vapours, protecting themselves by an equal privilege from pollution [inquinatione]? |
| Asphal. At necdum de nodis seu Draconis capite caudaque eorumque motu quicquam expressisti. | Asphal. But not yet have you expressed anything concerning the nodes [nodis], or the Dragon's head and tail, and their motion. |
| Ereu. Neque enim opus est. Cum enim singulis mensibus singula tellus oberret signa, neque eam luna destituat, velut comes in tantas officiosa vias (ut Poeta loquitur) ipsa quoque Tellurem spatio non multum inaequali semel cingat, necessum est intersectiones, E-[p. 256]clipticae quas facit luna in eas partes incidere quas tellus simul occupet: & quod sequitur, motum quemdam ordinatum in Ecliptica tenere. Neque amplius mysterii est. Neque alio itineris authore vel duce opus est. | Ereu. For neither is there need. For since in single months the earth [tellus] wanders through single signs, and the moon does not forsake it, [but], like a dutiful [officiosa] companion on such great ways (as the Poet speaks), itself also girds the Earth [Tellurem] once in a space not much unequal, it is necessary [necessum] that the intersections of the E-[p. 256]cliptic which the moon makes fall into those parts which the earth [tellus] at the same time occupies: and, what follows, [that they] hold a certain ordered motion in the Ecliptic. Nor is there more of mystery. Nor is there need of another author or guide of the journey. |
| Asphal. Nonne & hoc te movet quod non ἀπόγεια tantum & περίγεια sed etiam ἀπογειότατα & περιγειότατα lunae assignant Astronomi, inaequalitatem ubique astruentes, propterea tot circulis lunae theoriam componentes. | Asphal. Does not this also move you, that the Astronomers assign to the moon not only apogees [ἀπόγεια] and perigees [περίγεια] but also furthest apogees [ἀπογειότατα] and nearest perigees [περιγειότατα], building up [astruentes] inequality everywhere, [and] on that account composing the theory [theoriam] of the moon out of so many circles? ## Words and phrases with alternative or special meanings

- ***deficientia*** — rendered “deficiency”. lag, falling-short; not eclipse The moon’s apparent eastward motion is, for White, a lag behind the earth-driven diurnal whirl; faster driving under the ecliptic makes the lag smaller (cf. defectus / deficere in D2-N03).
- ***dissitus*** — rendered “set apart”. distant Already in glossary; used of the moon’s varying distance from the earth.
- ***nostras Gasco*** — rendered “our countryman Gasco”. Gascoigne William Gascoigne (1612–44), inventor of the filar micrometer, already cited in D1-N16 (‘quem heri commemini’ = Dialogue I, the previous day).
- ***terret*** — rendered “alarms”. impresses, deters; possibly misprint (tenet? movet?) Reading clear in the print but odd in sense; Gascoigne’s measurements of the lunar diameter are what weigh with Ereunius.
- ***sub Capricorni regno*** — rendered “under the reign of Capricorn”. in the southern (winter-solstice) sign Astrological ‘reign’ of a sign; vapours from the southern ocean.
- ***depacta / depactus*** — rendered “fixed down”. planted, embedded Glossary rendering kept (D2-N05).
- ***gravitare*** — rendered “gravitate”. tend downward Neo-Latin verb; White defines heaviness as a constant motion from an extrinsic law of nature, not an occult quality.
- ***centri rationem habere*** — rendered “have the nature of a centre”. hold the role/account of a centre Ratio = formal character; reconciles the moon’s being a centre to its own effluvia with its heaviness toward the earth.
- ***succinum / Electrica*** — rendered “amber / Electric [things]”. Gilbert’s electrics Gilbert, De magnete II.2: bodies that attract like amber.
- ***parerga*** — rendered “by-works”. digressions, appendices Greek πάρεργα; Asphalius regrets White’s refusal to add digressions, and awaits his promised ‘libellus’.
- ***libellus quem toties polliceris*** — rendered “the little book which you so often promise”.  Probably the promised treatise (cf. D1-N08 ‘libellus de immortalitate animae’ = Digby / White).
- ***aureo demisso coelitus fune catenave*** — rendered “golden rope or chain let down from heaven”.  Homer, Iliad VIII.19–27, the golden chain of Zeus; a stock image of cosmic suspension.
- ***nodus*** — rendered “knot”. node (astronomical) Pun: Asphalius’ ‘hoc te nodo’ (this knot = difficulty) and later ‘de nodis’ (the moon’s nodes) play on White’s section-name.
- ***male claudicare planum inter claudos*** — rendered “an impostor limps badly among the lame”. planus = vagabond/cheat (who fakes lameness), or ‘flat-foot’ Proverb: you cannot fake before experts; Ereunius means he is arguing before the skilled.
- ***obstetricante motu*** — rendered “motion acting as midwife”. assisting Glossary obstetricari = act as midwife.
- ***ruinam coeli … capturam alaudarum*** — rendered “the ruin of the heaven … the capture of larks”.  Proverb ‘Quid si coelum ruat? — alaudae capientur’ (cf. Erasmus, Adagia): idle fear of the sky falling.
- ***natura montestris*** — rendered “mountainous nature”. montestris = of mountains (rare) Refers to the moon’s mountainous surface, known from the telescope.
- ***morere … Olympionicarum Patri*** — rendered “die … to the Father of the Olympic victors”.  Diagoras of Rhodes, told by a Spartan ‘Die, Diagoras, for you will not ascend to heaven’ (Cicero, Tusc. I.111; Plutarch, Pelopidas 34).
- ***Ex illo Corydon, Corydon est tempore nobis*** — rendered “From that time Corydon, Corydon is [the one] for us”.  Virgil, Eclogue 7.70: the victory verdict of the singing contest.
- ***ante solem & pulverem*** — rendered “before the sun and the dust”. in the open arena, under real trial Athletic/military image of testing in public.
- ***calculus*** — rendered “vote”. pebble; reckoning The pebble used in voting; ‘solemn vote of the learned of some ages’.
- ***fabulae Lucianicae*** — rendered “Lucianic fables”.  Lucian’s True History (voyage to the moon).
- ***indoles (gravitatis)*** — rendered “disposition”. nature, character Glossary rendering; White refuses to explain the inner nature of heaviness (‘deprecatus sum’).
- ***ad amussim*** — rendered “exactly”. by the mason’s rule Glossary.
- ***Icario lapsu novum Oceano nomen*** — rendered “by an Icarian fall … a new name to the Ocean”.  Icarus gave his name to the Icarian Sea (Ovid, Met. VIII.229–35).
- ***fluores*** — rendered “flowings”. emanations Glossary (D1-N16): kept apart from fluida.
- ***paegnia (quae pueri miluos appellant)*** — rendered “toys (which boys call kites)”. παίγνια; milvus = kite (bird) Flying kites as an example of being sustained by motion against the wind.
- ***mutatis quasi equis*** — rendered “as it were by changed horses”. relays Image of a post-relay: motion continuously renewed.
- ***extra litem statuere*** — rendered “set outside the lawsuit”. put beyond dispute Forensic idiom.
- ***Cordubensis belli civilis historicus*** — rendered “the Cordovan historian of the civil war”.  Lucan (born at Córdoba), Pharsalia VI.375–377, the Titaresus flowing over the Peneus.
- ***regeri*** — rendered “be retorted”. be thrown back Glossary regerere = retort.
