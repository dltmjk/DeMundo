## Dialogue III, Knot xvi, part 2 (printed pp. 411–420)

| Latin | English |
|---|---|
| Ereu. Ego vero quod in me est libens exequar. Tu modo quibus praeeo vestigiis, insiste. Et hoc primum mihi excute, in quonam bona vita consistat. Neque de ea bonitate loquor quae utilitatis est, ut bona vita nominetur ex eo quod magnum gloriae pondus in futuro saeculo operetur. Nemo enim est, ut opinor, qui virtutis & rationis tramitem non illum esse asserat, quo futurus divitiarum cumulus aggeratur: Sed vitam impraesentiarum appello bonam, ut Philosophi bonam quaerebant: quae sui fruitione foelicissimum in hoc saeculo hominem faciat, tanquam nulla de futuro spes foret. | Ereu. I indeed, what is in me, will gladly carry out [exequar]. Do you only tread upon [insiste] the footsteps in which I go before. And this first shake out [excute] for me: in what a good life consists [consistat]. Nor do I speak of that goodness which is of utility [utilitatis], so that a life be named good from this, that it works [operetur] a great weight of glory in the age to come [futuro saeculo]. For there is no one, as I opine, who would not assert the path [tramitem] of virtue and reason to be that by which the future heap of riches is piled up [aggeratur]: but I call good the life at present [impraesentiarum], as the Philosophers were seeking a good [life]: which by the fruition of itself [sui fruitione] would make a man most happy [foelicissimum] in this age [saeculo], as though there were no hope concerning the future. |
| Asphal. Si sic loqueris, quis eam caeteris praeferre ambigat, quae potentia praepolleat, honoribus emineat, amicitiis floreat, divitiis fulciatur, voluptatum omni genere semper vernet & virescat? | Asphal. If you speak thus, who would hesitate [ambigat] to prefer to the rest that [life] which prevails [praepolleat] in power, is eminent in honours, flourishes in friendships, is propped up [fulciatur] by riches, [and] always is in spring [vernet] and grows green in every kind of pleasures? |
| Ereu. Haec autem quorsum appetuntur? Potentia primo & divitiae? | Ereu. But these, to what end [quorsum] are they desired [appetuntur]? Power first, and riches? |
| Asphal. Non experiris quanta sit in benefaciendo iucunditas quae per potentiam & divitias acquiritur? praeter eam quae ex amicitiis habetur, quarum copia ex his pendet; aestimari vero & honorari quantum homini placeat, non alius noverit quam experti. | Asphal. Do you not experience how great is the pleasantness [iucunditas] in doing good which is acquired through power and riches? besides that which is had from friendships, the plenty [copia] of which hangs on these; and to be esteemed and honoured, how much it pleases a man, no other would know than [those who have] experienced [it] [experti]. |
| [Margin, p. 412: Homines omnia propter iucunditatem facere.] | [Margin, p. 412: That men do all [things] for the sake of pleasantness [iucunditatem].] |
| Ereu. Ut video ergo ad unam iucunditatem seu voluptatem redigitur tota huius vitae foelicitas; haec autem iucunditas plures partes habet, quatenus ex pluribus obiectis vel fontibus hauritur, quarum alii aliis fortasse nobiliorem & meliorem iucunditatem affundant: unus tamen omnium fructus est iucunditas. Neque mihi videtur absque ratione hoc esse; si enim iucunditas est ille motus animi qui potitionem boni propter se amati sequitur, seu a bono per se possesso fit in ipso animo; nihil autem praeter bonum possessum ad foelicitatem seu beatitudinem integrandam ingreditur: evidens prorsus manet vel iucunditatem, vel iucundum ipsam foelicitatem constituere: ut non male cecinerit Epicureus, cuius iam regnum invadimus, nonne videmus nil aliud sibi naturam latrare nisi utque corpore seiunctus dolor absit, mensque fruatur iucundo sensu cura semota metuque. Caetera ad explicationem adiuncta sunt; quamdiu enim mens iucundo sensu fruitur, neque dolor aliquis esse potest, neque metus, vel cura. Haec itaque sic habento. Iucunditas modo mentis ex quo habetur vel quibus impeditur? | Ereu. As I see, therefore, to one pleasantness [iucunditatem] or pleasure [voluptatem] is reduced the whole happiness [foelicitas] of this life; but this pleasantness has more [than one] part, inasmuch as from more [than one] objects or fountains it is drawn [hauritur], of which some perhaps pour in [affundant] a nobler and better pleasantness than others [alii aliis]: yet the one fruit of all is pleasantness. Nor does this seem to me to be without reason; for if pleasantness is that motion of the mind [animi] which follows the getting-possession [potitionem] of a good loved for its own sake, or is made in the mind [animo] itself by a good possessed through itself [per se]; and nothing besides the good possessed enters into making whole [integrandam] happiness or beatitude: it remains altogether evident that either pleasantness or the pleasant [iucundum] constitutes happiness itself: so that not badly did the Epicurean sing, whose kingdom we are now invading: ‘do we not see that nature barks [latrare] for nothing else for itself except that pain, sundered from the body, be absent, and the mind [mens] enjoy a pleasant sense [sensu], with care and fear removed?’ The rest were adjoined for explication; for as long as the mind [mens] enjoys a pleasant sense, neither can there be any pain, nor fear, or care. These [things] therefore let them have themselves [habento] thus. Pleasantness of mind [mentis], now [modo], from what is it had, or by what [things] is it hindered? |
| Asphal. Palam est hostem iucunditatis esse dolorem; dolorem vero nasci ex malo praesenti, vel bono absente. | Asphal. It is manifest [Palam est] that the enemy of pleasantness is pain [dolorem]; and that pain is born from an evil present, or a good absent. |
| [Margin, p. 413: Improborum vitam esse poenosiorem.] | [Margin, p. 413: That the life of the wicked is more painful [poenosiorem].] |
| Ereu. Recte, quisquis itaque plura bona absentia vel nata abesse appetit, is maxime dolori subiectus est (malum enim cum sit privatio boni) nihil differunt bonum absens & malum praesens. Is e contra qui pauciora bona & minus defectibilia appetit, is minus doloribus est subiectus, & magis proinde iucunditati natus. | Ereu. Rightly. Whoever therefore desires [appetit] more goods [that are] absent or born [nata] to be absent, he is most subject to pain (for since evil is the privation of good) a good absent and an evil present differ in nothing. He, on the contrary [e contra], who desires fewer goods and less defectible [defectibilia] [ones], he is less subject to pains, and more, accordingly [proinde], born for pleasantness. |
| Asphal. Caetera evidentia sunt, hoc ultimum nisi magis inclaruerit, non libere tibi assentior: video enim inter ipsa bona, aliud alio esse maius, & quod ex hoc fit, maiorem iucunditatem afferre: quare diversa est haec, ut apparet mihi, quaestio. | Asphal. The rest are evident; this last, unless it shall have become more clear [inclaruerit], I do not freely assent to you: for I see among the goods themselves one to be greater than another, and, what comes about [fit] from this, to bring a greater pleasantness: wherefore this is, as it appears to me, a diverse [diversa] question. |
| Ereu. Esto. Prioremque partem prosequamur. Quid putas in causa esse cur quis plura bona ex his quae coram oculis nobis exposita sunt, appetat? | Ereu. Be it so. And let us pursue the prior part. What do you think to be in the cause [in causa esse] why anyone desires more goods out of these which are set out to us before [our] eyes? |
| Asphal. Quia ab ipsis multum movetur: hoc autem illi contingit, vel ex corpore, vel a mente. Corpus constitutione & usu indigere potest horum aliquo: Mens vero multa consideratione & opinione quam habet quasi ex fide (quia haec ab iis aestimantur quibus fidit) ab iisdem percelli potest. | Asphal. Because he is much moved by them: and this befalls [contingit] him either from the body, or from the mind [mente]. The body by [its] constitution and use can need some one of these: but the mind [mens], by much consideration and by opinion which it has as if from faith [fide] (because these are esteemed by those whom it trusts [fidit]), can be struck [percelli] by the same. |
| Ereu. At corpus necessitatem suam motione spirituum prosequitur: hanc autem, nisi fallor, passionem dixisti, sine qua neque mens multum considerare potest, ut ex his tribus capitibus necesse sit eos passioni deditos esse, qui haec prosequuntur. Qui autem fide haec prosequuntur, & necesse est iis quibus fidunt, similes esse & velle & fieri: & multum considerare quae credunt optima: & per consequens ipsos esse passionibus obnoxios: praeterquam quod haec bona multum aestimare inde plerumque accidit, quod alia bona vel non cognoscuntur vel non agnoscuntur. Et hoc necessario arguit modicam aliorum esse considerationem: & fit ex omni capite magis esse obnoxios appetitui qui haec magis appetunt. Passioni autem subditos eosdem esse prudentes & studiosos supra noluisti. Horum itaque vitam secundum rationem regi impossibile est. Addamus etiam hoc. Quaenam ea bona credis quae difficillime & obtinentur & conservantur? | Ereu. But the body pursues its necessity by a motion of the spirits [spirituum]: and this, unless I am deceived, you called passion [passionem], without which neither can the mind [mens] consider much; so that from these three heads [capitibus] it is necessary that they be given over to passion who pursue these [things]. And those who pursue these by faith [fide]: it is both necessary [for them] to be like to those whom they trust, both to will and to become [so] [& velle & fieri]; and to consider much [the things] which they believe best; and consequently [per consequens] themselves to be liable [obnoxios] to passions: besides that to esteem these goods much comes about [accidit] mostly from this, that other goods are either not known [cognoscuntur] or not acknowledged [agnoscuntur]. And this necessarily argues [arguit] the consideration of the others to be moderate [modicam]: and it comes about [fit] from every head that those are more liable to appetite who desire these more. But that the same [men], subject to passion, are prudent and studious, you would not [allow] above [supra noluisti]. Of these therefore it is impossible that the life be ruled according to reason. Let us add this also. Which, do you believe, are those goods which with most difficulty are both obtained and preserved? |
| Asphal. Aliam video ex rebus, aliam ex hominibus nasci difficultatem. Ex ipsarum rerum natura difficillime conservantur quae ex natura rei fluxa sunt, difficillime obtinentur quae rara sunt. Ex hominum vero conditione magis ea habitu sunt ardua quae a pluribus haberi cum non possint, a pluribus tamen quaeruntur. Et haec quidem eadem esse video quae passionibus dediti sequuntur. Cum enim vitiosi & imprudentes sint plures, necesse est ea ad quae passione ferimur, a pluribus desiderari, quorum cum usus talis sit ut vel usu consumantur, vel certe non eiusdem rei usus in plures simul didi possit, fit ut circa haec homo hominem impediat; maxime vero circa rara, quod & pluris aestimantur & minus acquiri possint & retineri. Unde non opus est ulterius hoc filum nere. Video enim quomodo Acherusia stultorum fit denique vita. Verum eandem non maioribus voluptatibus etiam redundare nondum liquet. | Asphal. I see one difficulty to be born from the things, another from men. From the nature of the things themselves, those are preserved with most difficulty which from the nature of the thing are flowing [fluxa]; those are obtained with most difficulty which are rare. But from the condition of men, those are more arduous to have [habitu] which, although they cannot be had by more [a pluribus], are nevertheless sought by more. And these indeed I see to be the same which those given over to passions follow. For since the vicious and imprudent are more [plures], it is necessary that those [things] to which we are borne by passion be desired by more; and since the use of these is such that either they are consumed by use, or at any rate [certe] the use of the same thing cannot be distributed [didi] to more at once, it comes about that about these man hinders man; but most of all about rare [things], because they are both esteemed at more [pluris] and can less be acquired and retained. Whence there is no need to spin [nere] this thread further. For I see how the life of fools becomes at last [denique] an Acherusia [Acherusia]. But that the same [life] does not also overflow with greater pleasures is not yet clear [liquet]. |
| [Margin, p. 416: Voluptates bonorum esse maiores.] | [Margin, p. 416: That the pleasures of the good are greater.] |
| Ereu. Quid enim voluptatem aliquam vel actionem nobiliorem seu fortiorem facit, nisi perpetuitas eiusdem quae extensio non male dicitur, & intrinseca vis quam non male intensionem licet appellare? his duabus quae voluptas praevalet, ea procul dubio maior est. Extensionem modo si conferas, vide quo victoria inclinet. Voluptates studiosorum sunt benefacere quibus potes, consulere patriae & civibus, protegere inopes contra iniurias fortiorum, consolari moestos, afflictos relevare, consilium manumque praestare indigentibus, & similia, quae probant in aliis, etiam qui non faciunt. Maiora vero sunt bona quibus semetipsos afficiunt: Contemplari naturalem & moralem maxime partem sive ea principia ex quibus vita, ratione & intelligentia constanter & sine alterius ducis indigentia regatur: seu quod canit Philosophus noster tenere, | Ereu. For what makes any pleasure or action nobler or stronger, except the perpetuity of the same, which is not badly called extension [extensio], and the intrinsic force, which it is permitted not badly to call intension [intensionem]? the pleasure which prevails in these two, that beyond doubt is greater. Now [modo], if you compare extension, see whither the victory inclines. The pleasures of the studious [studiosorum] are to do good to whom you can, to take counsel for [consulere] the fatherland and the citizens, to protect the needy against the injuries of the stronger, to console the sorrowful, to relieve the afflicted, to furnish counsel and hand to the indigent, and similar [things], which even those who do not do [them] approve in others. But greater are the goods with which they affect [afficiunt] their very selves: to contemplate the natural and, most of all [maxime], the moral part, or those principles from which life may be ruled by reason and intelligence constantly and without need of another leader: or, what our Philosopher sings, to hold [tenere] |
| Edita doctrina sapientum templa serena, | Raised high [edita] by the doctrine of the wise, the serene temples, |
| Despicere unde queas alios, passimque tueri | Whence you may be able to look down on others, and everywhere [passim] to behold [them] |
| Errare, atque viam palantes quaerere vitae. | Wandering, and, straying [palantes], seeking the way of life. |
| Haec confer cum actionibus imprudentium sequaciumque corporeae iucunditatis in eo quod est continue, & sine impedimento posse agi. Quis enim alteri haec invidet? Quis praeripere festinat? Corpus, cibis & voluptatibus perpetuo implicari horret: his desiderat & aestuat, corpore nihil repugnante, animus. Crescunt hae iucunditates cum aetate, minuuntur illae. Hae non onerant corpus, non macerant; quoniam sapienter a prudentibus exercentur: illae per immoderationem sauciant & sibi ipsis nocumento sunt. Morbos imbecillitates induunt. Rationales nullae satietatem vel fastidium inferunt: in quae naturaliter desinunt ex passione profectae voluptates. Tandem quae ratione gubernantur, vel ideo magis constantes sunt, quoniam ratio providet, ne eo quod amat destituatur in futurum, cum passio de praesenti tantummodo sit sollicita. Fortitudo modo & intensio ex principiis & effectis pensatur. Patet autem magis secundum naturam esse rationales & secundum naturam quidem magis potentem & efficacem, quam sit corpus cui placent quas passio sequitur. Adiice effectus rationis, non in horam vel diem vel annum, sed sine fine sese extendere. Si itaque ab effectis pendet decisio, infinite excellunt rationales. | Compare these with the actions of the imprudent and of the followers [sequacium] of corporeal pleasantness in this, that [they] can be done continually and without impediment. For who grudges [invidet] these to another? Who hastens to snatch [them] first [praeripere]? The body shudders [horret] to be entangled perpetually in foods and pleasures: in these [his] the mind [animus] desires and burns [aestuat], the body opposing [repugnante] nothing. These pleasantnesses grow with age; those are diminished. These do not burden the body, do not waste [macerant] [it]; since they are exercised wisely by the prudent: those through immoderation wound, and are a harm [nocumento] to themselves. They put on [induunt] diseases, weaknesses. No rational [pleasures] bring in satiety or disgust [fastidium]: into which [things] pleasures set out from passion naturally end. Finally, those which are governed by reason are more constant even [vel] for this reason, since reason provides [providet] lest it be deprived [destituatur] of that which it loves in the future, while passion is solicitous only about the present. Now [modo] strength and intension are weighed [pensatur] from principles and effects. And it is manifest that the rational [pleasures] are more according to nature, and [that the mind is] according to nature indeed more powerful and efficacious than is the body, to which those [pleasures] are pleasing which passion follows. Add that the effects of reason extend themselves not into an hour or a day or a year, but without end. If therefore the decision hangs on the effects, the rational [pleasures] excel infinitely. |
| Asphal. Unde ergo est quod plerumque homines ita naturales aestiment, ut eas solas voluptates vocent, nihilque prae iis omnes iucunditates honestarum actionum faciant? Immo & inter illas eas prae aliis habent, quae longe a ratione sunt alienissimae? | Asphal. Whence then is it that for the most part men esteem [those pleasures] so natural that they call them alone pleasures, and make all the pleasantnesses of honest [honestarum] actions nothing in comparison with [prae] them? Nay, and among those they hold before others [prae aliis] those which are by far most alien from reason? |
| Ereu. Qui naturam animae experimentis persequi instituerunt, in hoc incurrerunt, ut nihil immediate percipi a nobis nisi corporeum invenirent, quae vero animae propria sunt, sola collectione ex corporeis cognosci. Hinc est ut maxima pars hominum quibus crassior circa praecordia sanguis non permittit subtilium indagationem, vel quibus id oti non fuit, vel qui errore correpti deviaverunt a vero, ea maxime aestiment (ut fas est) quae in experimenta cadunt & cognitionem obviam: Ea vero sunt passionum motiva & voluptates ex iis motae. Unde necesse est eas a plerisque maximi haberi. Vides itaque falso aestimari vitam improborum foeliciorem vita bonorum, etiamsi nihil praeter hanc vitam spectaretur. | Ereu. Those who have undertaken [instituerunt] to pursue the nature of the soul [animae] by experiments [experimentis] have run into this: that they found nothing to be perceived by us immediately except [what is] corporeal, and that [the things] which are proper to the soul are known only by collection [collectione] from corporeal [things]. Hence it is that the greatest part of men — whom a thicker [crassior] blood about the praecordia [praecordia] does not permit the tracking-out [indagationem] of subtle [things], or who have not had leisure for it [quibus id oti non fuit], or who, seized by error, have strayed from the true — esteem most (as is right [fas]) those [things] which fall into experiments [experimenta] and [into] cognition lying in the way [obviam]: and these are the motives [motiva] of the passions and the pleasures moved from them. Whence it is necessary that these be held of greatest [value] [maximi] by most. You see therefore that the life of the wicked is falsely esteemed happier than the life of the good, even if nothing besides this life were regarded. |
| [Margin, p. 419: Probos caeteris paribus bonorum temporalium pleniores esse.] | [Margin, p. 419: That the upright [probos], other [things] being equal, are fuller of temporal goods.] |
| Asphal. Hoc equidem fateor & scire gaudeo: Non tamen propterea responsum quaestioni arbitror, neque illustratum esse, quomodo hic mundus non pro impiis creatus sit. Postquam iis adhuc manent quae in sensibilibus aestimari comperimus. Eum enim saltem fructum, qui ex potentia, honoribus, facultatibus, amicitiis, voluptatibus elicitur iis nasci, & quod hinc fit, etiam illud ex quibus fructus oritur eorum gratia natum esse credendum est. Substantias enim corporeas propter actionem ipsarum creari notum est. Fructum vero improborum in sorte esse omnimodis experimur. | Asphal. This indeed I confess, and I rejoice to know [it]: yet I do not therefore judge [arbitror] the question answered, nor [that it has been] made clear [illustratum] how this world was not created for the impious. Since [Postquam] to them still remain [the things] which we find [comperimus] to be esteemed among sensibles. For at least that fruit which is elicited from power, honours, resources [facultatibus], friendships, pleasures, is to be believed to be born for them, and, what comes about [fit] from this, that also out of which the fruit arises to have been born for their sake [gratia]. For it is known that corporeal substances are created for the sake of their action. But that the fruit is in the lot [sorte] of the wicked we experience in every way [omnimodis]. |
| Ereu. Potentiam itaque qui alicui largiuntur in se, quid ab eo sperant cui largiuntur? | Ereu. Those therefore who bestow [largiuntur] on someone power over themselves [in se], what do they hope from him on whom they bestow [it]? |
| Asphal. Iustitiam, & pacem, & abundantiam. | Asphal. Justice, and peace, and abundance. |
| Ereu. Haec autem ab improbis an a probis expectare fas est? | Ereu. And these, is it right [fas] to expect from the wicked or from the upright [probis]? |
| Asphal. Palam est, quod non nisi a probis. | Asphal. It is manifest that [it is] not except from the upright. |
| Ereu. Rursus ex corde cuius causa aliquem honoramus? | Ereu. Again: from the heart, for the cause of what do we honour anyone? |
| Asphal. Virtutis & scientiae, quae duo sunt quae hominem caeteris animalibus & hominibus praestare faciunt. | Asphal. Of virtue and of science, which are [the] two [things] which make a man excel [praestare] the other animals and men. |
| Ereu. Et haec non credo germina in impiorum hortis habitare. Ab amico vero quid optas, nisi ut recte consulat cum indigueris, ut opem ferat etiam suo cum periculo, soletur in malis, congaudeat in bonis, conveniat in multis, dissentiat in paucis; et si quae sunt huiusmodi quae vitam dulcem securamque faciant. | Ereu. And these sprouts [germina] I do not believe dwell in the gardens of the impious. But from a friend what do you wish [optas], except that he counsel rightly when you have been in need, that he bring help even with his own danger, console [you] in evils, rejoice with [you] in goods, agree in many [things], dissent in few; and if there are any [things] of this kind which make life sweet and secure? |
| Asphal. Sic est. Neque haec etiam piscor in improborum aequoribus, sed bonorum fluminibus, ut interrogationem tuam praeveniam. | Asphal. So it is. Nor do I fish for these either in the seas [aequoribus] of the wicked, but in the rivers of the good, that I may forestall [praeveniam] your interrogation. |
| Ereu. Medico si indigeas, non quaeras sollicitum, fidelem, prudentem, qui tibi vitam, non ludos faciat: si operarium, non diligentem, veracem, promissa tenentem postulas? si mercatorem a quo emas vel cui vendas, non eodem modo iustum, fidei tenacem, brevibus & sine ambiguitate merces exponentem desideras? | Ereu. If you should need a physician, would you not seek [one] solicitous, faithful, prudent, who may make for you life, not games [ludos]? if a workman, do you not demand [one] diligent, truthful, holding [to his] promises? if a merchant from whom you may buy or to whom you may sell, do you not in the same mode desire [one] just, tenacious of faith [fidei], setting out [his] wares in brief [words] [brevibus] and without ambiguity? |
| Asphal. Fateor. | Asphal. I confess [it]. |
| Ereu. Et si voluptatem ex cibis, Venere, vel quovis alio sensu captares, non praevia quadam aviditate seu fame te praepares, ut corpus optime constitutum habeas? | Ereu. And if you were catching at [captares] pleasure from foods, Venus [Venere], or any other sense you please, would you not prepare yourself by some previous [praevia] avidity or hunger, so that you may have the body best constituted? |
| Asphal. Sic est. | Asphal. So it is. |

## Words and phrases with alternative or special meanings

- ***iucunditas / iucundum / voluptas*** — rendered “pleasantness / the pleasant / pleasure”. iucunditas = delight, agreeableness, joy; voluptas = pleasure, sensual pleasure. Kept apart throughout: White equates them (‘iucunditatem seu voluptatem’) but uses iucunditas for the mind's state of enjoyment (the Epicurean ἡδονή in its broad sense) and voluptas for particular pleasures.
- ***foelicitas / beatitudo*** — rendered “happiness / beatitude”. felicity; blessedness. Foelicitas here is natural this-worldly happiness (‘in hoc saeculo’); beatitudo is the scholastic term for the perfect good, which White treats as its equivalent for the argument.
- ***magnum gloriae pondus … operetur*** — rendered “works a great weight of glory”. produces, achieves. 2 Corinthians 4:17 (Vulgate ‘aeternum gloriae pondus operatur in nobis’); Ereunius sets aside the theological (utility-for-the-next-life) sense of ‘good life’.
- ***impraesentiarum*** — rendered “at present”. for the time being. Archaic adverb (glossary); contrasted with ‘in futuro saeculo’.
- ***potitio (boni)*** — rendered “getting-possession”. acquisition, attainment. Rare noun from potior; with ‘a bono per se possesso’ it gives the scholastic definition of joy (gaudium) as the motion following the possession of the loved good.
- ***integrare*** — rendered “make whole”. complete, constitute. Glossary term; scholastic ‘integrate’ = constitute as a whole.
- ***nonne videmus nil aliud sibi naturam latrare …*** — rendered “do we not see that nature barks for nothing else …”. latrare = bark, clamour for, demand. Lucretius II.16–19 (‘nonne videre / nil aliud sibi naturam latrare, nisi ut qui / corpore seiunctus dolor absit, mente fruatur / iucundo sensu cura semotu’ metuque’), adapted into prose. ‘The Epicurean, whose kingdom we are now invading’ = Lucretius/Epicurus, since the question is now about pleasure.
- ***habento*** — rendered “let them have themselves”. future imperative: ‘let these things stand so’. Glossary (D1-N10); legal-formulaic idiom.
- ***nata abesse*** — rendered “born to be absent”. naturally such as to be absent, liable to be lost. Natum esse = naturally apt (glossary D3-N14).
- ***defectibilis*** — rendered “defectible”. liable to fail, perishable. Scholastic term (defectibilitas) for the transience of created goods.
- ***in causa esse*** — rendered “to be in the cause”. to be the reason, be responsible. Classical idiom (Cicero) rendered literally.
- ***fides / fidere*** — rendered “faith / trust”. belief on authority, credit; to rely on. Opinion held ‘as if from faith’ = belief taken on others' estimation, not religious faith.
- ***passio*** — rendered “passion”. emotion; being acted upon; property. Here the psychological sense — the motion of the spirits accompanying sensitive appetite (cf. D3-N15–16) — not the logical ‘property’ sense listed earlier in the glossary.
- ***spiritus*** — rendered “spirits”. animal spirits; breath. Galenic animal spirits (glossary D3-N15).
- ***capita*** — rendered “heads”. headings, grounds of argument. Rhetorical/logical ‘heads’; the three are body, mind's consideration, and opinion from trust.
- ***& velle & fieri*** — rendered “both to will and to become [so]”. both to wish to be and to become. The infinitives seem to depend on ‘similes esse’: those who rely on others must be like them in will and become like them. Rendered literally.
- ***supra noluisti*** — rendered “you would not [allow] above”. you refused, denied. Refers to the earlier part of the knot (part 1), where passion was set against prudence.
- ***habitu (supine)*** — rendered “to have”. in the having. Supine of habeo with ‘ardua’ (like ‘difficile dictu’).
- ***didi*** — rendered “be distributed”. be dealt out, spread. Present passive infinitive of dido, didere — not a misprint for dividi.
- ***pluris (aestimantur)*** — rendered “esteemed at more”. genitive of value: valued more highly. Cf. ‘maximi haberi’ below.
- ***hoc filum nere*** — rendered “spin this thread”. pursue this line of argument. Spinning metaphor; nere = to spin.
- ***Acherusia stultorum fit denique vita*** — rendered “the life of fools becomes at last an Acherusia”. Acheron-like, a hell. Lucretius III.1023 (‘hic Acherusia fit stultorum denique vita’): the punishments of the underworld are the passions of fools in this life.
- ***extensio / intensio*** — rendered “extension / intension”. duration / degree. Scholastic measures of a quality or act: extensive (here duration, ‘perpetuitas’) and intensive (degree of force).
- ***studiosi*** — rendered “the studious”. the zealous (for virtue), the good, scholars. Translating Aristotle's σπουδαῖος (the good man) as in the Latin Ethics; contrasted with the imprudent.
- ***consulere patriae*** — rendered “take counsel for the fatherland”. look after, provide for. Consulere + dative = care for; distinct from consulere = advise below (‘recte consulat’).
- ***quod canit Philosophus noster tenere, / Edita doctrina sapientum templa serena …*** — rendered “what our Philosopher sings, to hold / raised high by the doctrine of the wise, the serene temples …”. tenere = to hold (infinitive), not the adverb ‘tenderly’. Lucretius II.7–10 (‘sed nil dulcius est, bene quam munita tenere / edita doctrina sapientum templa serena, / despicere unde queas alios passimque videre / errare atque viam palantis quaerere vitae’). White prints ‘tueri’ for videre and ‘palantes’; kept as printed. ‘Our Philosopher’ is Lucretius, ironically adopted.
- ***induunt (morbos)*** — rendered “put on”. bring on, contract. The immoderate pleasures ‘clothe themselves with’ (i.e. bring on) diseases.
- ***fastidium*** — rendered “disgust”. loathing, satiety. Paired with satietas; Aristotelian point that rational pleasures do not cloy (EN X.4).
- ***crassior circa praecordia sanguis*** — rendered “a thicker blood about the praecordia”. praecordia = the midriff, heart region, seat of feeling. Virgil, Georgics II.484 (‘frigidus obstiterit circum praecordia sanguis’), the poet's excuse for not attaining natural philosophy; the humoral theory of wit.
- ***experimenta / experimur*** — rendered “experiments / experience”. experience, observation, trial. White's polemic against those who investigate the soul ‘by experiments’ (empirical observation) and conclude only corporeal things are perceived immediately — cf. Gassendist and Hobbesian empiricism; ‘collectio’ = inference.
- ***collectio*** — rendered “collection”. inference, gathering by reasoning. Scholastic term for discursive inference (from colligere).
- ***ut fas est*** — rendered “as is right”. as is natural, as one would expect. Ironical or concessive: given their condition it is fitting that they esteem what falls under experience.
- ***postquam*** — rendered “since”. after, since (causal). Late/scholastic causal use.
- ***facultates*** — rendered “resources”. means, wealth; faculties. Here property/wealth, not powers of the soul.
- ***in sorte esse*** — rendered “be in the lot”. belong to the share of. Sors = lot (glossary).
- ***germina … in hortis*** — rendered “sprouts … in the gardens”. shoots, buds. Metaphor continued by Asphalius' ‘seas … rivers’ (piscor).
- ***qui tibi vitam, non ludos faciat*** — rendered “who may make for you life, not games”. ludos facere alicui = to make a fool of someone, play tricks. Comic idiom (Plautus, Terence) played against ‘vitam’: a physician who preserves life rather than trifling with the patient.
- ***Venus (Venere)*** — rendered “Venus”. sexual pleasure. Classical metonymy.
- ***praevius*** — rendered “previous”. going before, preparatory. Appetite (hunger) as a precondition of pleasure; prepares the argument that virtue is the per se cause even of bodily pleasure.
