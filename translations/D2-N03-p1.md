# De Mundo — Dialogue II (De forma mundi, seu de motu magnorum corporum), Knot iii, part 1

*Latin title:* Nodus III. Mare a litoribus et ad littora moveri  
*English title:* Knot III. That the sea is moved from the shores and to the shores

## Dialogue II, Knot iii, part 1 (printed pp. 144–153)

| Latin | English |
|---|---|
| [p. 144] [Margin: Nodus III. Mare a litoribus et ad littora moveri.] Andab. Iam hoc dixi, Occidentem versus. | [p. 144] [Margin: Knot III. That the sea is moved from the shores and to the shores.] Andab. This I have already said: towards the West. |
| Ereu. Putasne aquam quae versus Occidentem est, resistere, vel non? | Ereu. Do you think that the water which is towards the West resists, or not? |
| Andab. Si prius quieta erat, video ad motum non posse sine resistentia perduci; sed non video, an quieta fuerit. | Andab. If before it was quiet, I see that it cannot be brought through [perduci] to motion without resistance; but I do not see whether it was quiet. |
| Ereu. Vide quid moveat fluvios? quid stagna teneat quieta? | Ereu. See what moves rivers? what holds pools [stagna] quiet? |
| Andab. Video flumina ab alto decurrere, stagnorum complanatissimum esse aequor. Unde te velle video (quoniam aqua maris in una parte non possit esse altior quam in alia) eam ex sese et remota vi extrinseca esse quietam. | Andab. I see that rivers [flumina] run down from on high, [and] that the level surface [aequor] of pools is most flattened [complanatissimum]. Whence I see you to mean [velle] (since the water of the sea cannot be higher in one part than in another) that it is of itself, and [any] extrinsic force [vi] being removed, quiet. |
| Ereu. Sensum meum tenes. Sed si resistit aqua Occidentem versus quid fiet, donec vincatur haec resistentia? | Ereu. You hold my sense. But if the water towards the West resists, what will happen until this resistance is overcome? |
| Andab. Retrorsum abire non poterit, quia ex pacifico nova semper aggeratur aqua; Necessum itaque est in omnem partem intumescere, donec debilior cedat fortiori locum, [p. 145] quam quoniam occasum versus existimo, post aliquod tempus ingenti cursu ad eum devolvendas aquas video. Debiliorem autem eam arguo, quod ex ea parte sola aqua quieta obsistit, cum ex lateribus littora cum suis montibus, a tergo ventus haec omnia operans cum non minori aquarum cumulo, longe maiori resistentia sese opponant. | Andab. It will not be able to go away backwards, because out of the Pacific new water is always heaped up [aggeratur]; it is necessary [necessum] therefore [for it] to swell up towards every part, until the weaker yields place [locum] to the stronger; [p. 145] which [weaker part] since I judge [it to be] towards the setting [occasum], I see that after some time the waters are to be rolled down towards it in a huge course. And I prove [arguo] it [to be] the weaker, because from that part only quiet water stands against [obsistit], whereas from the sides the shores with their mountains, [and] from behind the wind working all these things with a no smaller heap of waters, oppose themselves with far greater resistance. |
| Ereu. Ista vero aquarum in omnem partem intumescentia, cum secreta et insensibili semita vel insigni, et aperto motu perfici valeat, utro horum pacto credis confici? | Ereu. But that swelling [intumescentia] of the waters towards every part, since it is able to be accomplished either by a secret and insensible path or by a notable and open motion, by which of these ways [pacto] do you believe it is brought about [confici]? |
| Andab. Non satis perspicio. Attamen, cum ad hoc adiicio oculum, quod aqua quae primo movetur in superficie maris est, et vento impellente in aggerem et iugum imminet aquae cui advenit; non possum aliud expectare, nisi ut aperta vi et concussione mare, cui advenit, perturbet, et tumorem cum iactatione perficiat. | Andab. I do not see through [it] sufficiently. Yet, when I cast [adiicio] [my] eye at this, that the water which is first moved is in the surface [superficie] of the sea, and, the wind driving [it], hangs over into a mound [aggerem] and ridge [iugum] above the water to which it comes; I cannot expect other than that by open force [vi] and shaking [concussione] it disturbs the sea to which it comes, and accomplishes a swelling [tumorem] with tossing [iactatione]. |
| Ereu. Egregie. Sed hoc quoque attende, num aequaliter fiat haec iactatio in omnes partes? an magis in una parte quam in alia? | Ereu. Excellently. But attend to this also: whether this tossing [iactatio] happens equally towards all parts? or more in one part than in another? |
| Andab. Unde ventus fortior, inde necesse est hanc iactationem et purius oriri et magis fervere. Cum itaque ventus a sole ortum habeat, video verum [sic; ?= ventum] fortissimum sub ipsa solis semita [p. 146] debere aestimari; Unde ab ea parte Maris magis ferociter eiicientur aquae, et inde incipiet fortissima iactatio, et per consequens huius motus bis sub aequatore, sub tropicis singulis semel uno anno, maxima erit iactatio. | Andab. Whence the wind [is] stronger, thence it is necessary that this tossing both arises more purely and boils [fervere] more. Since therefore the wind has [its] rise from the sun, I see that the strongest [wind] [verum, read ventum] ought to be reckoned [to be] under the very path of the sun; [p. 146] whence from that part of the Sea the waters will be thrown out more fiercely, and thence the strongest tossing will begin, and consequently [per consequens] the greatest tossing of this motion will be twice under the equator, [and] under each of the tropics once in one year. |
| Ereu. Hoc itaque non dubito quin ex praedictis, deduci videas, iactationem si in medio mari incipit (quoniam sensibilis est) in utrumque littus ad sensum tendere: et hinc fieri motum quemdam Maris paulatim in utrumque littus succrescentis: et quod accidere necessarium est, eodem postmodum modo decrescentis, postquam in eam altitudinem surrexerit ut pellendo in occasum mari satis sit. Et hoc ipsum toto aequore continuari, donec restituta fuerit mari pacifico quam elargitum fuerat, aqua. | Ereu. This therefore I do not doubt that you see to be deduced from the things said before: that the tossing, if it begins in the middle sea (since it is sensible), tends towards each shore to sense [ad sensum]; and that hence there comes about a certain motion of the Sea growing up [succrescentis] little by little towards each shore; and, what it is necessary should happen, [a motion of the Sea] decreasing afterwards in the same mode, after it has risen into that height that it is sufficient for driving the sea towards the setting [occasum]. And that this very [thing] is continued over the whole level [aequore], until the water which it [the Pacific] had bestowed [elargitum] has been restored to the Pacific sea. |
| Andab. Heu, Asphali? aestum hic maris habemus, a littoribus euntem et redeuntem sicut experimur. | Andab. Ho, Asphalius? Here we have the tide [aestum] of the sea, going from the shores and returning, as we experience [it]. |
| Asphal. Ante ovum canis Andabata; quod enim dixisti recte sese habeat, si semel tantum aqua convertenda foret; nunc autem cum perpetuo fluxu in occasum feratur, nondum video causam cur littora impleantur et depleantur. Immo horum nihil appariturum sed constanti flumine medium mare decursurum, littora constanti tenore sibi constitura. | Asphal. You sing before the egg, Andabata; for what you have said would hold [sese habeat] rightly, if the water were to be turned back only once; but now, since it is borne in a perpetual flux towards the setting, I do not yet see the cause why the shores are filled and emptied [depleantur]. Nay rather, [I see] that none of these [things] will appear, but that the middle sea will run down in a constant stream [flumine], [and] that the shores will stand [constitura] constant [constanti tenore] to themselves. |
| [p. 147] Andab. Tu Ereuni, si quid adiutas curamve levassis: me enim Asphalius de magna spe immo laetitia importune deiecit. | [p. 147] Andab. You, Ereunius, if in anything you help, or will have lightened [my] care [curamve levassis]: for Asphalius has unseasonably thrown me down from a great hope, nay rather joy. |
| Ereu. Ipsus conare, et hoc primum vide, an mare talem impetum consequi possit, quo citra vim externam circumferri valeat, an ablata venti, quam diximus, incubatione, undulatione aliqua ad aequilibrium se componeret? si autem continuo motus principio opus est; vide an necesse etiam non sit renovari perpetuo iactationem quam investigasti? | Ereu. Try [it] yourself [ipsus], and see this first: whether the sea can obtain such an impetus [impetum] by which it may be able to be carried round without [citra] external force [vim], or whether, the brooding [incubatione] of the wind, which we have spoken of, being taken away, it would compose itself by some undulation to equilibrium? But if there is need of a continuous principle [principio] of motion, see whether it is not also necessary that the tossing which you have investigated be renewed perpetually? |
| Andab. Quod spectat aquarum cursum, satis perspicuum est, si quando ad quietam aquam appulsus fuerit, minui ipsius velocitatem; sed et quanto longius distat a principio externo, eo minus violentum esse. Nihil itaque ambigui restat quin cessaturus sit, si principii extrinseci non reparetur afflatu. An autem, cum principium illud perpetuo moveat, perpetuam iactationem vel potius interpolatam faciat, non aeque video. | Andab. As regards the course of the waters, it is clear enough that, if ever it has been driven [appulsus fuerit] against quiet water, its speed is diminished; and also that by how much the further it is distant from the external principle [principio], by so much the less violent it is. Nothing of ambiguity therefore remains [against the conclusion] that it will cease, if it is not repaired by the breathing-upon [afflatu] of the extrinsic principle. But whether, since that principle moves perpetually, it makes a perpetual tossing or rather an interrupted [interpolatam] [one], I do not see equally. |
| Ereu. Hoccine vides, aquam dum in aequilibrio est, non moveri, sed stare. | Ereu. Do you see this: that water, while it is in equilibrium, is not moved, but stands. |
| Andab. Video. | Andab. I see [it]. |
| Ereu. Nusquam itaque diffugituram, nisi vel demissior sit ante eam, vel altior post eam? | Ereu. [And] that it will therefore flee away nowhere, unless either [the water] before it be lower [demissior], or [the water] after it higher? |
| Andab. Et hoc video. | Andab. This too I see. |
| [p. 148] Ereu. Neque ante potest esse humilior nisi posterior creverit, cum ex sese omnis aqua in aequilibrio posita sit, quare nusquam fugiet aqua, nisi pone subeat alia elevatior, elevatior autem sine tempore fieri non potest, maxime cum ea vi et momento quod ad expellendam e loco suo vim aquae resistentem et eam super aliam longinquiorem eiiciendam sit idoneum. Vides itaque non posse mare quasi fluvium uno tenore ferri; sed debere quasi praeparatum se certo tempore alio spatio effundere. Sed necdum aestum habemus, nisi terminos fluxus huius et refluxus indagemus. Huc ergo rursus, Andabata, non putas a prima eiectione fluctuum ex pacifico reliquum in orbem motum pendere? | [p. 148] Ereu. Nor can [the water] before be lower unless the hinder [water] has grown, since of itself all water is placed in equilibrium; wherefore water will flee nowhere, unless other, more elevated, comes up behind; but [water] more elevated cannot come about without time, especially with that force [vi] and moment [momento] which is fit for expelling from its place the force of the resisting water and for throwing it out over other, more distant, [water]. You see therefore that the sea cannot be borne like a river in one tenor [uno tenore]; but that it must, as if prepared, pour itself out at a certain time into another space. But we do not yet have the tide [aestum], unless we track down the terms [terminos] of this flux and reflux. Hither therefore again, Andabata: do you not think that the rest of the motion round the orb [in orbem] hangs from the first ejection of the billows out of the Pacific? |
| Andab. Certum habeo. | Andab. I hold [it] certain. |
| Ereu. Hanc autem a vento, et ventum a Sole vim habere, num magis haeres? | Ereu. And that this [ejection] has [its] force from the wind, and the wind from the Sun, do you stick [haeres] [at it] any more? |
| Andab. Nihilo magis. | Andab. Not a whit more. |
| [Margin: Motum hunc sex horarum esse.] Ereu. Solem modo fac velle in dato sui cursus signo aliquem effectum habere, quanto ante tempore in partem eam agit? | [Margin: That this motion is of six hours.] Ereu. Now suppose [fac] the Sun to will to have some effect at a given mark [signo] of its course: by how much time before does it act upon that part? |
| Andab. Solem cum 24. horis totum orbem suum conficiat, necesse est ad datam partem sibi ad rubricam suppositam 6. horas conari, et ab eadem sex horas recedere. Ventum itaque in dicta parte sex horis augebit, sex minuet quidem; sed porro trahet: quare video, [p. 149] si certa pars existat, in qua sit principium huius motus (quam esse vel fixam vel mutabilem certum est) per senas horas vim ipsius auctum iri, per senas effusum et diminutum. | Andab. Since the Sun completes its whole orb in 24 hours, it is necessary that towards a given part, placed under it at the red line [ad rubricam], it strives [conari] for 6 hours, and for six hours recedes from the same. Therefore in the said part it will increase the wind for six hours, for six indeed diminish [it]; but it will draw [it] onward [porro]: wherefore I see, [p. 149] if there exists a certain part in which is the principle [principium] of this motion (which, it is certain, is either fixed or changeable), that its force will be increased through six hours [each time] [per senas horas], [and] through six poured out and diminished. |
| Asphal. Quam tibi faveam, Ereuni, et hanc tuam speculationem ipsis oculis plus amplexer, ipse dicerem, si te dubium crederem, non possum tamen dissimulare quod me anxium tenet. Cum primis enim quale est, quod ab una constanti et semper idem faciente causa, tot diversitates motuum sequi facis, quibus plures causae aptandae forent; Rursus ex hoc tuo discursu video constantem fluxus aquarum in Occidentem assignari causam, sed de reditu in Orientem, seu de refluxu nihil audio. | Asphal. How much I favour you, Ereunius, and embrace this speculation of yours more than my very eyes, I would myself say, if I believed you [to be] doubtful [of it]; yet I cannot dissemble what holds me anxious. For first of all [cum primis], of what sort is it, that from one cause, constant and always doing the same [thing], you make so many diversities of motions follow, to which more [than one] [plures] causes would have to be fitted? Again, from this discourse [discursu] of yours I see a constant cause assigned for the flux of the waters to the West, but of the return to the East, or of the reflux, I hear nothing. |
| Ereu. Et ego tibi nihil oculis minus pro tanta benignitate debeo, et nisi te coner hac anxietate liberare, non is sim qui hanc a te demerear. Et quod ad primum obiectum attinet, automatum tuum quod ad dirigendum diem circumfers interroga, num non ab uno in semetipsum retorto Chalybe quem motus fontem dicitis, in tot rotulas tam varii differentesque motus deriventur. Vide cor animalis, principium (si quid ego in natura intelligo) omnis motus in eo. Et vide num non unica illa vibratione qua in se movetur, non credendam diversorum in homine motuum [p. 150] varietatem continet? Vide tandem ipsum quem fecimus discursum et videbis non solum fieri, sed et quomodo perficiantur. De posteriori vero quid dicam? fac primo constare esse talem reditum in Orientem, et deinde causas exquire, certe nautae negant se aestum in alto sentire; et si vel concubia nocte aestum persentiscunt, sese proxime terram aliquam accedere suspicantur. Unde ergo constat reditum esse aquarum alium quam a littoribus in altum. Fortassis in fretis et maris angustiis? Sed similis reditus non in Orientem solum sed in quamlibet partem fit, secundum litorum et interdum fluviorum naturam, qui suo cursu, si qua prope littus Insula fuerit, secundum littus defluentes continuum cursum aquarum faciunt, ut nullibi insignius quam in illo rege fluminum Maragnone cernitur. Tandem credo hoc ex nostro discursu elici posse, aestum fluentem medio quodam fluxu in Occidentem simul et Oceani canalem ferri; unde qui indiligentius rem accipiunt, oppositum expectant, cum impleri littora vident, quod tamen si verum foret, cur facilior ex Oriente reditus foret quam in ipsum iter, non facilis erit ratio. | Ereu. And I owe you nothing less than [my] eyes for so great a kindness, and unless I should try to free you from this anxiety, I would not be he who deserves [demerear] this [kindness] from you. And as regards the first objection, question your self-moving [machine] [automatum], which you carry about for regulating [dirigendum] the day, whether from one Steel [Chalybe] twisted back into itself, which you [pl.] call the fountain of the motion, there are not channelled [deriventur] into so many little wheels [rotulas] motions so various and differing. See the heart of an animal, the principle (if I understand anything in nature) of every motion in it. And see whether by that single vibration with which it is moved in itself it does not contain a not-to-be-believed [non credendam] variety of diverse motions in man? [p. 150] See finally the very discourse which we have made, and you will see not only that they come about, but also how they are accomplished. But about the latter [objection] what shall I say? Make it first agreed [constare] that there is such a return to the East, and then seek out the causes. Certainly sailors deny that they feel the tide in the deep [in alto]; and if even in the dead of night [concubia nocte] they perceive [persentiscunt] the tide, they suspect that they are approaching very near to some land. Whence, then, is it agreed that there is a return of the waters other than from the shores into the deep? Perhaps in straits and narrows of the sea? But a like return comes about not only to the East but towards any part whatever, according to the nature of the shores and sometimes of the rivers, which by their course, if there be any Island near the shore, flowing down along the shore make a continuous course of waters, as is seen nowhere more notably than in that king of rivers, the Maragnon. Finally I believe this can be elicited from our discourse: that the flowing tide is borne by a certain middle flux to the West and at the same time [along] the channel [canalem] of the Ocean; whence those who take the matter less diligently expect the opposite, when they see the shores filled; yet if this were true, why the return from the East should be easier than the journey itself into [it], the reason [ratio] will not be easy. |
| Asphal. Mihi super unus haeret scrupulus: Guillelmum enim habeo Batavum illum freti ultra fauces Magellanicas repertorem, testem [p. 151] et assertorem ex Australi in Atlanticum magna vi aquas defluere, cum tu eas in Indicum promoveas, unde per Atlanticum in Australem alveum replicentur. | Asphal. One scruple [scrupulus] remains [super] sticking to me: for I have William, that Batavian discoverer of the strait beyond the Magellanic jaws [fauces], [as] witness [p. 151] and asserter that the waters flow down with great force out of the Southern [sea] into the Atlantic, whereas you move them forward into the Indian [sea], whence through the Atlantic they are folded back [replicentur] into the Southern channel [alveum]. |
| Ereu. Accipe modo hoc responsum, venti vim in tantis angustiis non satis sentiri, hunc autem defluxum ex aliis causis originem posse trahere, quas subinde notandi vix poterit deesse occasio. | Ereu. Take for now [modo] this answer: that the force of the wind is not sufficiently felt in such great narrows, and that this down-flow [defluxum] can draw its origin from other causes, an occasion of noting which [causes] will scarcely be able to be lacking from time to time [subinde]. |
| Asphal. Nescio quam nautae tibi sint applausuri, certe hi qui de Septentrionis mari nobis renunciant, aiunt libero mari ab Occidente in Orientem aeque atque contra aestum ferri. Rursus totum simul inflari mare, consentiunt quos audivi universi, sed quicquid de his sit, Mediterraneum certe mare tanta evidentia ab Occidente in Orientem, et e contra movetur, ut non sit ullus disputandi locus. | Asphal. I do not know how [much] the sailors will applaud you; certainly those who report back to us about the sea of the North say that in the free [libero] sea the tide is borne from West to East equally as the contrary [way]. Again, that the whole sea is blown up [inflari] at once, all [universi] whom I have heard agree; but whatever may be [the case] about these [things], the Mediterranean sea certainly is moved from West to East, and conversely, with so great evidence [evidentia] that there is no place for disputing. |
| [Margin: Cur aestus in mari Septentrionali sit ab Oriente in Occidente(m).] Ereu. Contemplare in figura mundi mare Septentrionale praesertim nostrum, i. inter Americam veteremque mundum conclusum. Nonne vides ab Austro versus Septentrionem, impleri necessario ab Oceano, qui ex Indiis allabitur. Necesse est itaque quantum ventus patitur, dilatato mari in utrumque littus aestum tendere, ab utroque in angustias remitti: si angustiae possunt dici, ubi quando {?minimae} 20. amplius sunt graduum. [p. 152] Necessarium est itaque mare cum impletur, versus Orientem his qui Europaea littora observant, cieri; his vero qui contraria littora observant, in Occidentem; et ibi maximos aestus, fortasse qui in toto sint mari, exerceri. Quod Maragnon testificatur tam immanis violentiae flumen, quod tamen centum integras leucas sursum pellitur. Neque nostrorum littorum contemnendi sunt aestus ab ultimo Septentrione repulsi. Unde difficilis mihi fides, esse per Septentrionem in Orientem aditum saltem insignem; sentiremus enim proculdubio [Margin: Totum mare non simul inflari.] Indici maris cum Occidentali praelia, quotiescumque ex utraque parte simul allaberentur undae. Quod autem putatur totum mare simul inflari, inane est, obvium quemquam lunaris motus calculum, quem circumforanei Astrologi singulis annis imprimunt scitare. Videbis notatos ab ultima Scotia Londinum usque ad nautarum commodum, aestuum diversitatem et singulorum tempora, ut aestum successive ferri, extra aleam positum esse debeat. [Margin: Cur aestus Mediterranei maris sit ab Oriente in Occidentem.] Quod autem de Mediterraneo affers, ut verissimum est, sic apertissimam habet solutionem. Intuenti enim telluris imaginem, patet illud non mare, sed maris sinum esse appellandum. Angusto enim collo Gaditani freti admittit Oceanum, et situs ipsius ab hoc ostio usque ad Aegyptum protenditur, Orientem [p. 153] versus. Unde cum necesse est sit eodem quo bibit Oceanum ore, ipsum revomere, necessum sit aquam in Orientem in aestu tendere, in Occidentem eodem remisso recurrere. Unde cum hoc constet illi aestum ab Oceano affundi (non enim suis aquis intumescere posset neque aliunde habere.) Eadem certitudine manifestum esse debet nullum ex Mediterraneo ad Oceanum argumentum sumi posse. Neque omnino tale esse hoc mare, a quo ad leges aestuum argumenta deduci possint. Unde doleo virum magnum suae de aestu sententiae hunc astipulatorem ascivisse. | [Margin: Why the tide in the Northern sea is from East to West.] Ereu. Contemplate in the figure [figura] of the world the Northern sea, especially ours, i.e. [the one] shut in between America and the old world. Do you not see that from the South towards the North it is necessarily filled by the Ocean, which glides in [allabitur] out of the Indies? It is necessary therefore, as far as the wind allows [patitur], that, the sea being widened, the tide tends towards each shore, [and] from each is sent back into the narrows: if [they] can be called narrows, where, when [they are] least [{?minimae}], they are of 20 degrees and more. [p. 152] It is necessary therefore that the sea, when it is filled, is stirred [cieri] towards the East for those who observe the European shores, but for those who observe the contrary shores, towards the West; and that there the greatest tides, perhaps that are in the whole sea, are exercised [exerceri]. Which the Maragnon testifies, a river [flumen] of such monstrous violence, which nevertheless is driven upwards a hundred whole leagues [leucas]. Nor are the tides of our shores, driven back from the furthest North, to be despised. Whence it is hard for me to believe [difficilis mihi fides] that there is through the North a passage [aditum] into the East, at least a notable [one]; for without doubt we would feel [Margin: That the whole sea is not blown up at once.] the battles of the Indian sea with the Western, as often as the waves glided in from each part at once. But as for its being thought that the whole sea is blown up at once, it is empty; consult [scitare] any calculation of the lunar motion that comes to hand [obvium quemquam], which the itinerant [circumforanei] Astrologers print every year. You will see noted, from furthest Scotland as far as London, for the convenience [commodum] of sailors, the diversity of the tides and the times of each, so that the tide's being borne successively must be placed beyond the die [extra aleam]. [Margin: Why the tide of the Mediterranean sea is from East to West.] But what you bring forward about the Mediterranean, as it is most true, so it has a most open solution. For to one looking upon an image of the earth [telluris], it is plain that that is not to be called a sea, but a bay [sinum] of the sea. For with the narrow neck of the Strait of Cadiz [Gaditani freti] it admits the Ocean, and its site [situs] is stretched out from this mouth as far as Egypt, towards the East. [p. 153] Whence, since it is necessary that it vomit back [revomere] the Ocean by the same mouth by which it drinks it, it is necessary [necessum] that the water tends in the tide towards the East, [and] runs back towards the West, the same [tide] being remitted [remisso]. Whence, since this is agreed, that the tide is poured into it from the Ocean (for it could not swell with its own waters nor have [it] from elsewhere), with the same certitude it ought to be manifest that no argument can be taken from the Mediterranean to the Ocean. Nor is this sea at all such [a one] that from it arguments to the laws of the tides can be deduced. Whence I grieve that a great man has adopted [ascivisse] this supporter [astipulatorem] of his opinion [sententiae] about the tide. |
| Asphal. Negas itaque refluxum in Orientem ferri, quo nihil notius vel constantius esse potest? | Asphal. You deny therefore that the reflux is borne to the East, than which nothing can be better known or more constant? |

## Words and phrases with alternative or special meanings

- ***Mare a litoribus et ad littora moveri*** — rendered “That the sea is moved from the shores and to the shores”. Margin and table spell ‘litoribus … littora’ inconsistently, as printed White's tide theory: the sun-driven trade wind heaps Pacific water westward, which spreads as a swell to both shores and then subsides; the tide is a to-and-fro from the shores, not a global oscillation of the basin (contra Galileo's Dialogo, Day 4).
- ***aestus (maris)*** — rendered “tide”. surge, heat, seething Kept as ‘tide’ for the sea (glossary); in D2-N02 aestus of wind = surge.
- ***iactatio*** — rendered “tossing”. agitation, buffeting; also boasting White's term for the wave-agitation by which the swell propagates (open force), as against an insensible flow.
- ***intumescentia / intumescere / tumor*** — rendered “swelling / swell up / swelling [tumorem]”. rising, bulge The swelling of the sea that constitutes flood tide.
- ***aequilibrium*** — rendered “equilibrium”. balance of level Water 'of itself' rests level; motion only from a difference of height — hydrostatic principle used against perpetual flux.
- ***aequor*** — rendered “level surface / level”. sea, plain Etymological sense (aequus, level) in ‘stagnorum aequor’; ‘toto aequore’ = over the whole sea.
- ***agger / iugum*** — rendered “mound / ridge”. embankment; yoke, mountain-ridge Wave-crest imagery.
- ***ad sensum*** — rendered “to sense”. perceptibly Scholastic ‘sensibly’, i.e. so as to be perceived.
- ***Ante ovum canis*** — rendered “You sing before the egg”. crow before the egg is laid Proverb of premature triumph (cf. Erasmus, Adagia, ‘ante victoriam canere triumphum’); the hen's cackle.
- ***si quid adiutas curamve levassis*** — rendered “if in anything you help, or will have lightened [my] care”.  Echo of Ennius, Annales (quoted at the opening of Cicero, De senectute 1: ‘O Tite, si quid ego adiuero curamve levasso’).
- ***ipsus*** — rendered “yourself [ipsus]”.  Archaic/comic form of ipse (Plautine).
- ***incubatio (venti)*** — rendered “brooding”. lying upon, pressure The wind lying continuously on the sea's surface.
- ***interpolatus*** — rendered “interrupted [interpolatam]”. furbished, interpolated Here ‘intermittent’ as opposed to perpetual.
- ***afflatus*** — rendered “breathing-upon [afflatu]”. blast, inspiration Cf. afflare/afflatio in glossary.
- ***momentum*** — rendered “moment [momento]”. impulse, weight Physical sense (cf. D1-N16).
- ***uno tenore*** — rendered “in one tenor”. uniformly, without break
- ***in orbem*** — rendered “round the orb”. in a circle, around the globe The rest of the motion round the globe.
- ***signum (cursus)*** — rendered “mark [signo]”. sign (zodiacal), point A given point of the sun's daily course.
- ***rubrica*** — rendered “the red line [ad rubricam]”. meridian line Kept literal (glossary: ? meridian line). The place lies ‘under’ the sun's line.
- ***per senas horas*** — rendered “through six hours [each time]”. for six-hour periods Distributive numeral: the alternating six-hour rise and fall, giving the semi-diurnal tide.
- ***automatum (ad dirigendum diem)*** — rendered “self-moving [machine] for regulating the day”. watch, clock A pocket watch: one coiled steel (mainspring, ‘fons motus’) driving many wheels — White's mechanical analogy for one cause producing many motions (cf. Cartesian/Digbean mechanism).
- ***Chalybs*** — rendered “Steel [Chalybe]”.  The mainspring.
- ***cor … principium omnis motus*** — rendered “the heart … principle of every motion”.  Aristotelian (De motu animalium; Part. An. III.4) and Harveian (1628) heart as origin of motion; Digby admired Harvey.
- ***concubia nocte*** — rendered “in the dead of night”. at the time of first sleep Roman division of night.
- ***Maragnon*** — rendered “the Maragnon”.  The Amazon (Marañón); its tidal bore (pororoca) pushes a hundred leagues upstream.
- ***Guillelmus Batavus*** — rendered “William, that Batavian”.  Willem Cornelisz Schouten, who with Le Maire found the strait east of Tierra del Fuego and rounded Cape Horn (1616), beyond the Strait of Magellan.
- ***fauces Magellanicae*** — rendered “the Magellanic jaws”. strait
- ***mare Septentrionale nostrum*** — rendered “the Northern sea, ours”. North Atlantic / North Sea Defined by White as the sea shut in between America and the old world, i.e. the North Atlantic.
- ***leuca*** — rendered “league”.  Glossary (Spanish sea league).
- ***circumforanei Astrologi*** — rendered “itinerant Astrologers”. market-place, hawking Almanac-makers whose annual almanacs carried tide tables for British ports.
- ***scitare*** — rendered “consult [scitare]”. inquire Imperative of the deponent scitor.
- ***extra aleam positum*** — rendered “placed beyond the die”. beyond doubt Glossary.
- ***sinus (maris)*** — rendered “bay [sinum]”. gulf, bosom The Mediterranean as a gulf of the Ocean.
- ***Gaditanum fretum*** — rendered “the Strait of Cadiz”. Strait of Gibraltar Gades = Cadiz.
- ***virum magnum … astipulatorem*** — rendered “a great man … this supporter [astipulatorem]”. co-stipulator, backer (Roman law) Galileo, who in the Dialogo (Day 4) used the Mediterranean tides (Venice) as prime evidence of tides from the earth's double motion.
- ***discursus*** — rendered “discourse [discursu]”. reasoning; running-about Here argument/reasoning (glossary has ‘running-about’ for planetary courses).
- ***demereri*** — rendered “deserve [demerear]”. earn, win the favour of
- ***scrupulus*** — rendered “scruple”. small sharp stone; doubt

## Translator's notes

- Unit: Dialogue II, Knot iii, part 1 of 2. PDF pp. 155–164 = printed pp. 144–153 (10 printed pages of about 24 lines), from ‘Andab. Iam hoc dixi, Occidentem versus.’ (p. 144, margin ‘Nodus III. Mare a litoribus et ad littora moveri’) to Asphalius’ question ‘…quo nihil notius vel constantius esse potest?’ (p. 153). Part 2 starts on p. 153 at ‘Ereu. Non factum nego, sed qui fiat declaro’ (margin ‘Cur in Oceano appareat aqua in Orientem redire’) and runs to the start of Knot iv on p. 161. Offset +11 holds (signatures T p. 145, T ij p. 147, T iij p. 149, V p. 153).
- Marginal headings placed in their rows: p. 144 ‘Nodus III. Mare a litoribus et ad littora moveri’; p. 148 ‘Motum hunc sex horarum esse’; p. 151 ‘Cur aestus in mari Septentrionali sit ab Oriente in Occidente’ (printed beside the end of Asphalius’ speech but belonging to Ereunius’ reply, placed there); p. 152 ‘Totum mare non simul inflari’ and ‘Cur aestus Mediterranei maris sit ab Oriente in Occidentem’.
- Uncertain readings / interpretations: (a) p. 145 ‘video verum fortissimum’ — printed ‘verum’, almost certainly a misprint for ‘ventum’ (the wind); transcribed as printed with [sic; ?= ventum] and translated as ‘ventum’. (b) p. 151 ‘ubi quando m nimae 20.’ — a gap and a missing dot in the type; read {?minimae}. (c) p. 147 ‘si quid adiutas curamve levassis’ — printed ‘curam veleuassis’ (word division ‘curam ve leuassis’); normalised to ‘curamve levassis’ (Ennian echo); ‘adiutas’ kept as printed (frequentative adiutare). (d) p. 149 ‘vim ipsius auctum iri … effusum et diminutum’ — participles neuter/masc. with feminine ‘vim’; the impersonal future passive infinitive ‘auctum iri’ explains the first; translated as referring to the force. (e) p. 149 the clause ‘num non … non credendam … varietatem continet’ has a doubled negative and an indicative in an indirect question; translated literally. (f) The margins on pp. 151–152 say the tide of the Northern and Mediterranean seas runs ‘ab Oriente in Occidentem’, while Asphalius says ‘ab Occidente in Orientem et e contra’; both kept as printed. (g) p. 150 ‘simul et Oceani canalem ferri’ — the accusative ‘canalem’ is taken as ‘along the channel’ ([along] supplied). (h) p. 150 ‘Unde ergo constat reditum esse aquarum alium quam a littoribus in altum.’ ends with a full stop but is translated as a rhetorical question, which the following ‘Fortassis in fretis…?’ requires; as a statement it would mean ‘Whence then it is agreed that…’.
- Verification pass (sentence by sentence against the Latin and the page images, with p. 151's last lines re-checked at 300 dpi): 4 corrections — (1) p. 145 ‘cum ex lateribus littora … a tergo ventus … sese opponant’ first rendered as a causal ‘since’; corrected to concessive/adversative ‘whereas’, and the wind's clause kept as a second subject of ‘opponant’; (2) p. 146 ‘eodem postmodum modo decrescentis’ — the genitive participle was first attached to ‘tide’; corrected to agree with ‘Maris’ (motion of the Sea decreasing); (3) p. 148 ‘quod … sit idoneum’ first rendered with ‘force’ as antecedent; corrected to agree with ‘momento’ (neuter) — ‘which is fit’ now refers to force and moment together; (4) p. 152 ‘obvium quemquam … calculum … scitare’ first taken as an infinitive (‘it is open to anyone to consult’); corrected to the imperative of the deponent scitor (‘consult any calculation … that comes to hand’), since ‘quemquam’ is accusative with ‘calculum’.
- Conventions: as in glossary.md — long s → s, ligatures expanded, accents (à, â, ò, è) dropped, consonantal u → v, ‘q;’ → que, ‘ę’ → ae, abbreviations like ‘stagnorũ’, ‘auctũ’ expanded; supplied English words in [square brackets]; Latin of technical or ambiguous words in [brackets] after the English; printed page changes marked [p. n] in both columns.
